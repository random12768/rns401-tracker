Introduction to Mood Disorders CORE

Mood disorders are characterized by an abnormal range of moods or internal emotional states with loss of control, causing distress and impairment in social and occupational functioning. They represent some of the most prevalent and disabling conditions in medicine, and are heavily tested on USMLE Step 1.

💡 Step 1 Pearl — Neurobiology
The monoamine hypothesis: depression = ↓ serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine. Most antidepressants increase synaptic availability of one or more of these NTs. The delay to clinical effect (4–6 weeks) is explained by receptor downregulation, not the initial NT increase itself.

The building blocks of all mood disorder diagnoses are mood episodes. No single mood episode is itself a diagnosis — the diagnosis depends on which episodes have occurred, their severity, duration, and the pattern over time.

Mood Episodes — The Building Blocks

🔴 Manic Episode

A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive, OR irritable mood and increased activity or energy, lasting ≥1 week (or any duration if hospitalization is required).

⚠ DSM-5-TR Update — Critical Rule
Diagnosis requires ≥3 symptoms from DIG FAST (or ≥4 if mood is only irritable) causing marked functional impairment. Psychotic features (delusions, hallucinations) CAN occur. If psychotic features are present during hypomania → reclassify as MANIA.
🧠 Mnemonic — DIG FAST

💎 Clinical Pearl — Decreased Need for Sleep vs. Insomnia
In mania, patients feel rested after only 3 hours of sleep — they do NOT feel tired. This is "decreased need" for sleep. In depression/anxiety, patients WANT to sleep but CANNOT. This distinction is frequently tested.

🟡 Hypomanic Episode

A hypomanic episode has the same symptoms as a manic episode but is defined by key distinctions:

💎 Step 1 Pearl
Patients with hypomanic episodes often describe feeling great — "best version of myself." They may juggle multiple jobs, sleep 3–4 hours and feel refreshed, and be unusually productive. They rarely seek treatment. This is why Bipolar II is often underdiagnosed.

🔵 Major Depressive Episode (MDE)

An MDE requires ≥5 of 9 symptoms for ≥2 weeks, and MUST include either depressed mood OR anhedonia (in children, irritability may substitute for depressed mood).

🔬 Sleep Architecture in MDD (High-Yield)
  • ↓ Slow-wave (deep) sleep
  • ↓ REM latency (enter REM sooner — most tested!)
  • ↑ REM early in sleep cycle
  • ↑ Total REM sleep
  • Repeated nighttime awakenings
  • Early morning awakening (terminal insomnia)

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) MDD

MDD is diagnosed when a patient has one or more MDEs AND no history of manic or hypomanic episodes. It is the most common mood disorder and affects approximately 7% of adults in the US at any given time.

📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria Summary
Criterion A: ≥5/9 SIG E CAPS symptoms for ≥2 weeks (must include depressed mood OR anhedonia)
Criterion B: Symptoms cause significant distress or functional impairment
Criterion C: Not due to substances or a medical condition
Criterion D: Not better explained by a psychotic disorder
Criterion E: NO history of manic or hypomanic episodes (if present → Bipolar disorder)
⚠ Critical Rule — Always Screen for Bipolar
Before diagnosing MDD, ALWAYS ask about past manic/hypomanic episodes. A patient presenting with depression may have unrecognized Bipolar disorder. Starting antidepressant monotherapy in a bipolar patient can precipitate mania. This is one of the most clinically dangerous errors in psychiatry.

MDD with Atypical Features

📋 Key Features (Most Common Depression Subtype)
  • Mood reactivity — mood brightens in response to positive events (pathognomonic feature)
  • Hypersomnia (not insomnia — "reversed" vegetative signs)
  • Hyperphagia — increased appetite (often carbohydrate craving), weight gain
  • Leaden paralysis — heavy, weighty feeling in arms and legs
  • Interpersonal rejection sensitivity — long-standing, causes significant distress
💎 Step 1 Pearl — MAOI for Atypical Depression
MAO inhibitors (phenelzine, tranylcypromine) are particularly effective for atypical depression. They are NOT first-line due to dietary restrictions and interaction risks. Remember: SSRIs are first-line. MAOIs are preferred when: (1) treatment-resistant, (2) atypical features present. (See MCQ 30)

MDD with Seasonal Pattern (Formerly "SAD")

📋 Key Features
  • Full MDE onset in a specific season (usually autumn/winter) and full remission in another season (usually spring/summer)
  • Pattern present for ≥2 consecutive years
  • Atypical features common (hypersomnia, hyperphagia, weight gain)
  • Treatment: Standard MDD therapies PLUS light therapy (phototherapy) — 10,000 lux lamp, 20–30 min each morning
  • Mechanism: shortened daylight hours → disrupted circadian rhythm and melatonin dysregulation

MDD with Psychotic Features

MDD with psychotic features is a severe subtype of unipolar MDD in which the patient develops delusions and/or hallucinations exclusively during the depressive episode.

MDD Clinical Questions — MCQ 1–8

MCQ 1MDD Diagnosis — Ruling Out Alternatives

A 35-year-old woman presents with insomnia, fatigue, depressed mood, feelings of worthlessness, weight loss, and poor concentration for the past 5 weeks after losing her job. TSH is 0.9 μU/mL (normal). She drinks wine occasionally and uses marijuana once a month. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Adjustment disorder with depressed mood
  • B. Bipolar II disorder
  • C. Depressive disorder due to another medical condition
  • D. Major depressive disorder ✓
  • E. Persistent depressive disorder
  • F. Premenstrual dysphoric disorder
  • G. Substance-induced depressive disorder
✅ D — Major Depressive Disorder

Key rule: Patients with sufficient depressive symptoms are diagnosed with MDD even if there is a clear psychosocial stressor (job loss). ≥5 SIG E CAPS for ≥2 weeks = MDD. The stressor does not change the diagnosis once criteria are met.

Why not A (Adjustment disorder)? Adjustment disorder is only diagnosed when criteria for a specific disorder like MDD are NOT met.

Why not C? Normal TSH rules out hypothyroidism as the cause. Medical cause must be physiologically linked.

Why not F (PMDD)? In PMDD, symptoms must REMIT with onset of menses, confirmed by prospective daily ratings over several cycles.

Educational Objective: MDD is diagnosed based on symptom criteria alone — a precipitating stressor does not prevent the diagnosis.

❌ Common Traps

Don't let a clear stressor (grief, job loss, divorce) prevent you from diagnosing MDD — DSM-5-TR removed the bereavement exclusion. If criteria are met, diagnose MDD.

MCQ 2MDD — Atypical Somatic Presentation

A 45-year-old woman presents with fatigue, weight gain, insomnia with early morning awakening, and muscle aches for 2 months. She worries about having cancer. She has stopped exercising and lost interest in family. Laboratory and thyroid tests are normal. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Depressive disorder due to another medical condition
  • B. Generalized anxiety disorder
  • C. Illness anxiety disorder
  • D. Major depressive disorder ✓
  • E. Somatic symptom disorder
✅ D — MDD

Key concept: MDD can present with SOMATIC symptoms (fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, appetite changes) without the patient reporting "feeling sad." Many patients seek care from primary care physicians for these physical complaints.

When medical illness is ruled out, MDD should be high on the differential when somatic symptoms cluster with anhedonia, guilt, or worthlessness.

Why not B (GAD)? GAD requires multiple worries (not just health) for ≥6 months. This patient's anxiety is focused on a possible medical illness.

Educational Objective: MDD can present without a prominent complaint of "depressed mood" — somatic symptoms and anhedonia may be the chief complaint in primary care settings.

MCQ 3MDD vs. Dementia — Pseudodementia

A 79-year-old retired professor presents with acute-onset memory problems over the past month: forgetting medications, grandchildren's names, leaving the stove on. He has stopped reading and playing with grandchildren. Also has insomnia, low appetite, decreased energy, and says "I feel worthless since retiring." MoCA = 23/30 (abnormal). Neurological exam is normal. Most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Alzheimer disease
  • B. Dementia with Lewy bodies
  • C. Depression-related cognitive impairment ✓
  • D. Frontotemporal dementia
  • E. Normal aging
  • F. Vascular dementia
✅ C — Depression-related Cognitive Impairment (Pseudodementia)

Pseudodementia = reversible cognitive impairment in the context of MDD, mimicking dementia. Key features that distinguish it from true dementia:

  • Acute onset (1 month) coinciding with depressive symptoms
  • Patient often says "I don't know" and doesn't try (vs. dementia: confabulation)
  • Treatment of depression → cognitive improvement
  • No focal neurological deficits

True dementia subtypes: Alzheimer's (memory-prominent, gradual), Lewy body (visual hallucinations, parkinsonism), Frontotemporal (personality changes first), Vascular (stepwise, cerebrovascular history).

Educational Objective: Depression in the elderly can cause reversible cognitive impairment that mimics dementia — always treat the depression before concluding it's dementia.

MCQ 4Antidepressant Onset of Action

A 32-year-old man with MDD was started on an SSRI. At 2-week follow-up, mood is "about the same." He initially had nausea and worsening insomnia which have resolved. Drinks 4–5 beers/week. He is discouraged. What is the most likely explanation for lack of response?

  • A. Adverse medication effects
  • B. Comorbid anxiety disorder
  • C. Concurrent alcohol use
  • D. Duration of treatment ✓
  • E. Treatment-resistant depression
✅ D — Duration of Treatment

Key pharmacology fact: An adequate antidepressant trial is at least 4–6 weeks. Some patients have early partial response within 2 weeks, but full assessment requires 4–6 weeks at therapeutic dose.

Treatment-resistant depression (E) = failure of ≥2 adequate trials. This patient has had only 2 weeks — inadequate duration.

Alcohol (C): 4–5 beers/week is below the threshold for problematic use (>14 drinks/week for men) and unlikely to cause or worsen his depression.

Educational Objective: Always counsel patients that SSRIs take 4–6 weeks for full antidepressant effect. Transient early side effects (nausea, insomnia) should not prompt discontinuation.

MCQ 5MDD with Psychotic Features

A 45-year-old man is hospitalized for suicidal ideation. He has been depressed for >1 month, with poor appetite, sleep disturbance, weight loss, and psychomotor agitation. He reports being "responsible for all the evil in the world" and hears a voice telling him he doesn't deserve to live. No psychiatric history. Most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Bipolar disorder with psychotic features
  • B. Delusional disorder
  • C. MDD with psychotic features ✓
  • D. Schizoaffective disorder
  • E. Schizophrenia
  • F. Schizophreniform disorder
✅ C — MDD with Psychotic Features

The psychotic symptoms (delusion of causing evil = mood-congruent; auditory hallucinations) occur exclusively within a depressive episode. This defines MDD with psychotic features.

Treatment: Antidepressant + antipsychotic OR ECT (ECT provides the most rapid response — critical in acutely suicidal patients).

Why not D (Schizoaffective)? Schizoaffective requires psychosis to occur ≥2 weeks in the ABSENCE of a mood episode. Here psychosis only occurs during MDE.

Educational Objective: MDD with psychotic features = psychosis only during MDE + mood-congruent features. Treat with combined antidepressant + antipsychotic or ECT.

MCQ 6MDD in Medical Illness — Nonsomatic Symptoms

A 63-year-old woman with metastatic breast cancer has depressed mood, weight loss, low energy, sleep disturbance, and social withdrawal. She no longer looks forward to family visits. No suicidal ideation. Which symptom is MOST indicative of MDD in this patient?

  • A. Loss of interest in family ✓
  • B. Low energy
  • C. Sleep disturbance
  • D. Thoughts of dying
  • E. Weight loss
✅ A — Loss of Interest in Family

Critical concept: In patients with serious medical illness (cancer, heart failure, etc.), somatic symptoms of depression overlap with symptoms of the underlying disease and its treatments. Therefore, somatic symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, sleep disturbance) are less reliable for diagnosing comorbid MDD.

NONSOMATIC symptoms are more reliable: Anhedonia (loss of interest), worthlessness, excessive guilt, and suicidal ideation. Anhedonia — losing interest in family, not looking forward to visits — is the most specific indicator.

Why not D? Thoughts of death are NORMAL in patients facing terminal illness. Suicidal ideation with intent/plan would be concerning.

Educational Objective: Focus on nonsomatic MDD symptoms (anhedonia, worthlessness, guilt) in medically ill patients.

MCQ 7SSRI Sexual Dysfunction → Switch to Bupropion

A 43-year-old man with MDD started antidepressant medication. Depressive symptoms mildly improved, but he now has significant decrease in libido and impaired sexual performance. He wants to switch medications. Which drug is most appropriate?

  • A. Bupropion ✓
  • B. Citalopram
  • C. Imipramine
  • D. Sertraline
  • E. Trazodone
  • F. Venlafaxine
✅ A — Bupropion

SSRIs cause sexual dysfunction in up to 50% of patients — this is the most common reason for non-adherence. SSRIs affect: libido, arousal, anorgasmia (women), delayed ejaculation (men).

Bupropion (NDRI — NE+DA reuptake inhibitor) does NOT cause sexual dysfunction and does not cause weight gain. Useful for patients with hypersomnia and low energy.

Bupropion CONTRAINDICATIONS: Bulimia nervosa, anorexia nervosa, seizure disorders (↑ seizure threshold lowering at higher doses). See MCQ 21.

Other options: B/D = SSRIs → same problem; C = TCA → also causes sexual dysfunction; E = Trazodone → priapism, sedation; F = SNRI venlafaxine → also causes sexual dysfunction.

Educational Objective: Bupropion is preferred when sexual dysfunction or weight gain are concerns. SSRIs cause sexual dysfunction in up to 50% of patients.

MCQ 8Suicide Risk Assessment — Most Important Factor

A 37-year-old woman with recurrent MDD presents after her father's death. She has anhedonia, insomnia, poor concentration. She has been collecting pills and has considered taking them all at once. No prior attempts. Her mother completed suicide at age 40. What is the MOST important factor in assessing her suicide risk?

  • A. Family history of suicide
  • B. Increasing social isolation
  • C. Lack of sleep
  • D. Medication access ✓
  • E. Recent bereavement
  • F. Recurrent episodes of depression
✅ D — Medication Access

This question tests acute risk assessment. The patient has both ACCESS to means AND a developing plan (collecting pills, considering taking all at once). This combination of access + plan + intent dramatically elevates IMMEDIATE suicide risk.

In acute assessment, means restriction is the most immediately actionable risk factor. Removing access to lethal means (medications, firearms) is the single most effective immediate intervention.

Family history (A) and recurrent depression (F) are significant chronic risk factors but are NOT modifiable in this immediate moment.

Educational Objective: Access to lethal means combined with a developing plan is a critical immediate risk factor requiring urgent intervention.

Postpartum Questions — MCQ 9–10

MCQ 9Postpartum Blues — Appropriate Response

A 24-year-old woman calls the office 5 days after uncomplicated delivery of a healthy boy. She reports "emotional ups and downs," tearfulness, exhaustion, and worry about breastfeeding. She has help from her sister and can nap. No suicidal ideation or thoughts of harming the baby. What is the most appropriate response?

  • A. "Your symptoms are normal; call me if they worsen."
  • B. "Being a new mom is challenging; give yourself time to adjust."
  • C. "I'm concerned your symptoms may develop into postpartum depression; would you like a counseling referral?"
  • D. "Postpartum hormonal shifts can cause mood changes; I'd like you to come in for an evaluation." ✓
  • E. "Your symptoms should resolve on their own; let's check in at your 6-week visit."
✅ D — Prompt In-Person Evaluation

Key concept: Postpartum blues and PPD have overlapping symptoms. Patients frequently underreport depressive symptoms due to shame, guilt, and perceived inadequacy ("I should be happy").

The correct approach:
1. Validate the experience ("hormonal shifts cause mood changes")
2. Screen for safety (no ideation here, but always ask)
3. Schedule in-person evaluation promptly — face-to-face allows better rapport and disclosure

Why not A or B? Dismissive — "call if worse" delays evaluation of potential PPD.
Why not C? Referring without evaluating first is premature.
Why not E? Delaying to 6-week visit is too long — PPD requires earlier detection.

Educational Objective: Due to underreporting risk and PPD overlap, all patients reporting mood symptoms postpartum need prompt in-person evaluation, not telephone reassurance.

MCQ 10Postpartum Depression Diagnosis

A 28-year-old woman at 6-week postpartum checkup reports increasing fatigue, anxiety about being a "terrible mother," waking multiple times to check on baby, skipping meals, and frequent tearfulness. She says "I don't know why I keep crying when I should be happy." No suicidal ideation or thoughts of harming baby. Most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Acute stress disorder
  • B. Adjustment disorder
  • C. Generalized anxiety disorder
  • D. Normal postpartum reaction
  • E. Postpartum depression ✓
✅ E — Postpartum Depression (MDD with Peripartum Onset)

PPD is diagnosed using the same MDE criteria — ≥5 SIG E CAPS for ≥2 weeks: fatigue, tearfulness (depressed mood), appetite changes, sleep disturbance, feelings of worthlessness ("terrible mother"), loss of interest (anxiety about parenting).

These symptoms have persisted beyond 2 weeks → not postpartum blues (which peaks at day 5, resolves by 2 weeks).

All patients should be screened with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) at the 6-week postpartum visit.

Why not D? Postpartum blues resolves within 2 weeks. This patient's symptoms have persisted at 6 weeks.

Educational Objective: PPD = MDE criteria (≥2 weeks) + peripartum onset. Differentiate from postpartum blues (self-limited, resolves by 2 weeks).

Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) CHRONIC

📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria
  • Depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (≥1 year in children/adolescents)
  • ≥2 of: poor appetite/overeating, insomnia/hypersomnia, low energy, low self-esteem, poor concentration, hopelessness
  • No symptom-free period lasting >2 months during the 2 years
  • Full MDE criteria may or may not be met during this period (includes "pure dysthymia" and chronic MDD)
  • No history of manic or hypomanic episodes
💎 Clinical Correlation — Double Depression
Double depression = PDD + superimposed MDE. The patient has the chronic background of dysthymia PLUS a full-blown depressive episode on top. Prognosis is worse than either alone. Treatment: antidepressants + psychotherapy.

Persistent Depressive Disorder — MCQ 11

MCQ 11Persistent Depressive Disorder vs. MDD

A 29-year-old woman reports 3 years of daily exhaustion, persistent unhappiness, loss of interest in jewelry-making, social avoidance, and hopelessness since her business closed. No changes in appetite, sleep, concentration, or suicidal thoughts. PE and labs normal. Most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Adjustment disorder with depressed mood
  • B. Avoidant personality disorder
  • C. Chronic fatigue syndrome
  • D. Major depressive disorder
  • E. Persistent depressive disorder ✓
✅ E — Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

Key distinguishing features:
- Duration: 3 years (≥2 years required for PDD)
- Depressed mood + loss of interest + hopelessness — but does NOT meet full MDE criteria during the entire 3-year period (no sleep, appetite, concentration changes, no SI)
- Chronic, low-grade depression

Why not D (MDD)? MDD requires ≥5/9 SIG E CAPS for ≥2 weeks. This patient has depressed mood + anhedonia + hopelessness = only 2–3 symptoms. This doesn't meet the 5-symptom threshold for an MDE.

Why not A? Adjustment disorder cannot be diagnosed if criteria for another disorder are met (PDD is met here). Also, adjustment disorder resolves within 6 months of stressor ending.

Educational Objective: PDD = chronic low-grade depression for ≥2 years that doesn't meet full MDE criteria. Often associated with "I've always been like this" thinking.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) DSM-5

📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria
  • In the majority of menstrual cycles during the last week before menses onset
  • ≥5 of the following symptoms (must include ≥1 from first four):
    Core: Marked affective lability, irritability/anger, depressed mood, anxiety/tension
    Additional: Decreased interest, concentration difficulty, fatigue, appetite change/cravings, hypersomnia/insomnia, feeling overwhelmed, physical symptoms (breast tenderness, bloating)
  • Symptoms remit within a few days after menses onset
  • Cause significant distress and functional impairment
  • Must be confirmed by prospective daily ratings over at least 2 cycles
💎 Clinical Pearl — PMDD Treatment
First-line: SSRIs (can be used continuously or only during the luteal phase — both effective). Second-line: OCP (drospirenone+ethinyl estradiol combination preferred). Third-line: GnRH agonists for severe cases.
💎 PMDD vs. MDD Distinction for USMLE
Key differentiator: In PMDD, symptoms must remit with onset of menses. In MDD or other depression that worsens premenstrually, symptoms do NOT fully resolve. Requires prospective daily ratings — retrospective reporting is insufficient.

Bipolar & Related Disorders BIPOLAR

Bipolar I Disorder

📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria
  • At least ONE manic episode (± hypomanic or depressive episodes)
  • The manic episode is not better explained by schizoaffective disorder or another psychotic disorder
  • Psychotic features (mood-congruent) may occur during manic OR depressive episodes
  • No depressive episode required — a patient who has had only mania technically meets criteria for Bipolar I
💎 Bipolar Genetic Risk (High-Yield)
  • General population: ~1%
  • 1st degree relative: 5–10%
  • Child with 2 bipolar parents: ~60%
  • Monozygotic twin: ~70% (NOT 100% → environmental factors contribute)
Strong genetic component — strongest of any psychiatric disorder.

Bipolar II Disorder

📋 Criteria (Requires 2 Types of Episodes)
  • At least ONE hypomanic episode
  • At least ONE major depressive episode
  • NO history of manic or mixed episodes (if a manic episode ever occurs → reclassify as Bipolar I)
  • Psychotic features can occur in MDE but NOT during hypomania
  • High suicide risk, particularly during depressive episodes
⚠ Critical Distinction
Bipolar II is NOT simply a "milder" form of Bipolar I. Bipolar II has higher burden of depressive episodes and significant functional impairment. The suicide risk is comparable to Bipolar I. Never minimize Bipolar II to patients or on USMLE.

Cyclothymic Disorder

📋 Criteria
  • ≥2 years (≥1 year in children/adolescents) of numerous periods with hypomanic AND depressive symptoms
  • Neither hypomanic nor depressive symptom sets meet FULL criteria for a hypomanic or major depressive episode
  • Symptoms present for at least half the time with no symptom-free period >2 months
  • Milder version of the bipolar spectrum

Bipolar Clinical Questions — MCQ 12–17

MCQ 12Bipolar I — Long-Term Maintenance

A 27-year-old man presents with 2 weeks of barely sleeping, spending thousands on tools, grandiosity ("architectural masterpiece"), and rapid frenzied speech. History of 2 prior depressive episodes. Which medication is most appropriate for LONG-TERM management?

  • A. Bupropion
  • B. Chlorpromazine
  • C. Haloperidol
  • D. Lorazepam
  • E. Paroxetine
  • F. Valproate ✓
✅ F — Valproate

Bipolar maintenance options: Lithium, Valproate, Lamotrigine, Quetiapine (second-generation antipsychotic).

AVOID antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar — risk of precipitating mania (A=bupropion, E=paroxetine are antidepressants).

B, C (typical antipsychotics): Used for acute mania adjunct but NOT for long-term maintenance monotherapy.

D (Lorazepam): Useful as adjunct in acute mania but NOT recommended for maintenance.

Educational Objective: Maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder requires mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate, lamotrigine) or atypical antipsychotics. Antidepressant monotherapy is contraindicated.

MCQ 13Screening for Bipolar Before Starting Antidepressant

A 30-year-old man with current MDE. Prior depressive episode responded rapidly to sertraline — he "felt really good and energetic" after a few days and discontinued. Prior to initiating pharmacologic treatment, what is MOST important to assess?

  • A. Complete blood count
  • B. Comprehensive metabolic panel
  • C. Electrocardiogram
  • D. History of mania ✓
  • E. History of sexual dysfunction
  • F. Urine toxicology
✅ D — History of Mania

RED FLAG in this vignette: The atypically rapid response to sertraline ("felt really good after a few days") strongly suggests treatment-emergent mania — a sign of underlying bipolar disorder unmasked by an antidepressant.

Before prescribing ANY antidepressant for a depressive episode, screen for:

  • Past manic/hypomanic episodes (elevated mood, decreased sleep, grandiosity, racing thoughts, impulsivity)
  • Family history of bipolar disorder

If bipolar is confirmed → use mood stabilizer, NOT antidepressant monotherapy.

Educational Objective: All patients presenting with depression must be screened for bipolar disorder before prescribing antidepressants. Antidepressants can precipitate mania in bipolar patients.

MCQ 14Bipolar I — Maintenance with Antisuicidal Benefit

A 48-year-old man with current severe MDE (suicidal thoughts, 16 hrs sleep, hypersomnia, weight gain) and history of weeklong periods with extra energy, decreased sleep, and believing he had "superhuman powers to control minds." Most appropriate long-term medication?

  • A. Bupropion
  • B. Clozapine
  • C. Diazepam
  • D. Duloxetine
  • E. Haloperidol
  • F. Lithium ✓
  • G. Sertraline
✅ F — Lithium

This patient has Bipolar I (manic + depressive episodes). Lithium is preferred here because:

  1. First-line mood stabilizer for Bipolar I
  2. Uniquely reduces suicidal ideation and suicide risk (anti-suicidal properties)
  3. Effective for both maintenance and acute mania

Avoid antidepressants (A, D, G) as monotherapy in bipolar — risk of precipitating mania.

Educational Objective: Lithium is first-line maintenance for Bipolar I and has demonstrated anti-suicidal properties — preferred when suicidality is a concern.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

📋 ECT — Complete Clinical Facts
  • Mechanism: Induces tonic-clonic seizure under general anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade (usually succinylcholine)
  • Indications: Refractory depression, MDD with psychotic features, acute suicidality with inability to wait for drug effect, catatonia, refusal to eat/drink, severe MDD in pregnancy
  • No absolute contraindications (per FA 2026)
  • Safe in pregnancy and the elderly
  • Side effects: Disorientation (post-ictal), headache, partial anterograde + retrograde amnesia (usually resolves within 6 months)
  • Fastest-acting antidepressant treatment available
  • Typically 6–12 sessions over 2–4 weeks

MDD Specifiers — Rapid Reference

🟦 Atypical Features (Most Common)

  • Mood reactivity (mood brightens with positives) — pathognomonic
  • Hypersomnia (not insomnia)
  • Hyperphagia + weight gain
  • Leaden paralysis
  • Rejection sensitivity
Rx: SSRIs (1st line), MAOIs (superior but not 1st line)

🔵 Psychotic Features

  • Delusions + hallucinations ONLY during MDE
  • Usually mood-congruent (guilt, punishment, nihilism)
  • Distinguish: psychosis ONLY during MDE (not independently)
Rx: Antidepressant + antipsychotic OR ECT

🌙 Seasonal Pattern (SADs)

  • Full MDE every winter, remission every spring/summer
  • ≥2 consecutive years pattern
  • Atypical features common (hypersomnia, hyperphagia)
Rx: SSRIs + Phototherapy (10,000 lux lamp)

🤱 Peripartum Onset

  • Onset during pregnancy OR within 4 weeks of delivery
  • Blues (50–85%) → resolves within 2 weeks
  • PPD (10–15%) → ≥2 weeks, meets MDE criteria
  • Psychosis (0.1–0.2%) → hospitalize, antipsychotics ± ECT
Rx PPD: CBT + SSRIs (1st line)

⚡ Rapid Review — Final Pass

🔑 Must-Know Points — Mood Disorders
  • Manic episode: DIG FAST ≥3 (≥4 if only irritable), ≥1 week, marked impairment, CAN have psychosis
  • Hypomanic: same as mania but ≥4 days, NO marked impairment, NO hospitalization, NO psychosis
  • MDE: SIG E CAPS ≥5 (including depressed mood OR anhedonia) for ≥2 weeks
  • Bipolar I = ≥1 manic episode | Bipolar II = hypomania + MDE (NO mania)
  • ALWAYS screen for bipolar history before starting antidepressants
  • Antidepressant monotherapy CONTRAINDICATED in bipolar — risk of inducing mania
  • Atypical depression: mood reactivity + hypersomnia + hyperphagia + leaden paralysis + rejection sensitivity
  • Seasonal pattern: phototherapy (10,000 lux) added to standard MDD treatment
  • Postpartum blues: 50–85%, peaks day 3–5, resolves within 2 weeks, supportive only
  • PPD: 10–15%, meets full MDE criteria, treat with CBT + SSRIs
  • Postpartum psychosis: 0.1–0.2%, hospitalize, antipsychotics ± ECT, DO NOT leave alone with infant
  • PDD (Dysthymia): chronic depressed mood ≥2 years, sub-MDE criteria
  • Previous suicide attempt = single strongest predictor of completed suicide
  • Men die more often; women attempt more often
  • Firearms = most common method in US; access ↑ risk 3×
  • SSRIs: first-line for MDD; sexual dysfunction in 50%; onset 4–6 weeks
  • Bupropion: no sex SE, no weight gain; CONTRAINDICATED in eating disorders + seizure disorders
  • MAOIs: tyramine crisis + serotonin syndrome risk; washout 2 weeks before SSRIs
  • TCA overdose → Na⁺ channel blockade → fatal arrhythmia (QRS widening); treat with NaHCO₃
  • Lithium: hypothyroidism, nephrogenic DI, Ebstein anomaly (teratogen), fine tremor; narrow TI
  • NSAIDs + thiazides + ACE inhibitors → ↑ lithium levels (↓ renal clearance)
  • Valproate: NTDs teratogen, hepatotoxicity; dual use: bipolar + seizures
  • Lamotrigine: SJS risk (slow titration); best for bipolar depression maintenance
  • Carbamazepine: CYP450 inducer (↓ other drug levels + autoinduction); SIADH, agranulocytosis
  • ECT: fastest-acting antidepressant; no absolute contraindications; safe in pregnancy + elderly
  • ECT indications: refractory depression, MDD with psychosis, acute suicidality, catatonia, refusal to eat/drink
  • SSRI → MAOI: wait 2 weeks (except fluoxetine → MAOI: wait 5 weeks — longest t½)
  • MAOI → SSRI: wait 2 weeks for MAO synthesis regeneration
