1. Antiemetic Agents

Exam snapshot
  • NTS (medulla) is the vomiting center; inputs: vagal GI afferents (5-HT3), vestibular system (M1, H1), and CTZ in area postrema (D2, NK-1, 5-HT3).
  • 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron): chemotherapy, radiation, postoperative and GI-infection nausea; adverse effects: constipation, headache, QT prolongation, serotonin syndrome.
  • D2 antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine): metoclopramide is also prokinetic for gastroparesis, contraindicated in bowel obstruction; adverse effects: EPS, tardive dyskinesia, NMS, hyperprolactinemia, QT prolongation, depression.
  • H1 antagonists (diphenhydramine, meclizine, promethazine) and M1 antagonist scopolamine treat vestibular nausea (motion sickness).
  • Aprepitant (NK-1 antagonist) plus 5-HT3 antagonist plus dexamethasone for highly emetogenic chemotherapy; benzodiazepines and cannabinoids are adjuncts.
  1. 1Nucleus Tractus Solitarius: The nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla is the vomiting center. It integrates input from the GI tract, vestibular system and chemoreceptor trigger zone and coordinates the emetic response.⭐ Antiemetics work by blocking one of the inputs to the NTS.
  2. 2Vomiting: The NTS (vomiting center) coordinates the vomiting response to many different emetogenic stimuli.
  3. 3Las Vegas near stomach patch: The GI tract signals the NTS mainly through the vagus nerve (CN X) afferents.
  4. 4Happy face hammer throw: Enterochromaffin cells in the GI mucosa release serotonin in response to GI irritants. Serotonin activates 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferent nerves, which stimulate the NTS and cause nausea and vomiting.
  5. 5Hammer Throw 1,2,3: Same pathway: GI irritants cause serotonin release from enterochromaffin cells, 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents are activated, and the NTS triggers nausea and emesis.⭐ The 5-HT3 receptor is a ligand-gated ion channel, unlike most other serotonin receptors.
  6. 6Semicircular canal: Stimulation of the vestibular system (e.g. motion) causes vestibular nausea, mediated by M1 muscarinic and H1 histamine receptors.
  7. 7M1 parking spot: Vestibular input reaches the NTS via CN VIII, with M1 (muscarinic) and H1 (histamine) receptors as the key neurotransmitter receptors.
  8. 8Beehive: Histamine (H1) and acetylcholine (M1) receptor activation in the vestibular system drives the NTS (via CN VIII), producing vestibular nausea.
  9. 9Trigger Zone: The chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) senses emetogenic substances (drugs, toxins, metabolites) in the blood and CSF of the 4th ventricle. It is not protected by a well-developed blood-brain barrier.
  10. 10Pommel horse for posturing: The CTZ is located in the area postrema on the floor of the 4th ventricle.⭐ Area postrema lies outside the effective blood-brain barrier, so it can sample blood-borne emetogens.
  11. 11plaNK-1: NK-1 (neurokinin-1) receptors, which bind substance P, are found in the CTZ and contribute to nausea and vomiting.
  12. 12D-shaped rings: Dopamine D2 receptors are located in the CTZ; stimulation causes nausea and vomiting.
  13. 13Dancer blocking hammer throw: Ondansetron is a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist that blocks serotonin action at 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents. Others include granisetron, dolasetron and palonosetron (-setron suffix).⭐ The -setron drugs are first-line for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and for postoperative nausea.
  14. 14Chemical flask bottle, radiation spotlight, bacteria lamp, scalpel javelin: 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron treat nausea and vomiting caused by chemotherapy, radiation, GI infections (gastroenteritis) and surgery.
  15. 15Plunger baton: Constipation is an adverse effect of 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron.
  16. 16Hit in the head: Headache and dizziness are adverse effects of 5-HT3 antagonists such as ondansetron.
  17. 17Dancer's torsading ribbon: 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron) can prolong the QT interval and predispose to torsades de pointes.⭐ Use caution with other QT-prolonging drugs and electrolyte abnormalities.
  18. 18Pile of smiley faces: Ondansetron can contribute to serotonin syndrome, especially when combined with other serotonergic drugs such as SSRIs.⭐ Serotonin syndrome: hyperthermia, clonus, agitation, diarrhea, autonomic instability.
  19. 19Bee swatter: Antihistamines are H1 receptor antagonists. First-generation agents (diphenhydramine, meclizine, promethazine) cross the blood-brain barrier and block central H1 receptors.⭐ First-generation agents are sedating and have anticholinergic effects.
  20. 20Seasickness + life vests: H1 antagonists (antihistamines) and M1 antagonists (antimuscarinics) treat vestibular nausea such as seasickness and motion sickness.
  21. 21Telescope blocking the M1 parking spot: Scopolamine is a muscarinic (M1) receptor antagonist used for motion sickness, often as a transdermal patch.⭐ Anticholinergic effects: dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion.
  22. 22Fallen off the D rings: Metoclopramide is a D2 receptor antagonist. Others include prochlorperazine (also blocks H1 and M1 receptors) and olanzapine (an antipsychotic that also blocks serotonin receptors).⭐ Prochlorperazine and promethazine are phenothiazine antiemetics.
  23. 23Metal clogs: Metoclopramide acts as a D2 receptor antagonist, blocking dopamine signaling in the CTZ.⭐ It also has prokinetic activity (5-HT4 agonism in the gut).
  24. 24Chemical flasks: D2 antagonists such as metoclopramide are used to prevent and treat chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.
  25. 25Pushing out brown liquid: Metoclopramide increases gastric emptying (prokinetic) and is used for gastroparesis, for example in diabetic patients.
  26. 26Do not obstruct: Metoclopramide is contraindicated in small bowel (mechanical GI) obstruction because increased motility can cause harm or perforation.
  27. 27Brown liquid: Diarrhea is an adverse effect of D2 antagonists such as metoclopramide (increased GI motility).
  28. 28Crying at judge: Depression is an adverse effect of D2 antagonists including metoclopramide.
  29. 29Passed out judge: Drowsiness and fatigue are adverse effects of D2 antagonists including metoclopramide.
  30. 30Extra pyramid newspaper hat + stuck out tongue: D2 antagonists can cause extrapyramidal symptoms (acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism) and tardive dyskinesia with chronic use.⭐ Metoclopramide: avoid prolonged use (greater than 12 weeks) because of tardive dyskinesia risk.
  31. 31Now More Spicy!: Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (fever, rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, elevated CK) is a rare adverse effect of D2 antagonists including metoclopramide.⭐ Treat with stopping the drug, supportive care, dantrolene or bromocriptine.
  32. 32Shooting milk upwards: D2 blockade removes dopamine's inhibition of prolactin, causing hyperprolactinemia (galactorrhea, gynecomastia, amenorrhea).
  33. 33Torsades ribbon: QT prolongation is an adverse effect of D2 antagonists including metoclopramide.
  34. 34Substance Pee Check: Substance P activates NK-1 receptors in the CTZ, contributing to nausea and vomiting.
  35. 35All Participants: Aprepitant is an NK-1 receptor antagonist (-pitant suffix). It is typically combined with a 5-HT3 antagonist and dexamethasone for highly emetogenic chemotherapy.⭐ Aprepitant is a CYP3A4 substrate and moderate inhibitor; it is especially useful for delayed chemotherapy-induced emesis.
  36. 36Moon face: Glucocorticoids (dexamethasone, methylprednisolone), benzodiazepines and cannabinoids have additional antiemetic effects and may be added to regimens, particularly for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.⭐ Benzodiazepines (e.g. lorazepam) help anticipatory nausea.
  37. 37Ben's Diner advertisement: Glucocorticoids, benzodiazepines and synthetic cannabinoids (dronabinol, nabilone) are adjunct antiemetics for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.

2. H2 Receptor Blockers & PPIs

Exam snapshot
  • Acid secretion by parietal cells is stimulated by histamine (ECL, H2, Gs, cAMP), acetylcholine (vagus, M3, Gq) and gastrin (G cells, CCK-B, Gq); ECL histamine is gastrin's main route.
  • H2 blockers (-tidine: cimetidine, ranitidine, famotidine, nizatidine) block H2 receptors; used for GERD and duodenal ulcer but PPIs are first line.
  • Cimetidine: CYP450 inhibitor and antiandrogen (gynecomastia, impotence, raised prolactin).
  • PPIs (-prazole) irreversibly inhibit H+/K+ ATPase: first line for GERD, ulcers, H. pylori regimens and Zollinger-Ellison; adverse effects: C. diff, pneumonia, fractures, hypomagnesemia, reduced Ca/Fe/Mg/B12 absorption.
  • Somatostatin inhibits G cells and ECL cells; octreotide (long-acting analog) can treat gastrinoma/Zollinger-Ellison.
  1. 1Pour it all: Parietal cells of the gastric mucosa (body and fundus) are responsible for gastric acid (HCl) secretion.
  2. 2Battery powered puree pump: The H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) on the luminal (apical) membrane of the parietal cell pumps H+ into the stomach lumen.
  3. 3Banana into the pump, lemons out: The H+/K+ ATPase exchanges one K+ (into the cell) for one H+ (into the lumen).
  4. 4Sidewalk: The sidewalk represents the lumen of the stomach.
  5. 5Three P batters: The proton pump is an ATPase: acid secretion is an ATP-driven (active) process.
  6. 6Bees swarming honey pot: Histamine, released by ECL cells, binds H2 receptors on the parietal cell to stimulate acid secretion.
  7. 7Honey pot with 2 S handles: H2 receptors are on the basolateral membrane and are Gs-coupled, increasing cAMP (via adenylyl cyclase).⭐ Gs (H2) increases cAMP; Gq (M3, gastrin/CCK-B) increases IP3/Ca2+.
  8. 8Honey pot kid knocking over acid pitcher: H2 receptor activation (cAMP and protein kinase A) increases activity and insertion of the H+/K+ ATPase, increasing acid secretion.
  9. 9Enter CarefuLly: Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells are in the gastric mucosa near parietal cells.
  10. 10Bees released from ECL tree: ECL cells release histamine, which acts on parietal cells in a paracrine fashion.
  11. 11Gas Powered blower releasing bees from ECL tree: Gastrin, released by G cells, stimulates ECL cells to release histamine (this is gastrin's major route of stimulating acid secretion).
  12. 12Gas truck releasing gas tanks: G cells (in the gastric antrum) release gastrin, which acts on ECL cells and parietal cells.
  13. 13Gas powered acid pump: Gastrin also acts directly on parietal cell CCK-B (gastrin) receptors to stimulate H+ secretion, but this is a minor effect; its major effect is histamine release from ECL cells.
  14. 14Motorcycle attached to stand in M3: M3 muscarinic acetylcholine receptors are located on the parietal cell (Gq-coupled, increase IP3 and Ca2+).
  15. 15Vegas sticker: Vagal stimulation (acetylcholine) directly stimulates the parietal cell through M3 receptors.⭐ Vagus also acts on ECL cells (histamine release).
  16. 16Vegas Sticker: Vagal stimulation also causes G cells to release gastrin, via gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP).
  17. 17Gate release pull: Gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP, bombesin-like), released by vagal nerve endings, activates G cells.
  18. 182 bee swatters: H2 receptor antagonists (e.g. ranitidine, cimetidine) block histamine at the parietal cell H2 receptor and reduce acid secretion.⭐ H2 blockers reduce acid mainly by blocking histamine and are most effective for nocturnal acid secretion.
  19. 19Tie dye t shirt: H2 antagonists share the -tidine suffix: cimetidine, ranitidine, famotidine, nizatidine.⭐ Ranitidine was withdrawn from many markets because of NDMA contamination.
  20. 20Gargling: H2 blockers treat GERD (mild or intermittent symptoms), but PPIs are first line.
  21. 21Ulcerated sidewalk: H2 blockers can be used for duodenal ulcers, though PPIs are first line.
  22. 22Tie dye kid on the cement: Cimetidine is the H2 blocker with antiandrogenic effects and the most drug interactions and adverse effects of the class.
  23. 23Dented chrome bumper: Cimetidine inhibits the cytochrome P-450 system and raises levels of drugs metabolized by it (e.g. warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline).⭐ Famotidine and nizatidine have minimal CYP inhibition.
  24. 24Pot lids on chest: Cimetidine can cause gynecomastia with long-term or high-dose use (antiandrogen effect).
  25. 25Droopy honey wand: Cimetidine can cause impotence (erectile dysfunction) via antiandrogenic effects.
  26. 26Milk shooting from nose: Cimetidine can raise serum prolactin levels (galactorrhea/hyperprolactinemia).
  27. 27Girl scout blocking puree pump: Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) irreversibly bind and inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase, the final common pathway for acid secretion.⭐ PPIs are prodrugs activated in acidic canaliculi and work best taken 30 minutes before meals; effect lasts until new pumps are synthesized.
  28. 28PRIZE: PPIs share the -prazole suffix: omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole.
  29. 29Gargling: H2 blockers treat GERD, but PPIs are first line because they suppress acid more effectively.
  30. 30Ulcerated sidewalk: PPIs relieve symptoms and heal gastric and duodenal ulcers faster than H2 blockers.⭐ Discontinue NSAIDs and test for H. pylori in peptic ulcer disease.
  31. 31Jumbo gas tank on mower with crab logo: A gastrinoma causes excessive gastrin and gastric acid hypersecretion (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome), treated with high-dose PPIs.⭐ Zollinger-Ellison: refractory, multiple or distal ulcers, diarrhea; check fasting gastrin and secretin stimulation test; associated with MEN1.
  32. 32Swarm of bees over lawn mower: Excess gastrin acts mainly by increasing histamine release from ECL cells, which stimulates parietal cells (and also directly); PPIs are the best medical treatment.
  33. 33Helicopter hat: PPIs are part of H. pylori eradication regimens, combined with clarithromycin plus amoxicillin or metronidazole (triple therapy).⭐ Bismuth quadruple therapy: PPI, bismuth, metronidazole, tetracycline.
  34. 34Chocolate fondue fountain: PPIs increase the risk of Clostridioides difficile infection (loss of gastric acid barrier).
  35. 35Dirty lung spots: PPIs increase the risk of respiratory infections such as pneumonia.
  36. 36Medals bound to wagon: PPIs decrease absorption of Ca2+ (calcium carbonate), Mg2+ and iron, which need an acidic environment; also B12 can be reduced.
  37. 37Fractured Axel: Long-term PPI use increases the risk of osteoporotic fractures, including hip fracture, partly due to reduced calcium absorption.
  38. 38Porous wood: PPIs may worsen osteoporosis because of decreased calcium absorption.
  39. 39Falling magnets on girl scout: PPIs can cause hypomagnesemia (reduced Mg2+ absorption), which can lead to arrhythmias or tetany.
  40. 40Stop sign: Somatostatin (from D cells) inhibits histamine release from ECL cells, reducing acid secretion.
  41. 41Stop sign: Somatostatin (SST) inhibits gastrin release from G cells, and its analogs can act on SST-receptor positive gastrinomas.⭐ Acid in the antrum stimulates somatostatin, a negative feedback loop on gastrin.
  42. 42Octagon shape of stop sign: Octreotide, a long-acting somatostatin analog, inhibits histamine release/ECL cell activity, reducing acid secretion.
  43. 43Octagon: Octreotide inhibits G cells (gastrin release) and is useful in gastrinoma/Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.⭐ Octreotide is also used for carcinoid syndrome, acromegaly, VIPoma and variceal bleeding.

3. Laxatives

Exam snapshot
  • Stepwise treatment of constipation: bulk-forming (psyllium), stool softener (docusate), osmotic (Mg salts, PEG, lactulose), stimulant (senna, bisacodyl), then suppository or enema.
  • Osmotic laxatives draw water into the lumen: magnesium products (caution in CKD, chelate tetracyclines), PEG (colonoscopy prep), lactulose (hepatic encephalopathy).
  • Lactulose is metabolized by colonic bacteria to acids, trapping NH3 as NH4+; rifaximin kills ammonia-producing bacteria.
  • Stimulant laxatives (senna, bisacodyl) activate the enteric nervous system; chronic senna causes melanosis coli; overuse causes hypokalemia and dehydration.
  • Secretagogues: lubiprostone (ClC-2 chloride channel activator) and linaclotide (guanylate cyclase-C agonist, raises cGMP) for CIC/IBS-C; peripheral opioid antagonists for OIC.
  1. 1Toilet cleaning schedule: Constipation is defined by straining, hard stools and fewer than 3 bowel movements per week. It is common in elderly and debilitated patients.⭐ Common causes: low fiber/fluid intake, immobility, opioids, anticholinergics, hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia.
  2. 2Step-up stool: Constipation is treated stepwise: bulk-forming agent, then stool softener, then osmotic laxative, then stimulant laxative, then enema or suppository.
  3. 3Psyllium seaweed: Psyllium is a bulk-forming laxative: an indigestible hydrophilic colloid that absorbs water, distends the bowel, stimulates peristalsis and produces a bowel movement.⭐ Other bulk-formers: methylcellulose, polycarbophil. Safest for long-term use.
  4. 4Ocean water: Bulk-forming laxatives must be taken with adequate water to avoid obstruction or worsening constipation.
  5. 5SURFboard: Surfactant agents (stool softeners) soften stool by letting water and lipids penetrate into the stool.
  6. 6DOCKusate: Docusate is a commonly used stool softener, often combined with a stimulant such as senna.
  7. 7Red cross: Docusate is used in hospitalized patients to prevent constipation and minimize straining (e.g. after surgery or myocardial infarction).⭐ Evidence for efficacy is weak; it is mainly preventive.
  8. 8Mineral bath: Osmotic laxatives (magnesium compounds, lactulose, polyethylene glycol) are poorly absorbed substances that draw water into the intestinal lumen, causing distention and peristalsis.
  9. 9Magazine: Magnesium-based compounds (magnesium hydroxide, magnesium citrate) are osmotic laxatives.
  10. 10Mineral bath timer: Magnesium-based laxatives relieve acute constipation and help prevent chronic constipation (e.g. milk of magnesia).
  11. 11Shriveled kidney paperweight: Use magnesium-based agents cautiously in chronic kidney disease, because reduced renal excretion can cause hypermagnesemia.⭐ Hypermagnesemia: loss of deep tendon reflexes, hypotension, respiratory depression, cardiac arrest.
  12. 12Mineral spa filter: Magnesium-based osmotic laxatives can bind (chelate) other drugs such as tetracyclines and reduce their absorption.⭐ Fluoroquinolones also chelate with divalent cations.
  13. 13Peg drain cover: Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is an osmotic laxative; it is a non-absorbable polymer (not a sugar).
  14. 14Drain cleaning: Polyethylene glycol (high volume) is used for pre-procedural colonic cleansing, such as before colonoscopy.
  15. 15Relaxulose: Lactulose is an osmotic laxative: a non-absorbable sugar (disaccharide).
  16. 16Relaxing bubbles: Lactulose is metabolized by gut bacteria, producing gas that leads to cramping, bloating and flatus.
  17. 17Relaxulose in liver and brain coral tank: Lactulose is used to treat hepatic encephalopathy.
  18. 18Cirrhotic liver + brain coral: Hepatic encephalopathy is a neurologic complication of cirrhosis caused by buildup of ammonia and other gut-derived toxins that bypass hepatic clearance.⭐ Precipitants: GI bleed, infection, constipation, hypokalemia, sedatives, high protein load.
  19. 19Fishing out ammonia: Lactulose prevents absorption of ammonia (produced by gut bacteria) from the GI tract.
  20. 20Fish eating lactulose: Intestinal bacteria metabolize lactulose to acidic metabolites, causing an acidic shift in luminal pH; NH3 is converted to NH4+.
  21. 21Acidic pH meter: Bacterial metabolism of lactulose creates acidic metabolites and lowers colonic pH, favoring conversion of NH3 to NH4+.
  22. 22Positive net: Ammonia (NH3) is trapped as charged ammonium (NH4+) in the acidic lumen, which cannot be absorbed and is excreted in stool (ion trapping).⭐ Lactulose also increases stool frequency, speeding removal of ammonia.
  23. 23Fisherman kid: Rifaximin is a poorly absorbed antibiotic that reduces ammonia-producing intestinal bacteria and is used to treat (and prevent recurrence of) hepatic encephalopathy, along with lactulose.⭐ Rifaximin is also used for traveler's diarrhea and IBS-D.
  24. 24Mud bath + spilling water: Laxative use can cause diarrhea and dehydration (with electrolyte disturbances).
  25. 25Belly rub: Stimulant laxatives directly stimulate the enteric nervous system and increase colonic secretions and motility.
  26. 26Stimulating suntan lotion: Senna is a stimulant laxative (cathartic) that stimulates the enteric nervous system and colonic secretions.
  27. 27Sunny side effect: Chronic senna use can cause melanosis coli, a benign brown-black pigmentation of the colon due to toxic effects of anthraquinones on colonic epithelial cells (apoptosis and lipofuscin-like pigment).⭐ Melanosis coli is reversible after stopping the laxative and is not premalignant.
  28. 28BICYCodal: Bisacodyl is a stimulant laxative available in oral and rectal formulations.
  29. 29Trashed peanuts + banana + wing: Continuous use of stimulant laxatives can cause hypokalemia, salt loss and protein loss (protein-losing enteropathy), as well as dependence.⭐ Chronic laxative abuse: hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis (or non-gap acidosis from stool bicarbonate loss).
  30. 30OUT OF ORDER: Severe constipation may warrant a suppository or enema laxative.
  31. 31Fart darts: Suppositories (glycerin or bisacodyl formulations) are indicated for severe constipation; they act rapidly in the rectum.
  32. 32Super pooper: Enemas (e.g. warm tap water) are indicated for severe constipation to evacuate the bowel.⭐ Avoid phosphate enemas in kidney disease because of phosphate absorption.
  33. 33Lubricating oil: Lubiprostone is indicated for chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC), opioid-induced constipation (OIC) and IBS with constipation (IBS-C) in adult women.⭐ Adverse effects: nausea, diarrhea; avoid in pregnancy.
  34. 34Spilt chlorine: Lubiprostone activates chloride channels (ClC-2) on the intestinal lumen, causing secretion of chloride-rich fluid; the increased fluid raises intestinal motility and eases passage of stool.
  35. 35Line of clothes: Linaclotide is indicated for chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) and IBS with constipation (IBS-C).⭐ Adverse effect: diarrhea; contraindicated in children under 2 years (risk of dehydration).
  36. 36Grumpy Suds: Linaclotide is a guanylate cyclase-C agonist: it increases intracellular cGMP, which drives chloride secretion, increased luminal fluid and faster intestinal transit.⭐ cGMP also reduces visceral pain signaling in IBS-C.
  37. 37Dripping clothes: Linaclotide (guanylate cyclase-C agonist) raises cGMP, stimulating chloride (and bicarbonate) secretion, increasing luminal fluid and accelerating transit.
  38. 38Ruined Mutopia: Peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (e.g. methylnaltrexone, naloxegol, naldemedine) are used for opioid-induced constipation (OIC).⭐ Contraindicated in known or suspected mechanical bowel obstruction.

4. Antidiarrheal Agents

Exam snapshot
  • Antidiarrheals treat a symptom; avoid them in bloody diarrhea or fever.
  • Bismuth subsalicylate: antimicrobial and antisecretory; used for traveler's diarrhea; causes black stool and tongue, salicylate toxicity, Reye risk in children.
  • Loperamide (mu agonist, no CNS entry) and diphenoxylate-atropine (Lomotil) slow transit by increasing segmenting activity; CYP and P-gp inhibitors raise loperamide levels.
  • Eluxadoline (mu and kappa agonist, delta antagonist) treats IBS-D; risk of pancreatitis, contraindicated with gallbladder or pancreatic disease.
  • Octreotide (somatostatin analog) treats secretory diarrhea from VIPoma, carcinoid, chemotherapy, HIV, and short bowel.
  1. 1Poopy cleaning schedule: Diarrhea is 3 or more loose or watery stools per day. It is common and usually self-limited.
  2. 2Out of order: Diarrhea results from insufficient intestinal water absorption or excessive active water secretion. Acute causes are usually infectious; chronic causes are usually functional disorders or systemic illness.
  3. 3Muddy slippers on doormat: Antidiarrheal agents treat the symptom, not the cause. Diarrhea can stem from infection, systemic illness, or structural or functional disorders, so the underlying etiology still needs to be addressed.
  4. 4Red stool + inflammatory candle: Antidiarrheals are contraindicated in bloody diarrhea or fever, which suggest invasive or inflammatory infection (e.g., Shigella, EHEC, C. difficile). Slowing motility can prolong exposure and raise the risk of complications such as toxic megacolon or HUS.⭐ Avoid antimotility drugs in bloody diarrhea, fever, or suspected C. difficile or EHEC.
  5. 5Adsorptive sweeper: Adsorptive agents (kaolin-pectin, bismuth subsalicylate) bind diarrhea-causing toxins and block bacterial adhesion to the intestinal wall.
  6. 6Pink cleaning goop: Bismuth subsalicylate is antimicrobial (bismuth salts) and antisecretory (the salicylate inhibits prostaglandin action).
  7. 7Travel poster: Bismuth subsalicylate is commonly used for traveler's diarrhea. It is also used in H. pylori regimens (bismuth quadruple therapy).
  8. 8Reye's rays: Bismuth subsalicylate can cause salicylate toxicity and, like aspirin, raises the risk of Reye syndrome in children, especially with viral illness (influenza, varicella).⭐ Avoid in children with viral illness; Reye syndrome = hepatic steatosis plus encephalopathy.
  9. 9Dropping black stool: Bismuth subsalicylate causes transient, benign blackening of the stool and tongue. Do not mistake it for melena.
  10. 10Utopia (label garbled in OCR): Opioid agonists (diphenoxylate, loperamide) treat diarrhea.check original
  11. 11Sausage (label garbled in OCR): Opioid agonists act on mu-opioid receptors in the GI tract, slowing motility and reducing secretion.check original
  12. 12Lop-eared rabbit: Loperamide is a mu-opioid agonist that does NOT cross the blood-brain barrier at normal doses. It treats acute and chronic diarrhea, including diarrhea-predominant IBS and IBD-related diarrhea. It is available over the counter.⭐ Because it lacks CNS effects, loperamide has low abuse potential at standard doses.
  13. 13Lop-eared rabbit hopping back and forth: Opioids such as loperamide increase colonic phasic segmenting (non-propulsive) activity. This slows colonic transit and increases water absorption, giving firmer stool. Adverse effects include constipation, abdominal distension, and (rarely) toxic megacolon.
  14. 14Deluxe Massage Chairs: Eluxadoline is a prescription-only drug for diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D).
  15. 15Mu Kappa Delta shirt: Eluxadoline is a mu and kappa opioid receptor agonist and a delta opioid receptor antagonist.
  16. 16Pancreatitis sponge: Eluxadoline is associated with pancreatitis and is contraindicated in patients with a history of pancreatic disease or biliary (gallbladder) disease, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, or heavy alcohol use.⭐ Contraindicated without a gallbladder or with prior pancreatitis.
  17. 17Dolphins: Diphenoxylate is a mu-opioid agonist that can cross the blood-brain barrier to some degree. It is combined with atropine to discourage abuse.
  18. 18Atropine Alice: Atropine is combined with diphenoxylate (Lomotil) in subtherapeutic doses. At high doses it causes unpleasant anticholinergic effects, deterring overdose or abuse of the opioid (euphoria, physical dependence).⭐ Lomotil overdose in a child: respiratory depression plus anticholinergic signs; treat with naloxone.
  19. 19Warning sign: All opioids carry abuse potential, including misuse at non-therapeutic doses. Loperamide levels can rise with CYP inhibitors (e.g., cimetidine, grapefruit juice) or P-glycoprotein inhibitors (e.g., erythromycin), increasing systemic and CNS exposure.⭐ Loperamide at very high doses can cause QT prolongation and arrhythmias.
  20. 20VIP customers only crab: VIPoma and carcinoid tumors cause secretory diarrhea (VIP and serotonin respectively).⭐ VIPoma: watery diarrhea, hypokalemia, achlorhydria (WDHA).
  21. 21Octagon stop sign: Octreotide, a somatostatin analog, treats the symptoms of VIPoma and carcinoid syndrome (secretory diarrhea). It can also be used for diarrhea from chemotherapy, HIV, or short bowel syndrome.⭐ Octreotide is also used for acromegaly, variceal bleeding, and gallstone risk is an adverse effect.

5. TNF Inhibitors

Exam snapshot
  • Infliximab (chimeric), adalimumab and golimumab (human), certolizumab (PEGylated Fab) are anti-TNF-alpha mAbs; etanercept is a TNF receptor-IgG1 Fc decoy.

6. First-Generation Antipsychotics

Exam snapshot
  • FGAs block D2 receptors; used for schizophrenia (positive symptoms), mania, agitation, and Tourette syndrome.
  • High potency (haloperidol, fluphenazine, trifluoperazine, pimozide): more EPS - dystonia (minutes), akathisia (days), parkinsonism (weeks), tardive dyskinesia (months to years).
  • Low potency (chlorpromazine, thioridazine): anticholinergic, antihistamine (sedation), and alpha-1 blocking effects; corneal deposits (chlorpromazine), retinal deposits (thioridazine).
  • Other toxicities: QT prolongation and torsades, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, hyperprolactinemia.
  • Treatment: benztropine for dystonia and parkinsonism, propranolol for akathisia, VMAT2 inhibitors for tardive dyskinesia, dantrolene for NMS.

  16. 16Theory of Art: Low-potency FGAs include thioridazine (sounds like Theory of Art) and chlorpromazine.

7. Second-Generation Antipsychotics

Exam snapshot
  • SGAs (quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, ziprasidone, clozapine) antagonize D2 and 5-HT2A receptors; aripiprazole is a partial D2 agonist.
  • Treat schizophrenia (positive and negative symptoms); also bipolar disorder, adjunct in treatment-resistant depression and OCD, and Tourette (risperidone).
  • Side effects from receptor blockade: H1 (sedation, weight gain), alpha-1 (orthostatic hypotension), muscarinic (anticholinergic, esp. clozapine and olanzapine).
  • Metabolic syndrome (weight gain, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia) mainly with olanzapine and clozapine; clozapine also causes agranulocytosis, myocarditis and seizures.
  • EPS, hyperprolactinemia and NMS are less frequent than with FGAs (risperidone is worst for EPS/prolactin); QT prolongation and torsades risk (ziprasidone).
  1. 1Atypical surrealist: Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics (SGAs): the newer antipsychotic class, with antagonism of both D2 and 5-HT2A receptors and generally fewer extrapyramidal effects than first-generation agents.

  3. 3Zipper: Ziprasidone is an SGA.⭐ Ziprasidone has notable QT prolongation; take with food for absorption.
  4. 4Closet: Clozapine is an SGA, reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and for reducing suicidality in schizophrenia, because of its serious toxicities.⭐ Clozapine is the most effective antipsychotic for refractory schizophrenia.
  5. 5Snapping double rope: SGAs block D2 receptors in the CNS (mesolimbic pathway), reducing positive psychotic symptoms.
  6. 6Cut smiley cake: SGAs also block serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, which is thought to contribute to efficacy against negative symptoms and to the lower rate of EPS and hyperprolactinemia compared with FGAs.
  7. 7Hearing positive and negative voices: SGAs treat schizophrenia, including both positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) and negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition).⭐ FGAs mainly help positive symptoms; SGAs have better (though modest) effect on negative symptoms.
  8. 8Happy and sad masks: SGAs can treat depression, as adjunctive therapy in treatment-resistant depression (e.g. aripiprazole, quetiapine) and in bipolar depression/mania.

  12. 12Bee swatter: SGAs block H1 histamine receptors, causing sedation and contributing to weight gain.⭐ Most H1 blockade: olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine.
  13. 13Extinguished alpha candle: SGAs block alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, causing orthostatic hypotension, dizziness and reflex tachycardia.
  14. 14Antimuscarinic tea party: SGAs, especially clozapine (and olanzapine), block muscarinic receptors: dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention.⭐ Clozapine can paradoxically cause sialorrhea (drooling) despite its anticholinergic activity.
  15. 15Obscured tea party: SGAs generally have lower muscarinic affinity than low-potency FGAs (e.g. chlorpromazine, thioridazine), so antimuscarinic effects are milder overall.
  16. 16Passed out: Sedation and orthostatic hypotension result from H1 and alpha-1 blockade respectively; counsel on slow position changes and start low in older patients.
  17. 17Fat face: SGAs, especially olanzapine and clozapine, cause weight gain (metabolic syndrome risk).⭐ Lowest metabolic risk: aripiprazole and ziprasidone.
  18. 18Elevated butter: Olanzapine and clozapine can cause dyslipidemia (raised triglycerides and cholesterol).
  19. 19Bunch of candy: Olanzapine and clozapine can cause hyperglycemia and new-onset type 2 diabetes. Monitor weight, glucose/HbA1c and lipids.

8. SSRIs, SNRIs, Cyproheptadine

Exam snapshot
  • SSRIs (fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, citalopram/escitalopram) block presynaptic serotonin reuptake; SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine) block serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake.
  • First-line for depression, GAD, panic disorder, PTSD, social anxiety; SSRIs also for OCD and bulimia; SNRIs also for diabetic neuropathy, chronic pain and fibromyalgia.

  • Adverse effects: sexual dysfunction, weight gain, SIADH/hyponatremia, drowsiness or activation; SNRIs can raise blood pressure.

9. Tricyclic Antidepressants

Exam snapshot
  • TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine, desipramine, clomipramine) block reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin.

10. MAO Inhibitors

Exam snapshot
  • MAO-A metabolizes serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine (and dietary tyramine in the gut); MAO-B mainly metabolizes dopamine.
  • Nonselective, irreversible MAOIs: tranylcypromine, phenelzine, isocarboxazid; used for atypical and treatment-resistant depression, not first line.
  • Selegiline is a selective MAO-B inhibitor that raises CNS dopamine; used in Parkinson disease.
  • Tyramine (aged cheese, cured meats, wine) with an MAOI causes hypertensive crisis; manage with phentolamine.
  • MAOIs plus SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs or other serotonergic drugs cause serotonin syndrome.

16. Varenicline

Exam snapshot
  • Nicotine is a nicotinic ACh receptor agonist (ligand-gated Na+/Ca2+ channel) causing dopamine release in the reward pathway; tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal drive relapse; CYP2A6 metabolizes it.
  • Varenicline is a partial agonist at alpha4-beta2 nicotinic receptors: relieves craving and withdrawal while blocking nicotine reward; used for smoking cessation, 12-week course.
  • Titrate up (0.5 mg daily x3 days, 0.5 mg BID x4 days, then 1 mg BID); reduce dose when CrCl is below 30 mL/min.
  • Adverse effects: nausea/vomiting (most common), constipation, flatulence, vivid dreams; also neuropsychiatric effects, seizure risk, alcohol intolerance, possible CV events.
  • Avoid varenicline with high-dose NRT; NRT is titrated down. After quitting smoking, CYP1A2 induction is lost, so theophylline, warfarin, clozapine levels can rise (monitor/adjust).
  1. 1Smokey holding up 1 claw: Smoking is the leading cause of preventable disease and death in the US (lung cancer, COPD, CAD, stroke, PAD). Cessation counseling plus pharmacotherapy at every visit is high-yield.⭐ Combining counseling with medication roughly doubles quit rates versus either alone.
  2. 2Trail map with interconnecting paths: Tobacco addiction is multifactorial: pharmacologic (nicotine), learned/conditioned cues, genetic, and social/environmental factors all interact, so treatment is usually multimodal.
  3. 3Happy smoking hiker with salty sodium peanuts, Calci-Yum milk, rope: Nicotine is an agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (ligand-gated cation channels) in the brain. Channel opening causes Na+ influx and depolarization, which opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels for further Ca2+ influx and triggers neurotransmitter release (notably dopamine in the mesolimbic reward pathway), improving mood and producing reward.⭐ Varenicline targets the alpha4-beta2 nicotinic receptor subtype.
  4. 4Frayed reinforcements + dependence: With repeated exposure, tolerance develops and the primary reinforcing effects diminish. Physical dependence follows, so withdrawal symptoms (irritability, anxiety, poor concentration, increased appetite, insomnia, craving) occur when nicotine is absent.
  5. 5P450 license plate: Nicotine levels depend on rate of metabolism, mainly by CYP2A6. Faster metabolizers clear nicotine quickly and tend to smoke more and have more difficulty quitting.
  6. 6Replacement Firefighter Nico: Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) substitutes nicotine from tobacco with safer sources (gum, lozenges, patches, inhaler, nasal spray) that avoid tar and combustion products. It is designed to be titrated down over time. Patch plus a short-acting form (gum or lozenge) is more effective than a single form.
  7. 7Firefighter Varenica: Varenicline is a partial agonist at nicotinic receptors (alpha4-beta2) indicated for smoking cessation. It provides modest receptor stimulation (reducing craving and withdrawal) while blocking nicotine from the receptor, which reduces the reward from smoking.⭐ Among the most effective single agents for cessation; superior to bupropion and to single-form NRT in trials.
  8. 8Pressure adjuster: Varenicline is started with upward dose titration: 0.5 mg once daily for 3 days, then 0.5 mg twice daily for 4 days, then 1 mg twice daily; a usual course is 12 weeks. Patients typically set a quit date about 1 week after starting. Slow titration limits nausea.
  9. 9Falling brain helmet: Neuropsychiatric adverse effects have been reported (mood changes, agitation, psychosis, hallucinations, suicidal ideation or attempts). Monitor patients for mood and behavior changes and stop the drug if they occur.⭐ The former boxed warning was removed after large trials showed no significant excess of serious neuropsychiatric events, but monitoring is still advised.
  10. 10Shaking ladder: Varenicline has been associated with new or worsening seizures; use caution in patients with a seizure history.
  11. 11Alcohol warning sign: Varenicline may increase the effects of alcohol (decreased tolerance, unusual or aggressive behavior, amnesia). Patients should reduce alcohol intake until they know how varenicline affects them.
  12. 12Heart patch + Varenica balancing: Varenicline may increase the risk of cardiovascular events (MI, stroke), though the data are limited. This concern should be weighed against the substantial cardiovascular benefit of quitting smoking.
  13. 13No Road Thru: Combining varenicline with high-dose NRT is associated with intolerable adverse effects (nausea, headache, dizziness) and treatment discontinuation, so avoid the combination.
  14. 14Damaged P450 license plate: Smoking cessation can change the pharmacokinetics of other drugs. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in smoke induce CYP1A2, so after quitting CYP1A2 activity falls and levels of substrates such as theophylline (and warfarin, clozapine, olanzapine) can rise toward toxicity. Consider therapeutic drug monitoring and dose reduction.⭐ Classic: patient on theophylline or clozapine quits smoking and develops toxicity.
  15. 15Adjustable, shrivelled kidney tank on LOW: Varenicline is renally eliminated, largely unchanged. Reduce the dose in severe renal impairment (estimated CrCl below 30 mL/min), with a lower maximum daily dose.
  16. 16Vomiting firefighter: Nausea and vomiting are the most common adverse effects of varenicline; taking it with food and a full glass of water helps.⭐ Nausea is the most common adverse effect and the usual reason for discontinuation.
  17. 17Blocked truck: Constipation is a common adverse effect of varenicline.
  18. 18Tailpipe exhaust: Flatulence is a common adverse effect of varenicline.
  19. 19Dreaming bear: Patients may report vivid, unusual, or strange dreams (and insomnia) with varenicline.

17. Amphetamine, Dexamphetamine, Lisdexamfetamine, Methylphenidate

Exam snapshot
  • Indications: ADHD (all four), narcolepsy (methylphenidate and amphetamines), binge eating disorder (lisdexamfetamine).
  • Amphetamines: TAAR1 agonism, VMAT2 inhibition, and reversal of DAT/NET cause release of dopamine and NE; methylphenidate simply blocks DAT/NET reuptake.
  • Lisdexamfetamine is a long-acting prodrug (lysine cleaved in RBCs to dextroamphetamine) with lower euphoria and abuse potential.
  • Contraindications: MAOI use or within 14 days, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma; caution with substance abuse, psychiatric and cardiovascular disease.
  • Adverse effects: insomnia, weight loss, nausea, headache; avoid afternoon/evening dosing; monitor weight, CV function, and height in children.
  1. 1AD/HD shirt: Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the primary indication for amphetamine, dextroamphetamine, lisdexamfetamine, and methylphenidate. Stimulants are first-line pharmacotherapy.⭐ Paradoxically calming in ADHD because they enhance prefrontal dopamine and norepinephrine signaling (attention and executive control).
  2. 2Dog binging on picnic: Moderate to severe binge eating disorder can be treated with psychostimulants; lisdexamfetamine is the FDA-approved agent for this.
  3. 3Narcoleptic pig + CH3 collar: Narcolepsy is another indication (classically methylphenidate; amphetamines are also used). The CH3 collar marks methylphenidate.
  4. 4Rope: Dopamine, the monoamine neurotransmitter represented by the rope, mediates reward, motivation, and attention.
  5. 5NE compass: Norepinephrine (NE, noradrenaline) is the second key monoamine; it promotes alertness, attention, and sympathetic activation.
  6. 6Hyper dogs releasing ropes: Amphetamines increase release of monoamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, and some serotonin) from presynaptic terminals, rather than just blocking reuptake.
  7. 7Tar pit: Amphetamine is an agonist of trace amine-associated receptor 1 (TAAR1), an intracellular receptor in monoaminergic neurons; this signaling promotes transporter reversal and release of monoamines.
  8. 8V-neck + Mat the dog walker: Vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) packages cytosolic monoamines (dopamine, NE, serotonin) into synaptic vesicles.⭐ Reserpine and tetrabenazine also inhibit VMAT2, but they deplete monoamines.
  9. 9Inhibiting Mat from packing ropes: Amphetamine inhibits VMAT2, so dopamine is not stored in vesicles. Cytosolic dopamine rises and becomes available for reverse transport out of the neuron.
  10. 10"Best Phriend of Mine" dog vest: Identifies amphetamine (the stimulant that acts as the releasing agent) in the scene.
  11. 11Turnstile: Plasma membrane monoamine transporters (dopamine transporter DAT, norepinephrine transporter NET/NAT) normally reuptake dopamine and NE from the synapse, ending their action.
  12. 12Turnstile spinning in reverse + dogs escaping with rope: Amphetamines reverse the direction of DAT and NET, so instead of reuptake, dopamine and norepinephrine flow out of the neuron into the synapse (non-vesicular efflux), increasing synaptic levels.
  13. 13Sleeping pig with CH3 collar blocking turnstile: Methylphenidate is a reuptake blocker of DAT and NET; it increases synaptic dopamine and NE without causing substantial release.⭐ Amphetamine = releaser and reuptake reverser; methylphenidate = pure reuptake blocker.
  14. 14LISt: LISdexamfetamine: the LIS in the name is the mnemonic for lisdexamfetamine.
  15. 15PROduct: Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug: it is inactive until red blood cells hydrolyze it, cleaving off lysine to release active dextroamphetamine.
  16. 16Long dog: Lisdexamfetamine is long-acting because conversion to the active drug is gradual (once-daily dosing).
  17. 17Low belly: Because activation is rate-limited and slow, it produces less euphoria and has lower abuse and diversion potential than immediate-release amphetamines. It is also less effective if taken by non-oral routes.
  18. 18"We've been mouse free for 14 days": Contraindicated during or within 14 days of stopping a monoamine oxidase inhibitor, because of the risk of hypertensive crisis from excessive sympathetic activation (and serotonin-type reactions).
  19. 19"Prevent Dog Abuse": Precaution in patients with a history of drug abuse or dependence; stimulants are Schedule II controlled substances with misuse and diversion risk.
  20. 20Red bow tie: Contraindicated in hyperthyroidism (neck bow tie represents the thyroid) because of additive sympathetic overactivation (tachycardia, arrhythmia).
  21. 21Frozen, chromatic shaved ice: Contraindicated in pheochromocytoma (chromaffin cell tumor) because of risk of hypertensive crisis from sympathetic overactivation.
  22. 22Brain hat: Use with caution in psychiatric disorders; stimulants can precipitate or worsen psychosis, mania, anxiety, agitation, or tics.
  23. 23Heart patch: Use with caution in cardiovascular disorders; stimulants raise heart rate and blood pressure and are linked to arrhythmia and sudden death in those with structural heart disease.
  24. 24Stimulated, awake person: Insomnia is a common side effect owing to increased alertness.
  25. 25Thin person: Weight loss and decreased appetite are common; in children this can lead to growth suppression.
  26. 26Nauseated: Nausea (and abdominal pain) is a common side effect.
  27. 27Tight headband: Headache is a common side effect.
  28. 28"Park Closes at Noon": Avoid dosing in the early afternoon or evening to minimize insomnia.
  29. 29Scales: Monitor weight (assessment of weight loss and appetite suppression).
  30. 30EKG machine: Monitor cardiovascular function (heart rate, blood pressure, ECG as appropriate).
  31. 31Measuring length: Monitor height in children, because chronic stimulant use can slow growth velocity.
