1. Brainstem Overview

The brainstem consists of three levels — medulla oblongata, pons, and midbrain — each identifiable by its cranial nerve attachments, internal landmarks, and cross-sectional pattern. Understanding which structures cross (decussate) at each level is the single most important skill for localizing brainstem lesions. The general rule: motor tracts cross in the lower medulla; sensory tracts cross at their respective nuclei.

Cranial nerve levels: Midbrain = CN III–IV  |  Pons = CN V–VIII  |  Medulla = CN IX–XII. CN IV is the only cranial nerve to exit dorsally and the only one to decussate before exiting. CN VI is the most common false localizing sign with raised ICP (stretches over the petrous ridge).

The descending sympathetic tract runs ipsilaterally through the entire lateral tegmentum of the brainstem from hypothalamus to the ciliospinal center (C8–T2). A lesion anywhere along this tract produces ipsilateral Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) — a key localizing finding in lateral brainstem strokes.

Two aminergic nuclei in the brainstem are high-yield: the locus ceruleus (rostral pons) is the primary source of norepinephrine for the entire CNS, and the raphe nuclei (distributed throughout the brainstem midline) are the primary source of serotonin. Both modulate arousal, mood, and pain.

The Rule of 4's — Rapid Stroke Localization

The Rule of 4's is the primary clinical heuristic for rapidly localizing brainstem strokes. It categorizes structures into midline (M) and lateral (S) groups that correspond to the medial and lateral vascular territories respectively. A crossed deficit — ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy plus contralateral long-tract signs — is the hallmark of a brainstem lesion.

4 Medial (M) structures — supplied by paramedian arteries (ASA or basilar perforators):
Motor pathway (CST)  |  Medial lemniscus (proprioception/vibration)  |  MLF (conjugate gaze)  |  Medial CN XII (hypoglossal)

4 Lateral (S) structures — supplied by circumferential arteries (PICA, AICA, SCA):
Spinocerebellar tract (ipsilateral ataxia)  |  Spinothalamic tract (contralateral pain/temp body)  |  Spinal trigeminal (ipsilateral face pain/temp)  |  Sympathetic tract (ipsilateral Horner's)

Medial lesion → hemiparesis + DCML loss + CN XII palsy
Lateral lesion → ataxia + alternating hemianesthesia + Horner's

2. Medulla Oblongata

The medulla is the most caudal brainstem segment, beginning at the spinomedullary junction — defined by a horizontal line just above the first pair of cervical nerves, approximately at the level of the middle of the odontoid process (dens of C2). Its superior boundary is the pontomedullary junction. It contains the vital respiratory and cardiovascular centers, and is the site of the two major decussations that define contralateral motor and sensory control.

Center Function Clinical Impact if Destroyed
Cardiovascular center Cardioinhibitory + cardioacceleratory centers; vasomotor center controls vascular tone Fatal fluctuations in heart rate and blood pressure
Respiratory center Controls rate and depth of breathing; rhythmicity (receives input from pons) Immediate respiratory arrest
Reflex centers Emesis, deglutition, coughing, hiccupping, sneezing Dysphagia; loss of protective airway reflexes

External Features

The ventral surface shows the pyramids flanking the ventral median fissure — these are the corticospinal tracts before they cross. The olives bulge laterally and overlie the inferior olivary nuclei. Dorsally, the gracile and cuneate tubercles mark the underlying nuclei of the dorsal column system; the caudal medulla forms the floor of the fourth ventricle. The inferior cerebellar peduncle (restiform body) connects the medulla to the cerebellum on each side. CN XII emerges from the pre-olivary sulcus (anterolateral sulcus, between the pyramid and olive); CNs IX, X, and XI emerge from the post-olivary sulcus (retro-olivary sulcus, lateral to the olive). At the pontomedullary junction, the striae medullares mark the division between pons and medulla on the posterior (floor of fourth ventricle) surface. CNs VI, VII, and VIII emerge at this junction in medial-to-lateral order: VI is most medial, VII between the olive and lower pons, and VIII most lateral.

Internal Features by Level

The medulla is conventionally described at three cross-sectional levels, each defined by a key internal landmark.

Caudal Medulla — Level of the Motor (Pyramidal) Decussation

At this level the corticospinal tract fibers cross the midline (approximately 85–90% of fibers), producing the pyramidal decussation visible on the ventral surface. This is why upper motor neuron lesions above this level cause contralateral weakness. The spinal trigeminal nucleus (carrying pain and temperature from the ipsilateral face) is present throughout the medulla and continues down to C2.

Motor decussation = caudal medulla (pyramidal fibers cross). Sensory decussation = mid medulla (internal arcuate fibers — DCML system crosses after synapsing in gracile/cuneate nuclei). These two decussations occur at different levels.

Mid Medulla — Level of the Sensory (Arcuate) Decussation

The dorsal column fibers — fasciculus gracilis (lower limb) and fasciculus cuneatus (upper limb and trunk) — synapse in the gracile and cuneate nuclei of the dorsal medulla. Their second-order axons sweep ventrally as the internal arcuate fibers, decussate at the mid-medulla, and ascend as the medial lemniscus, carrying vibration, proprioception, and discriminative touch to the contralateral cortex.

Rostral Medulla — Level of the Olive

The rostral medulla contains the greatest density of functionally important nuclei. The inferior olivary nucleus is the exclusive source of climbing fiber afferents to the cerebellum (olivocerebellar pathway via the inferior cerebellar peduncle). The nucleus solitarius receives all visceral afferents (CN VII, IX, X) — its rostral portion is the gustatory nucleus. The nucleus ambiguus provides branchiomotor (SVE) output for CN IX, X, and the cranial root of XI, controlling the pharynx, larynx, and soft palate. The hypoglossal nucleus (CN XII) lies beneath the hypoglossal triangle in the floor of the fourth ventricle. The dorsal motor nucleus of vagus is the preganglionic parasympathetic source for thoracic and abdominal viscera. The area postrema on the floor of the fourth ventricle lacks a blood-brain barrier — this is the chemoreceptor trigger zone for vomiting. Vestibular nuclei and the dorsal/ventral cochlear nuclei also appear at this level. The medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) runs close to the midline, coordinating gaze across all brainstem levels.

Inferior olivary nucleus: only structure that sends climbing fibers to the cerebellum. Each climbing fiber contacts one Purkinje cell with high-frequency, powerful excitation. Projects via the inferior cerebellar peduncle (contralateral after decussation in the medulla).

Clinical Syndromes

Medullary vascular syndromes follow a reliable pattern based on which vessel is occluded and which structures lie in that vascular territory. The two classic syndromes are medial and lateral.

Medial Medullary Syndrome (Dejerine Syndrome / Inferior Alternating Hemiplegia / Crossed Hypoglossal Hemiplegia)
Vessel: Anterior spinal artery or vertebral artery branch.
Structures hit: Pyramids (CST), medial lemniscus, CN XII nucleus or fascicles.
Signs: Contralateral hemiplegia; contralateral loss of vibration and proprioception (medial lemniscus crosses at mid medulla, so already crossed); ipsilateral tongue weakness with deviation toward the lesion (LMN CN XII — tongue goes toward the weak side).
Lateral Medullary Syndrome — Wallenberg's Syndrome (Alternating Hemianesthesia)
Vessel: Posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) or vertebral artery.
Structures hit: Spinal trigeminal nucleus, spinothalamic tract, descending sympathetic tract, inferior cerebellar peduncle, nucleus ambiguus, vestibular nuclei.
Signs: Ipsilateral face pain/temp loss (spinal trigeminal nucleus); contralateral body pain/temp loss (spinothalamic tract, already crossed); ipsilateral Horner's syndrome; dysphagia and hoarseness (nucleus ambiguus — CN IX, X); ipsilateral limb ataxia (inferior cerebellar peduncle); vertigo, nausea, and nystagmus (vestibular nuclei). Note: vibration/proprioception is SPARED (medial lemniscus is intact).
Wallenberg's = PICA territory = lateral structures: Pain and temperature on the face (ipsilateral) and body (contralateral) are split — this crossed sensory pattern combined with Horner's and dysphagia is pathognomonic for lateral medullary syndrome.

Horner's syndrome — full sign set: Miosis (iris dilator paralysis) | Ptosis (superior tarsal muscle) | Anhidrosis (ipsilateral facial sweating loss) | Enophthalmos (sunken eye — orbital fat loculi lose sympathetic supply) | Vasodilation (flushing of ipsilateral face/neck — loss of vasomotor tone). Exam tip: Horner's = small pupil; CN III palsy = large pupil.

3. Pons

The pons lies between the medulla and midbrain. It is divided into a ventral basilar part (motor and cerebellar relay) and a dorsal tegmentum (cranial nerve nuclei, ascending sensory tracts). It contains CN V–VIII nuclei and is the site of conjugate horizontal gaze control.

External Features

The ventral surface shows the prominent basilar groove housing the basilar artery, flanked by the massive middle cerebellar peduncles (brachium pontis) — the largest peduncles, carrying mainly afferent signals from pontine nuclei to the contralateral cerebellum. Transverse pontine fibers cross the ventral surface. CN V attaches at the mid-pons; CNs VI, VII, and VIII attach at the pontomedullary junction.

Internal Features

Basilar (Ventral) Part

The basilar pons contains scattered corticospinal fibers (which become the organized pyramids in the medulla), pontine nuclei, and transverse pontocerebellar fibers. Cortical signals reach the pontine nuclei via the corticopontine pathway (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital cortex), which relay to the contralateral cerebellum via the middle cerebellar peduncle. This corticopontocerebellar pathway carries voluntary motor planning signals to the lateral cerebellar hemisphere.

Tegmentum — Caudal Pons (Level of CN VI and VII)

The abducens nucleus (CN VI) lies beneath the facial colliculus in the floor of the fourth ventricle. The facial nerve fascicles loop dorsally around the abducens nucleus, forming the internal genu of the facial nerve — this is why a lesion at the facial colliculus produces both CN VI and CN VII palsies. The facial motor nucleus itself lies ventrolaterally. The trapezoid body (second-order auditory fibers crossing the midline) is also visible at this level. Importantly, at this level the medial lemniscus rotates 90° from a vertical to a horizontal orientation, now lying ventral to the trapezoid body and dorsal to the basilar pontine fibers — this rotation marks the tegmental-basilar boundary and is a key landmark in cross-sectional identification.

A lesion at the facial colliculus hits the abducens nucleus + genu of facial nerve → ipsilateral CN VI palsy + ipsilateral complete (LMN) facial palsy. This combination localizes the lesion to the caudal pons floor of the fourth ventricle.

Tegmentum — Rostral Pons (Level of CN V)

The trigeminal nerve has three sensory nuclei in the brainstem: the chief (principal) sensory nucleus in the rostral pons processes discriminative (fine) touch from the face; the spinal trigeminal nucleus (pons to C2) processes pain and temperature; and the mesencephalic nucleus in the midbrain handles proprioception from the jaw muscles — notably, this is the only sensory nucleus in the CNS whose primary afferent cell bodies reside within the CNS (rather than in a ganglion). The jaw-jerk reflex depends on this nucleus. The motor nucleus of V provides branchiomotor output to muscles of mastication.

The CN V motor nucleus provides SVE output to the muscles of mastication (1st pharyngeal arch derivatives): masseter, temporalis, medial and lateral pterygoids, tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini, and anterior belly of digastric. A CN V motor lesion causes weakness of jaw clenching and side-to-side jaw movement with deviation toward the side of the lesion on opening.

The paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF) in the rostral-to-mid pons controls ipsilateral horizontal gaze. The superior cerebellar peduncles are visible in the lateral walls of the fourth ventricle at this level; the superior medullary velum spans between them to form the roof of the fourth ventricle.

Ocular Pathways and Gaze

Conjugate horizontal gaze requires coordinated activation of the PPRF, abducens nucleus, and MLF. The contralateral frontal eye field (FEF) initiates the movement by driving the PPRF, which simultaneously activates the ipsilateral lateral rectus and — via the ascending MLF — the contralateral medial rectus.

INO: MLF lesion severs the "Via MLF" branch — ipsilateral eye cannot adduct on contralateral gaze; convergence preserved (different pathway). PPRF lesion abolishes both branches — all ipsilateral conjugate gaze fails.

Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO): MLF lesion between CN VI and CN III nuclei. On attempted lateral gaze toward the lesion side: the ipsilateral eye cannot adduct (medial rectus fails); the abducting contralateral eye shows nystagmus. Adduction on convergence is preserved (because convergence uses a different pathway). Bilateral INO = multiple sclerosis until proven otherwise.
One-and-a-half syndrome: Combined lesion of the PPRF (or abducens nucleus) AND the ipsilateral MLF on the same side. Result: the ipsilateral eye cannot move horizontally at all (both abduction via PPRF/VI and adduction via MLF are lost); the contralateral eye can only abduct. The only remaining horizontal movement is abduction of the contralateral eye — a "one-and-a-half" loss of horizontal gaze.

8-and-a-half syndrome: One-and-a-half syndrome plus ipsilateral CN VII fascicular palsy → adds ipsilateral LMN facial paralysis. The "half" is borrowed from the VII nerve (1.5 + 7 = 8.5). Both localize to a unilateral caudal pontine tegmentum lesion.

The vestibuloocular reflex (VOR) stabilizes gaze during head movement. The doll's eye maneuver tests this reflex; intact doll's eyes (eyes move opposite to head) confirm a structurally intact brainstem from medulla to midbrain. The pupillary light reflex projects bilaterally at the pretectal level — this bilateral projection explains the consensual light response.

4. Midbrain

The midbrain is the most rostral brainstem segment, continuous with the diencephalon above. It is divided into the dorsal tectum, the tegmentum, the substantia nigra, and the ventral crus cerebri (basis pedunculi). It contains CN III and CN IV nuclei and houses the periaqueductal gray matter — a key pain modulation center.

External Features

The dorsal surface shows the superior and inferior colliculi (corpora quadrigemina) flanked by the superior cerebellar peduncles. The ventral surface shows the paired crura cerebri separated by the interpeduncular fossa, where CN III exits and where the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus are visible. The cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius runs through the center of the midbrain, connecting the third ventricle to the fourth ventricle — obstruction here causes obstructive hydrocephalus. CN IV exits dorsally and decussates immediately — the only cranial nerve with both of these properties.

"Mickey Mouse" cross-section of the midbrain: The two crura cerebri form the large "ears"; the red nuclei are the "eyes" in the tegmentum; the substantia nigra forms the dark "grin" between the tegmentum and the crus cerebri. The cerebral aqueduct is the central midline landmark (the "head"). Use this to orient any midbrain cross-section: ventral = big ears (crus cerebri); dorsal = tectum (colliculi).

Internal Features

Tectum (Dorsal Midbrain)

The superior colliculi are visual reflex and orienting centers — they receive retinal input and drive the tectospinal tract (crosses in the dorsal tegmental decussation, descends to cervical cord) to orient the head toward visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli. The inferior colliculi are auditory relay nuclei in the ascending auditory pathway (lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body → primary auditory cortex). The pretectal nuclei mediate the pupillary light reflex — they project bilaterally to the Edinger-Westphal nuclei, explaining the consensual light reflex.

Tegmentum

The periaqueductal gray matter (PAG) surrounds the cerebral aqueduct and is the primary site of endogenous opioid-mediated pain modulation. Opioids and stress activate the PAG, which relays via the raphe nuclei and reticulospinal tracts to inhibit pain signals at the dorsal horn — the anatomical basis of opioid analgesia.

The red nucleus in the rostral midbrain tegmentum receives input from the contralateral dentate nucleus (via superior cerebellar peduncle) and gives rise to the rubrospinal tract, which crosses in the ventral tegmental decussation. The oculomotor nucleus (CN III) lies in the rostral midbrain near the midline; the Edinger-Westphal nucleus is its parasympathetic component (pupillary constriction and lens accommodation via the ciliary ganglion). The trochlear nucleus (CN IV) is in the caudal midbrain; its fibers decussate in the superior medullary velum.

Crus cerebri fiber arrangement (medial → lateral): Corticobulbar (medial 1/5) → Corticospinal (middle 3/5) → Corticopontine (lateral 1/5). A Weber's lesion hits the crus cerebri and the CN III fascicles passing through it.

Substantia Nigra

The substantia nigra has two parts: the pars compacta contains dopaminergic neurons that project to the striatum (nigrostriatal pathway), modulating basal ganglia circuitry. The pars reticulata contains GABAergic neurons and is functionally part of the basal ganglia output (similar to the internal globus pallidus). Loss of pars compacta neurons causes Parkinson's disease.

The decussation of the superior cerebellar peduncles (brachium conjunctivum) occurs in the caudal midbrain tegmentum — after this crossing, fibers from the dentate nucleus ascend to the contralateral VA/VL thalamus. This is why cerebellar lesions cause ipsilateral ataxia (the cerebellum already accounts for the double crossing via the thalamus and descending motor pathways).

Corticobulbar (Corticonuclear) Pathway

The corticobulbar tract carries voluntary motor commands from the motor cortex to cranial nerve nuclei. It is clinically important because some nuclei receive direct (monosynaptic) cortical drive while others are indirectly innervated — this determines whether a UMN lesion causes contralateral weakness or bilateral sparing.

Direct corticobulbar innervation (contralateral UMN control): CN V motor (jaw) and CN XII (tongue) nuclei receive direct corticobulbar fibers → a unilateral cortical lesion causes contralateral jaw and tongue weakness.

Indirect/bilateral innervation (bilateral UMN control): CN III, IV, VI (extraocular) and most CN VII (forehead only) are bilaterally represented → unilateral cortical lesions spare these muscles. Exception: lower face (CN VII) is contralaterally dominant, hence spared forehead in UMN lesions.

Purely sensory nerves (I, II, VIII) have no LMN component — cannot be classified as UMN vs LMN.

Pathological Posturing

Pathological posturing is the involuntary position the limbs assume in response to noxious stimuli in a patient with severe brainstem or cortical injury. The key distinction is whether the red nucleus (and therefore the rubrospinal tract) is intact or damaged.

Posture Upper Limbs Lower Limbs Lesion Level Prognosis

Clinical Syndromes

Weber's Syndrome (Ventral Midbrain)
Lesion: Crus cerebri + CN III fascicles (posterior cerebral artery or posterior communicating artery territory).
Signs: Ipsilateral CN III palsy — ptosis, eye deviated "down and out," fixed dilated pupil (compression of parasympathetic fibers on the outside of CN III); contralateral hemiplegia (CST in crus cerebri, not yet decussated at this level).
Claude's Syndrome (Midbrain Tegmentum)
Lesion: Red nucleus + CN III fascicles.
Signs: Ipsilateral CN III palsy + contralateral ataxia and tremor (red nucleus, dentate nucleus pathway). Distinguished from Weber's by the ataxia rather than hemiplegia.
Benedikt's Syndrome (Paracentral Tegmentum)
Lesion: Red nucleus + subthalamic nucleus + CN III fascicles (paramedian perforators of basilar or interpeduncular branches of PCA).
Signs: Ipsilateral CN III palsy + contralateral hemichorea (subthalamic nucleus involvement) + contralateral ataxia and tremor (red nucleus). Distinguished from Claude's by the addition of hemichorea/hemiathetosis due to subthalamic nucleus damage.
Midbrain syndrome comparison:
Weber's = CN III + contralateral hemiplegia (crus cerebri / CST)
Claude's = CN III + contralateral ataxia (red nucleus only)
Benedikt's = CN III + contralateral hemichorea + ataxia (red nucleus + subthalamic nucleus)

5. Reticular Formation

The reticular formation is a diffuse network of neurons extending throughout the brainstem tegmentum from medulla to midbrain. It integrates sensory input with autonomic, motor, and arousal outputs. Rather than forming discrete nuclei (except for the raphe and locus ceruleus), it forms a matrix with both ascending and descending projections.

The reticulospinal tracts modulate spinal cord motor neurons: the pontine (medial) reticulospinal tract facilitates extensor tone; the medullary (lateral) reticulospinal tract facilitates flexor tone and inhibits extensors. The ascending reticular activating system (RAS) projects from the midbrain reticular formation diffusely to the thalamus and cortex, maintaining wakefulness and modulating the sleep-wake cycle. Bilateral RAS disruption causes coma.

Reticular formation functions: Respiratory and cardiovascular control (medullary RF) | Horizontal gaze (PPRF) | Sleep-wake (RAS) | Pain modulation (via raphe → PAG circuit) | Cortical arousal (ascending RAS).
Locus ceruleus (rostral pons) = norepinephrine. Raphe nuclei (midline throughout) = serotonin.

6. Cranial Nerve Nuclei

Cranial nerve nuclei are organized in columns running longitudinally through the brainstem. Understanding the functional column organization tells you which nucleus a given fiber type belongs to and, when injured, what deficits to expect.

Functional Components

The seven functional fiber types are organized from medial to lateral in the brainstem tegmentum (sulcus limitans divides efferent from afferent columns). The general somatic efferent (GSE) column is the most medial; the special somatic afferent (SSA) column is the most lateral.

SVE (branchiomotor) Efferent Trigeminal motor, facial motor, nucleus ambiguus V, VII, IX, X, XI
GVE (parasympathetic) Efferent Edinger-Westphal, superior/inferior salivatory, dorsal motor nucleus of vagus III, VII, IX, X

Afferent Nuclei in Detail

Trigeminal Sensory Nuclei

The trigeminal system has three sensory nuclei for different modalities. The mesencephalic nucleus is unique: it contains primary afferent cell bodies within the CNS (not in the trigeminal ganglion), carrying proprioceptive signals from the muscles of mastication and the periodontal ligament. It is the afferent limb of the jaw-jerk reflex. The chief (principal) sensory nucleus in the pons processes discriminative touch from the face — its second-order axons cross and ascend in the trigeminal-thalamic tract (ventral trigeminothalamic tract) to VPM of the thalamus. The spinal trigeminal nucleus processes pain and temperature from the face, extending from the pons to C2 of the spinal cord — it can be damaged by syringobulbia or herpes zoster infections.

The ascending auditory pathway crosses and re-crosses multiple times, ensuring bilateral cortical representation. No unilateral cortical lesion can cause complete deafness in one ear.

Cranial Nerve Lesions — High-Yield Patterns

The most tested cranial nerve lesion scenarios all hinge on localizing whether damage is at the nucleus, the fascicle within the brainstem, the nerve as it exits, or distally. For most purposes, nuclear and fascicular lesions produce the same ipsilateral deficit.

CN III palsy: Ptosis + eye "down and out" (unopposed SO and LR) + fixed dilated pupil (parasympathetic fibers run on the outside of CN III — compressed first by aneurysm or uncal herniation). Contrast with Horner's: ptosis + miosis (small pupil) — no eye movement deficit.
CN IV palsy: Hypertropia (affected eye elevated) and extorsion; patient tilts head to opposite side to compensate. The only CN that exits dorsally and decussates — a dorsal midbrain trauma (e.g., shearing) is the most common cause.
CN VI palsy: Ipsilateral esotropia (medial deviation); patient cannot abduct the eye. Most common false localizing sign in raised ICP — the nerve has a long intracranial course and is stretched over the petrous ridge before it even reaches the pons.
Bell's Palsy (CN VII LMN lesion)
Mechanism: Inflammation / compression of CN VII in the facial canal (often viral — HSV-1 reactivation).
Signs: Ipsilateral complete facial paralysis including the forehead (LMN). This distinguishes it from a UMN (cortical) lesion, where the forehead is SPARED because the forehead muscles receive bilateral cortical representation. May have associated hyperacusis (stapedius branch) and loss of taste (chorda tympani branch).
Ramsay Hunt Syndrome (Herpes zoster CN VII)
Mechanism: VZV reactivation in the geniculate ganglion.
Signs: Ipsilateral LMN facial paralysis + painful vesicles in the external auditory canal and auricle + ipsilateral sensorineural hearing loss ± vertigo (CN VIII involvement).
Acoustic Neuroma (Vestibular Schwannoma, CN VIII)
Site: Schwann cells at the cerebellopontine angle (usually superior vestibular nerve).
Signs: Progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing loss + tinnitus. As tumor grows: ipsilateral CN VII palsy, then CN V involvement (trigeminal pain/numbness), then cerebellar signs (compresses middle cerebellar peduncle).

7. Cerebellum

The cerebellum coordinates the timing and accuracy of movement; it does not initiate movement. Cerebellar lesions cause ipsilateral deficits because the cerebellum connects to the contralateral cortex via two decussations (superior cerebellar peduncle crosses in midbrain; CST crosses in medulla), which cancel each other out.

External Features and Lobes

The cerebellum is divided into three lobes by two key fissures: the primary fissure separates the anterior from the posterior (middle) lobe; the posterolateral fissure separates the posterior lobe from the flocculonodular lobe. The vermis runs along the midline; the cerebellar hemispheres are lateral. The superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles connect to the midbrain, pons, and medulla respectively.

Spinocerebellar Tracts

The spinocerebellar tracts carry unconscious proprioception (muscle and joint position) from the limbs to the cerebellum. They are critical for coordination of ongoing movement and represent a key clinical pathway — damage produces ipsilateral ataxia without sensory loss to consciousness.

Cortical Layers and Cell Types

The cerebellar cortex has three layers from superficial to deep. The molecular layer contains stellate and basket cells (inhibitory) and the parallel fibers (axons of granule cells running longitudinally). The Purkinje cell layer contains a single row of Purkinje cells — the sole output of the cerebellar cortex, projecting to the deep nuclei with inhibitory (GABAergic) signals. The granular layer contains granule cells (the only excitatory cells in the cortex) and Golgi cells.

Cerebellar afferents arrive as two types: mossy fibers from all sources except the inferior olive (spino-, vestibulo-, pontocerebellar pathways) excite granule cells, whose parallel fibers then excite Purkinje cells. Climbing fibers arise exclusively from the inferior olive and synapse directly on Purkinje cells in a powerful 1:1 excitatory connection (each climbing fiber contacts exactly one Purkinje cell).

Purkinje cells are the ONLY output of the cerebellar cortex → they are GABAergic and INHIBIT the deep cerebellar nuclei. Disease of the cerebellar cortex therefore disinhibits the deep nuclei. Climbing fibers come ONLY from the inferior olive — the only 1:1 excitatory connection in the cerebellar cortex.

Deep Cerebellar Nuclei and Functional Zones

The deep nuclei are the final output of the cerebellum. From medial to lateral: Fastigial, Globose, Emboliform, and Dentate. The globose and emboliform together form the interposed nucleus.

Medial → Lateral deep nuclei: "Fat Girls Eat Doughnuts" = Fastigial, Globose, Emboliform, Dentate. Dentate is the largest and most lateral; Fastigial is the most medial (and most phylogenetically old).

Clinical Signs and Lesion Patterns

Cerebellar disease produces a characteristic constellation of findings that are all ipsilateral. It is critical to distinguish the Romberg test (dorsal column/proprioception) from true cerebellar ataxia — a patient with cerebellar disease is equally unsteady with eyes open and closed (cannot use visual compensation); a patient with a positive Romberg test is unsteady with eyes CLOSED only, and can stand with eyes open.

Charcot's Triad of Cerebellar Disease: Nystagmus + Intention tremor + Scanning (staccato) dysarthria.
Cerebellar vs. Parkinson's tremor: Intention tremor (worsens approaching target) = cerebellar. Resting tremor (present at rest, suppressed by movement) = Parkinson's.

Friedreich's Ataxia (Most Common Hereditary Ataxia)
Mechanism: Autosomal recessive; GAA trinucleotide repeat expansion in the FXN gene (chromosome 9) → frataxin protein deficiency → mitochondrial iron accumulation → degeneration of spinocerebellar tracts, dorsal columns, corticospinal tract, and dorsal root ganglia.
Signs (onset <25 years): Progressive gait and limb ataxia; dysarthria; loss of vibration and proprioception (dorsal column); absent deep tendon reflexes (DRG degeneration); positive Babinski (CST damage); hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (most common cause of death); pes cavus (high-arched foot) and kyphoscoliosis. Notable: ataxia + absent reflexes + extensor plantar response = Friedreich's until proven otherwise.

8. Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia are a group of subcortical nuclei that modulate voluntary movement by filtering and gating cortical motor plans. Unlike the cerebellum (which fine-tunes ongoing movement), the basal ganglia primarily select which movements to execute and suppress unwanted ones. They do not send direct output to the spinal cord — all output goes via the thalamus back to the cortex.

Components and Anatomy

The corpus striatum is the largest component of the basal ganglia and comprises the caudate nucleus, putamen, and globus pallidus. The caudate nucleus is a C-shaped structure whose head bulges into the frontal horn of the lateral ventricle, body runs along the lateral wall of the lateral ventricle, and slender tail courses along the roof of the temporal horn — terminating at the amygdaloid nucleus. The caudate and putamen together form the striatum (neostriatum) — the primary input station, rich in GABA. The putamen and globus pallidus together form the lentiform nucleus.

The globus pallidus is divided by two medullary laminae of white matter: the external medullary lamina separates the GP from the putamen; the medial medullary lamina bisects the GP into the external (GPe) and internal (GPi) segments. GPi is the primary output nucleus. The subthalamic nucleus (STN) in the diencephalon is an excitatory relay in the indirect pathway. The nucleus accumbens (limbic striatum) connects limbic input to motor output. The substantia nigra pars compacta provides dopaminergic input to the striatum via the nigrostriatal pathway.

Direct and Indirect Pathways

Cortical motor signals enter the striatum (caudate + putamen) and travel via two competing circuits. Both ultimately modulate the GPi (and SNr), which tonically inhibits the thalamus via GABAergic projections. The thalamic target for both basal ganglia and cerebellar output is the VA/VL complex of the thalamus.

Both branches compete at the GPi → VA/VL thalamus → Cortex axis. Dopamine D1 activates direct (more movement); D2 inhibits indirect (also more movement). Dopamine facilitates movement via BOTH pathways: D1 (direct) activates the facilitatory path; D2 (indirect) inhibits the inhibitory path. Net effect: more thalamic drive to cortex → more movement.

Loss of dopamine (Parkinson's): Direct ↓ + Indirect ↑ → excessive GPi inhibition of VA/VL thalamus → hypokinesia and bradykinesia.

Clinical Disorders

Parkinson's Disease
Mechanism: Degeneration of substantia nigra pars compacta dopaminergic neurons → loss of nigrostriatal dopamine → decreased direct pathway + increased indirect pathway → excessive GPi inhibition of VA/VL thalamus → hypokinesia.
Signs (TRAP): Tremor (resting, pill-rolling, 4–6 Hz, improved by movement), Rigidity (cogwheel — passive movement meets repeated "catches"), Akinesia/bradykinesia, Postural instability. Expressionless (masked) facies, micrographia (handwriting shrinks progressively — hallmark of sustained motor control failure), shuffling gait with festination, drooling, soft monotonous speech.
Drug-induced parkinsonism: Antipsychotics (dopamine D2 blockers) and metoclopramide can block nigrostriatal dopaminergic transmission → drug-induced parkinsonism or tardive dyskinesia.
Huntington's Disease
Mechanism: CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the HTT gene (chromosome 4) → mutant huntingtin protein → preferential degeneration of striatal neurons projecting via the INDIRECT pathway (GABA/enkephalin neurons) → indirect pathway underactive → GPi disinhibited → thalamus released → hyperkinesia (chorea).
Signs: Chorea (brief, random, involuntary movements), cognitive decline, psychiatric symptoms (depression, personality change). Caudate atrophy on imaging ("box-car" lateral ventricles). Autosomal dominant, anticipation with paternal inheritance.
Hemiballismus
Mechanism: Contralateral subthalamic nucleus (STN) lesion (most commonly lacunar stroke). Loss of STN excitation of GPi → GPi underactive → thalamus disinhibited → wild, flinging movements of the contralateral proximal limbs.
Signs: Sudden onset, violent flinging movements of the arm/leg contralateral to the lesion. Unlike chorea, movements are large amplitude and proximal. Movements cease during sleep and can be physically exhausting (potentially fatal from exhaustion if untreated). Treated with phenothiazines or haloperidol.
Wilson's Disease (Hepatolenticular Degeneration)
Mechanism: Autosomal recessive defect in ATP7B (copper transporter) → copper accumulates in liver, basal ganglia (putamen > caudate), and Descemet's membrane of the cornea.
Signs: Kayser-Fleischer rings (gold-brown corneal deposits, nearly pathognomonic), liver disease (cirrhosis), neuropsychiatric symptoms, dysarthria, dyskinesia, tremor (wing-beating). Onset typically in young adults. Treatable with copper chelation.
