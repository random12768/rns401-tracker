⚡ Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)
Core Concepts & Diagnostic Criteria

ADHD is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders of childhood, characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity that interfere with functioning. Onset must occur before age 12, though the diagnosis cannot be reliably established before age 4. Symptoms must be present in ≥2 settings (e.g., school and home) to rule out situational/contextual causes.

⚠ DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Anchors

Minimum 6 symptoms of inattention AND/OR hyperactivity-impulsivity lasting ≥6 months (≥5 in adults ≥17). Symptoms must cause impairment and not be better explained by another condition (psychosis, anxiety, mood disorder).

Neurobiology: ADHD is associated with hypofunctioning of the prefrontal cortex due to reduced dopamine (DA) and norepinephrine (NE) signaling. This explains both the cognitive symptoms (working memory, attention) and why stimulants (which increase DA/NE) are effective.

🏥 Clinical Correlate

Children with ADHD have normal intelligence — school failure results from behavioral/attentional disruption, not cognitive incapacity. ADHD frequently coexists with learning disorders, oppositional defiant disorder, anxiety, and depression. ADHD commonly persists into adulthood (up to 60–70% of cases).

Treatment combines behavioral and pharmacological strategies, titrated to age and severity.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl

For children under age 6, behavioral therapy alone is the preferred first step before medications. For ages 6+, combined therapy is most effective. Stimulants are Schedule II controlled substances — monitor for growth suppression (weight/height at each visit).

🧩 Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)
Core Diagnostic Criteria (DSM-5-TR)

ASD is a lifelong neurodevelopmental disorder with onset in early childhood (symptoms recognized before age 3, though often diagnosed later). The two core domains are:

📋 DSM-5-TR Two-Domain Model

Domain A: Persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts — reduced eye contact, lack of reciprocal conversation, failure to develop peer relationships, reduced sharing of emotions.

Domain B: Restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities — stereotyped movements, insistence on sameness, highly restricted/fixated interests, hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input.

Epidemiology: Prevalence ~1 in 36 children (CDC 2023). Male:Female ratio ≈ 4:1. Associated with increased head circumference/brain size in early childhood. No causal link to vaccines (definitively refuted).

🏥 Clinical Correlate: Savant Skills

Approximately 10% of individuals with ASD display exceptional abilities in specific domains (music, mathematics, art, memory). The vignette clue of a child who "cannot speak in sentences but can name 50 dinosaurs" is a classic ASD presentation combining language deficit with restricted special interest.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl — The Rett Regression Clue

The USMLE loves the regression after normal development + hand-wringing + female sex triad. If you see this: RETT, not ASD. The MECP2 gene encodes a methyl-CpG binding protein critical for neuronal maturation. Rett is X-linked dominant — lethal in males (most 46,XY males die in utero or early infancy).

Rett Syndrome is not listed in DSM-5-TR as a separate diagnosis — it falls under ASD if criteria met, but it is a distinct genetic/neurological condition. USMLE tests it as a differential for ASD. Key: MECP2 mutation → X-linked dominant → affects females.

⚠️ Conduct Disorder (CD)
Core Concept & Diagnostic Criteria

Conduct Disorder is characterized by a repetitive and persistent pattern of behavior that violates the rights of others or societal norms. The behavior falls into four categories:

CategoryExamples
Aggression toward people/animalsBullying, fighting, using weapons, rape, cruelty
Destruction of propertyFire-setting, vandalism
Deceitfulness/theftBreaking and entering, shoplifting, lying for gain
Serious rule violationsTruancy, running away, staying out at night before age 13
⚠ Critical Distinction: CD vs. ODD

CD = Violates RIGHTS of others (cruelty, theft, destruction, aggression). ODD = Defies AUTHORITY (argumentative, vindictive, non-compliant). CD is more severe. A child with CD does not necessarily have ODD first — they can be independent diagnoses. However, ODD is a risk factor for CD.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl — Adult Trajectory

After age 18, Conduct Disorder is often reclassified as Antisocial Personality Disorder. The mnemonic: Conduct = Children, Antisocial = Adults. The question "what disorder will this child be at highest risk for in adulthood?" → always ASPD.

Treatment: Psychotherapy (CBT), family therapy, parent management training. No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy specific to CD.

😤 Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD)
Diagnostic Criteria & Clinical Profile

ODD is defined as a pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behavior, and/or vindictiveness toward authority figures lasting ≥6 months. Three symptom categories:

📋 ODD Symptom Triads

1. Angry/Irritable Mood: Loses temper easily, touchy/easily annoyed, angry/resentful
2. Argumentative/Defiant Behavior: Argues with adults, refuses to comply, deliberately annoys others, blames others
3. Vindictiveness: Spiteful or vengeful ≥2 times in past 6 months

Key differentiator from CD: ODD does NOT involve serious aggression, cruelty, theft, or property destruction. The behavior is disruptive but stays within the domain of defiance, not rights violation.

Treatment: Psychotherapy (CBT), parent management training, family therapy.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl

ODD requires ≥6 months duration. Do not diagnose before confirming chronicity. ODD can coexist with ADHD (both are common in childhood). If a question shows a child with ODD who later shows stealing/aggression → that evolution suggests Conduct Disorder has developed.

🌩️ Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD)
Core Concept & Why It Was Created

DMDD was introduced in DSM-5 specifically to reduce overdiagnosis of pediatric bipolar disorder. It captures children who have severe temper dysregulation but do NOT have distinct manic/hypomanic episodes.

📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria

• Severe, recurrent temper outbursts (verbal or behavioral) grossly disproportionate to trigger
• Outbursts occur ≥3×/week
• Mood between outbursts is persistently irritable or angry most of the day, nearly every day
• Symptoms in ≥2 settings; present for ≥12 months (no symptom-free period ≥3 months)
• Onset before age 10; diagnosis between ages 6–18
• CANNOT diagnose if ever had a manic or hypomanic episode

⚠ DMDD vs. Bipolar Disorder — The Critical Distinction

In DMDD: Mood is persistently irritable between episodes — there is no distinct "up" or "down" cycling. In Bipolar Disorder: Episodes are discrete, with clear periods of elevated/expansive mood (mania) or depression. The child with DMDD is chronically irritable, not episodically elevated.

Treatment: CBT, stimulants (for comorbid ADHD), atypical antipsychotics (for severe cases).

⭐ Step 1 Pearl

The USMLE distinction: DMDD = chronic irritability + explosive outbursts (NO cycling). Bipolar = discrete mood episodes. Remember: DMDD was invented to stop calling persistently irritable children "bipolar."

💢 Intermittent Explosive Disorder (IED)
Diagnostic Criteria & Profile

IED is characterized by recurrent behavioral outbursts representing failure to control aggressive impulses. Important features:

FeatureDetail
Age of onsetAfter age 6; most often adolescence to early adulthood
Duration of outbursts<30 minutes
Nature of outburstsVerbal aggression or physical aggression/destruction
ProportionalityGrossly disproportionate to provocation
PremeditationNOT premeditated — impulsive
Emotional sequelaeImmediate relief/release → followed by remorse and embarrassment
ConsequencesLegal, financial, occupational, social consequences common

Treatment: Psychotherapy (CBT, impulse control training), SSRIs.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl — IED vs. CD vs. ASPD

IED: Impulsive + remorseful + brief + adult context. CD: Premeditated/patterned, children. ASPD: Planned, no remorse, pervasive. The remorse/guilt after the episode is a key IED differentiator — antisocial individuals feel no remorse.

🧠 Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)
Core Criteria & Classification
📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria (all 3 required)

1. Deficits in intellectual functions (reasoning, problem-solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgment, academic learning, learning from experience) — typically IQ ≤70
2. Deficits in adaptive functioning — conceptual (language, reading, money), social (interpersonal, social judgment), or practical (self-care, job tasks, health management)
3. Onset during the developmental period

SeverityIQ RangeAdaptive Functioning
Mild~50–70Can live independently with support; 6th-grade academic level
Moderate~35–50Needs supervision; 2nd-grade level; simple jobs possible
Severe~20–35Needs close supervision; basic self-care with support
Profound<20Dependent for all activities; significant motor/communication limitations
⭐ Step 1 Pearl — Genetic Causes

Most common genetic cause: Down syndrome (Trisomy 21)
Most common inherited cause: Fragile X syndrome (trinucleotide CGG repeat expansion in FMR1 gene on X chromosome; X-linked dominant with reduced penetrance)
Fragile X: Males severely affected; females carrier or mildly affected; associated with large testes (macroorchidism), large ears, prominent jaw, autism-like features.

Treatment: Psychotherapy, occupational therapy, special education (no pharmacologic cure).

📚 Specific Learning Disorder (SLD)
Diagnostic Criteria

SLD involves difficulty learning and using academic skills despite instruction and intervention. The deficit is specific to one or more academic domains (reading, written expression, mathematics) while general intelligence and other domains are preserved.

SpecifierCommon NameHallmark
Impairment in readingDyslexiaDifficulty decoding words, slow reading, poor spelling despite normal IQ
Impairment in written expressionDysgraphiaDifficulty with spelling, grammar, punctuation, and written expression
Impairment in mathematicsDyscalculiaDifficulty processing numbers, learning arithmetic, math reasoning
⚠ SLD vs. Intellectual Disability — Critical Distinction

SLD: Normal IQ, normal adaptive functioning, specific academic deficit only. Intellectual Disability: Global cognitive deficits + impaired adaptive functioning across domains. The question "which finding is inconsistent with dyslexia?" → difficulty with personal hygiene (adaptive functioning) → that's ID, not SLD.

Treatment: Academic support, individualized education programs (IEP), counseling, extracurricular activities to build confidence.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl

Diagnosis requires ≥6 months of symptoms despite targeted intervention, onset during school-age years. IQ must be ≥70 to rule out intellectual disability. A child with "above average IQ" + struggles in only one subject → SLD.

🔄 Tourette Syndrome (TS)
Diagnostic Criteria & Tic Spectrum
📋 DSM-5-TR Criteria for Tourette's

• Both multiple motor tics AND at least one vocal tic present at some point during illness
• Tics may wax and wane but have persisted >1 year since first onset
• Onset before age 18
• Not due to substances or another medical condition

TypeSimpleComplex
Motor TicsEye blinking, grimacing, shoulder shrugging, head jerkingJumping, obscene gestures (copropraxia), touching
Vocal TicsThroat clearing, grunting, sniffing, squeakingCoprolalia (involuntary obscene speech) — only 10–15% of patients
⚠ Coprolalia ≠ Required for Diagnosis

Coprolalia (involuntary cursing) is the most well-known but least common tic — only present in ~10–15% of patients. Do not require coprolalia to diagnose Tourette's. The USMLE may test this by presenting a "clean" vignette of motor + vocal tics and asking if Tourette's is still possible → YES.

⭐ Step 1 Pearl — Comorbidities

Tourette's has extremely high comorbidity with: ADHD (~50–60%) and OCD (~40–50%). The comorbid ADHD often causes more functional impairment than the tics themselves. Treatment sequence: address ADHD first (methylphenidate), then tics if distressing.

🤫 Selective Mutism
Core Profile

Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder (not a communication disorder) characterized by consistent failure to speak in specific social situations where speaking is expected, despite speaking normally in other settings.

FeatureDetail
OnsetBefore age 5; diagnosed after ≥1 month (not just first month of school)
Classic presentationSilent at school, talkative at home
Language developmentNormal — not impaired (key distinction from language disorder)
MechanismSocial anxiety → inhibition of speech in unfamiliar/public settings
ComorbiditiesSocial anxiety disorder (highly common), separation anxiety
TreatmentBehavioral therapy, family therapy, play therapy; SSRIs (fluoxetine)
⭐ Step 1 Pearl

Selective mutism is classified under anxiety disorders in DSM-5. The child CAN speak — they CHOOSE not to (due to anxiety). Language and speech are structurally intact. Contrast with Language Disorder where the underlying speech mechanism is impaired.

👧 Separation Anxiety Disorder
Diagnostic Profile

Excessive, developmentally inappropriate fear or anxiety about separation from attachment figures. Key: can be normal behavior up to age 3–4; only pathological after that developmental window.

FeatureDetail
Duration required≥4 weeks (children); ≥6 months (adults)
Somatic complaintsHeadaches, nausea, stomachaches — often used to avoid school (school refusal)
Nighttime symptomsNightmares about separation, refusing to sleep without attachment figure nearby
WorriesFear of harm to attachment figure; fear of getting lost/kidnapped/dying
TreatmentCBT, play therapy, family therapy; SSRIs for refractory cases
🏥 Clinical Correlate — School Refusal

The child who presents with recurrent abdominal pain/headaches every Monday morning, normal physical exam, and whose symptoms resolve on weekends → classic separation anxiety with school refusal. First step: rule out organic cause, then behavioral interventions.

💔 Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD)
Core Concept & Diagnostic Criteria

RAD results from pathogenic care in early childhood — severe neglect, repeated changes in primary caregivers (orphanage, foster care), or social deprivation during the critical attachment period.

📋 Two Forms of Attachment Disorders

Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD): Child is emotionally withdrawn/inhibited; rarely seeks comfort; minimal positive affect; unexplained irritability/sadness/fear during nonthreatening interactions.

Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (DSED): Child is indiscriminately sociable with strangers; shows no hesitation approaching/following unfamiliar adults; reduced checking back with caregiver.

⚠ RAD vs. ASD — High-Yield Distinction

Both show social deficits. KEY: History of neglect/maltreatment strongly favors RAD. ASD is neurobiological and not caused by environment. If a vignette mentions "raised in an institution," "multiple foster homes," "severe neglect" → think RAD first. ASD features (repetitive behaviors, restricted interests) are absent in RAD.

Treatment: Stable, nurturing caregiving environment; child-parent psychotherapy; trauma-focused CBT.

💧 Elimination Disorders
Enuresis

Repeated voluntary or involuntary voiding of urine into bed or clothes. Requires age ≥5 years for diagnosis. Types: nocturnal (most common), diurnal, or both.

TreatmentMechanismNotes
Behavioral modificationsFluid restriction before bed, timed voidingFirst step; limited efficacy alone
Enuresis alarm (bell-and-pad)Conditioned avoidance — alarm wakes child at first urine drop1st-Line Evidence-Based — highest long-term remission
DesmopressinADH analog → ↓ urine production at nightPharmacotherapy; good for short-term/special occasions; no long-term cure
ImipramineTCA; anticholinergic + ADH-like effectLast resort — risk of cardiac toxicity, overdose danger
⭐ Step 1 Pearl — Enuresis Alarm

When behavioral modifications (fluid restriction) have failed, the next step is enuresis alarm — NOT desmopressin. The alarm has the best long-term evidence and lowest relapse rate. Desmopressin is useful short-term but has high relapse on discontinuation.

Encopresis

Repeated passage of feces into inappropriate places (clothing, floor). Age ≥4 years required. Most commonly due to constipation with overflow incontinence — stool withholding → impaction → liquid stool leaks around impaction.

Treatment: Disimpaction (enemas/laxatives), maintenance laxatives, behavioral interventions (scheduled post-meal toilet sitting), dietary fiber.

🏥 Clinical Correlate

Children with encopresis often suffer shame and social isolation. The key clinical point: rule out Hirschsprung disease (especially if congenital). Hirschsprung has a transition zone on barium enema; functional encopresis does not. Most encopresis is functional (constipation-related).

⭐ Step 1 Pearl — Language Delay Workup

For ANY language delay: First step is ALWAYS formal audiology evaluation. Hearing loss is the most common and most treatable cause of language delay. Even if the child "seems to hear" (responds to loud sounds), high-frequency hearing loss may be missed clinically.

⚠ Red Flags for ASD Screening

• No babbling by 12 months
• No pointing or waving by 12 months
• No single words by 16 months
• No 2-word phrases by 24 months
• ANY loss of previously acquired language or social skills at any age → immediate evaluation

👁️ Childhood-Onset Psychotic Disorders
Schizophrenia in Children

Childhood-onset schizophrenia (onset before age 13) is rare but represents the same disorder as adult schizophrenia. Features include hallucinations (auditory most common), delusions, disorganized thinking, and negative symptoms. Critically: distinguish from normal childhood fantasy/imaginary friends.

⚠ Distinguishing Psychosis from Normal Fantasy

Normal fantasy: Child knows the friend is imaginary, content is benign, no functional impairment, no distress. Psychosis: Child believes hallucinations/delusions are real, content is threatening, significant functional decline (dropping grades, withdrawal), persistent over months.

Treatment: Antipsychotics (atypical preferred in children — risperidone, aripiprazole), family support, school accommodations.

Stimulants first (methylphenidate, amphetamines)
Atomoxetine if stimulants contraindicated/failed
Guanfacine/Clonidine (α₂-agonists) — especially with tics
→ "SAG when stimulants fail"
Conduct Disorder → Adult Outcome
Conduct disorder = Children
Antisocial personality = Adults
→ "C → A: Children Grow into Adults (with ASPD)"
Rett Syndrome Key Features
Regression after normal development
Exclusively females (MECP2, X-linked dominant)
Twisting/wringing hands (stereotyped)
Tiny head (acquired microcephaly)
→ "RETT = Regression + Exclusively Female + Twisting + Tiny head"
Tourette Comorbidities
Tourette = Tics
ADHD (50–60% comorbid)
OCD (40–50% comorbid)
→ "TAO — Tourette-ADHD-OCD Triangle"
Intellectual Disability — Most Common Causes
Down syndrome = most common Genetic cause
Fragile X = most common Inherited cause
→ "Down = most common Genetic; Fragile X = most common Inherited"
→ Fragile X: CGG repeat in FMR1 gene + macroorchidism + large ears
