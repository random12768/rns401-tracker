1. Immunity & the Immune System

Host defense is split into an innate arm (immediate, nonspecific, germline encoded, no memory) and an adaptive arm (slower on first exposure, highly specific, with memory). Innate cells sense conserved microbial structures called PAMPs through pattern recognition receptors such as the Toll like receptors, while adaptive lymphocytes generate near limitless specificity through gene segment recombination of their antigen receptors. This foundational section also covers where immune cells originate and mature, the architecture of primary and secondary lymphoid organs, and the cell types you must recognize on an exam smear.

High-Yield Notes

Innate immunity buys time within minutes to hours; adaptive immunity arrives over days but remembers. NF-κB is the central transcription factor switched on by Toll like receptor signaling, driving cytokine production for both arms. The thymus (third pharyngeal pouch, Hassall corpuscles) matures T cells; the bone marrow matures B cells. Lymph nodes and spleen partition into a B cell zone (follicles) and a T cell zone (paracortex in nodes, PALS in spleen).

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Three professional APCs are dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells; only the dendritic cell can prime a truly naive T cell.
  • The paracortex (nodes) and PALS (spleen) are T cell zones, so both shrink when T cell development fails, as in DiGeorge syndrome.
  • A nonfunctional spleen removes the main filter for blood borne encapsulated organisms, which is why those patients need vaccination and prophylaxis.
  • Do not equate NK cells with cytotoxic T cells: NK killing needs no antigen specific receptor and is part of innate immunity.

2. Complement System & Cytokines

The complement system is a set of more than twenty plasma proteins, numbered C1 through C9, that circulate as inactive zymogens and, once triggered, form a self amplifying proteolytic cascade. Three pathways feed into it, and despite different starting signals all converge on the cleavage of C3 and then C5, generating fragments that opsonize microbes, recruit and activate leukocytes, and assemble the lytic membrane attack complex. Cytokines are the soluble messenger proteins that coordinate these and the wider immune responses, and a handful of them carry most of the exam weight.

High-Yield Notes

Each pathway has a distinct trigger but a shared destination. The cleavage products do the work: C3b is the principal opsonin recognized by CR1 on phagocytes, its further cleavage product C3d engages CR2 (CD21) to boost B cell responses, and the small fragments C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins that make mast cells release histamine. C5a is also the strongest chemoattractant of the cascade. The terminal components C5b through C9 build the MAC, whose C9 ring resembles perforin and lyses the target. Regulation matters as much as activation: C1 inhibitor restrains the classical pathway and kallikrein, Factor H and Factor I dismantle the alternative convertase, properdin stabilizes it, and the GPI anchored proteins DAF (CD55) and CD59 protect host cells from accidental lysis.

Cytokines are the soluble messengers that coordinate every step above and the wider adaptive response. Memorize source, then function.

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Do not confuse the two anaphylatoxins: both C3a and C5a release histamine, but only C5a is a dominant chemoattractant.

  • Properdin is the only positive regulator of the alternative pathway; Factor H and Factor I are its brakes, so their loss looks opposite to a properdin defect.
  • TNF and the IL-1, IL-6 group are the acute inflammation cytokines, while IL-12 with IFN gamma drives the cell mediated axis; do not blur the two roles.
  • IL-2 vs IL-15: both use the common γ-chain receptor and expand NK and T cells, but IL-2 (from T cells) also drives Tregs and activation-induced cell death (tolerance), whereas IL-15 (from macrophages/dendritic cells) preferentially sustains NK cells and CD8+ memory T cells.

3. Acute Inflammatory Response

Acute inflammation is the body's immediate, innate, memoryless response to injury or invasion, aimed chiefly at extracellular bacteria, fungi, and viruses. It unfolds as vascular changes that produce the cardinal signs followed by an ordered recruitment of leukocytes from blood into tissue. A defined set of mediators drives each step, and the response can end in several different outcomes depending on how much tissue is damaged.

High-Yield Notes

After a brief reflex vasoconstriction, vasodilation raises local blood flow while increased vascular permeability lets a protein rich exudate leak out, together producing the five cardinal signs. Leukocytes then follow an invariant sequence: margination, rolling, firm adhesion, and transmigration, after which they crawl up a chemotactic gradient toward the focus of injury. The rolling step is low affinity and reversible; the arrest step is firm and is the one lost in leukocyte adhesion deficiency. Mediators split into preformed amines released within minutes, lipid mediators built on demand from arachidonic acid, and cytokines that act both locally and systemically. The chemoattractants that steer phagocytes are a short, testable list, and the eventual outcome ranges from complete restoration to scarring or chronicity.

4. Antigen, Hapten, Superantigen & Antibody Structure

An antigen is any molecule the immune system recognizes; only those that actually provoke a response are immunogens. Small molecules called haptens bind antigen receptors but cannot trigger a response until coupled to a carrier protein. This section also covers superantigens, which short circuit normal T cell activation, and the four chain antibody molecule whose Fab arms bind antigen while the Fc tail dictates isotype and effector function.

High-Yield Notes

Immunogenicity rises with size (proteins greater than 10 kDa), structural complexity, and the number of accessible epitopes; an epitope is the smallest part of an antigen seen by one receptor, and multivalent antigens make the best immunogens. Proteins are the strongest immunogens because phagocytes can enzymatically cleave them, whereas carbohydrates, lipids, and steroids are weak, and free amino acids or haptens alone are not immunogenic. A hapten such as penicillin or the catechol of poison ivy becomes immunogenic only after binding a carrier protein, the basis of penicillin allergy and contact dermatitis. An adjuvant is added to a vaccine to amplify the response without contributing epitopes of its own.

Superantigens (staphylococcal TSST-1, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins A to C) bypass normal processing: they bridge MHC class II on the APC directly to the Vβ region of the TCR outside the peptide groove. This crosslinks a huge fraction of T cells regardless of specificity, driving polyclonal activation, a flood of IL-2 and IFN-γ from T cells and IL-1 and TNF-α from macrophages, and the shock, fever, rash, and capillary leak of toxic shock syndrome.

The antibody monomer is two identical heavy and two identical light chains in a Y, joined by disulfide bonds. Each chain has a variable and a constant region; one heavy plus one light variable domain forms an antigen binding site, so every monomer has two identical sites. The Fab arms bind antigen; the Fc tail sets the isotype and mediates complement fixation, opsonization, and cellular binding. The flexible hinge lets the two Fab arms swing to grasp epitopes at varying distances.

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Class switching changes the Fc and therefore function, but the idiotype and antigen specificity stay fixed; it is irreversible.
  • A single antibody carries either kappa or lambda light chains, never both; free kappa chains in urine are Bence Jones proteins of multiple myeloma.

  • IgG, not IgM, crosses the placenta; finding elevated IgM in a newborn means the fetus itself responded to a congenital infection.

5. MHC & Antigen Processing/Presentation

T cells never see free antigen; they recognize short peptides displayed in the groove of a major histocompatibility complex molecule, encoded by the HLA genes on chromosome 6. Two pathways split the work: MHC class I samples the cytosol and reports to CD8 cells, while MHC class II samples the extracellular space and reports to CD4 cells. This section maps each pathway end to end and ties the HLA loci to their classic disease associations.

High-Yield Notes

Class I (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) is an alpha heavy chain paired with beta 2 microglobulin, a light chain encoded outside the MHC but mandatory for surface expression; lose it and class I disappears. Class I sits on all nucleated cells and platelets but not mature red cells, and its short 8 to 9 residue groove displays endogenous cytosolic and viral peptides to CD8 cells. Those peptides are chopped by the proteasome and pumped into the ER by TAP; a TAP failure blocks loading and causes bare lymphocyte syndrome type 1 (low CD8, normal CD4). Class II (HLA-DP, DQ, DR) is two similar chains, expressed only on professional APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells). It is built with an invariant chain blocking its groove; in acidic endosomes the invariant chain is degraded to CLIP and exchanged for an exogenous peptide. A class II transcription defect gives bare lymphocyte syndrome type 2 (low CD4, normal CD8). The rule of 8 keeps pairing straight: MHC 1 times CD8 and MHC 2 times CD4 both equal 8. HLA alleles are codominant and highly polymorphic; each person carries two haplotypes, so a sibling has a 25 percent chance of a full match.

6. Antigen Receptors & Receptor Diversity

B and T cells recognize antigen through surface receptors, but they neither sense the same form of antigen nor signal the same way. The B cell receptor is membrane IgM and IgD that binds intact antigen of almost any composition, while the T cell receptor sees only processed peptide. This section covers their structure and signaling chains and the genetic gymnastics that let a finite genome generate a near limitless repertoire.

High-Yield Notes

The BCR signals through the invariant pair Igα and Igβ, flanked by a coreceptor complex of CD19, CD21, and CD81; CD21 doubles as the receptor for Epstein-Barr virus. The TCR is an αβ heterodimer (about 95 percent of T cells; the rest are γδ) whose signal is transmitted by the associated CD3 complex and ζ chains through tyrosine kinases, with CD4 or CD8 as coreceptors; a cell lacking CD3 can present antigen but cannot be activated. Receptor diversity is generated before any antigen is met, by three antigen independent mechanisms: random gene segment recombination of V, D, and J for the heavy chain and V and J for the light chain, random pairing of heavy with light chains, and random nucleotide addition at the junctions by TdT. The recombinases RAG1 and RAG2 cut the DNA; loss of activity gives SCID (no activity) or Omenn syndrome (partial). TdT inserts template independent N nucleotides at heavy chain and all TCR junctions, the single largest source of junctional diversity, but switches off during light chain rearrangement. For B cells, a fourth process, somatic hypermutation, sharpens affinity after antigen encounter in the germinal center. Allelic exclusion ensures that once one allele rearranges successfully the other is silenced, so each lymphocyte expresses a single specificity.

7. Humoral Immune Response

The humoral arm controls freely circulating pathogens through antibody secreted by plasma cells. Naive B cells are activated by antigen and, depending on the antigen type, either respond alone or recruit T follicular helper cells. The most powerful responses run through the germinal center, where B cells refine their antibodies and lay down memory. This section tracks B cell activation, the germinal center reaction, and the kinetic difference between a first and a repeat exposure.

High-Yield Notes

T independent antigens are large multivalent polysaccharides or lipids that cross link many B cell receptors and are handled by marginal zone and B-1 cells. They cannot be presented to T cells, so they yield only IgM, no class switching, and no memory, which is why an unconjugated polysaccharide vaccine such as PPSV23 is weak and needs boosters. T dependent antigens contain a protein component: the B cell internalizes antigen through its receptor, presents peptide on MHC class II, and recruits a T follicular helper cell. In the germinal center the clones receiving the most CD40L and cytokines proliferate, undergo somatic hypermutation with affinity maturation in the variable region, and undergo isotype switching in the constant region. Switching is irreversible because intervening DNA is excised; cytokines steer the destination (IL-4 to IgE, IL-5 to IgA). Activated cells emerge in two waves: an early short lived IgM plasma cell wave that exits the tissue, and a germinal center wave yielding high affinity class switched plasma cells and long lived memory B cells. This is why the primary response is slow and IgM dominant, while the secondary response is fast, higher in titer, and IgG dominant. Failure of CD40 to CD40L signaling produces hyper IgM syndrome: only IgM is made and patients suffer recurrent sinopulmonary and GI infections.

8. Cell-Mediated Immune Response

Cell-mediated immunity is the T lymphocyte arm that defends against microbes living inside host cells, where antibody cannot reach. Naive T cells are primed in the paracortex of lymph nodes by mature dendritic cells, then clonally expand and differentiate into CD4 helper effectors that orchestrate other cells through cytokines and CD8 cytotoxic effectors that directly kill infected and tumor cells. Activation is deliberately gated by a two-signal requirement so that T cells respond to pathogens but not to self. This section also covers the CD4 helper subsets and their signature cytokines, the killing machinery of cytotoxic lymphocytes and NK cells, ADCC, and how viruses evade these defenses.

High-Yield Notes

Naive T cells need two signals to activate. Signal 1 is the TCR recognizing peptide on MHC, stabilized by the CD4 or CD8 coreceptor and transduced by CD3; signal 2 is costimulation, CD28 on the T cell binding B7 (CD80/CD86) on the APC. Signal 1 without signal 2 produces anergy, functional unresponsiveness that enforces self-tolerance. Activated T cells secrete IL-2 and express the high-affinity IL-2 receptor, driving autocrine clonal proliferation. Activation physically occurs in secondary lymphoid organs, with adhesion molecules (LFA-1 binding ICAM-1, CD2 binding CD58) holding the conjugate together. The inhibitory receptors CTLA-4 and PD-1 are induced later to terminate responses; both also restrain anti-tumor immunity, making them checkpoint-blockade targets.

The CD4 subset chosen depends on the innate cytokine milieu, set by the pathogen. IL-12 from macrophages and IFN-gamma from NK cells push Th1 (T-bet); IL-4 pushes Th2 (GATA3); IL-6, IL-23, and TGF-beta push Th17 (RORgammaT). Th1 IFN-gamma activates macrophages (M1) to kill facultative intracellular organisms; Th17 IL-17 recruits neutrophils against extracellular bacteria and fungi; Th2 drives IgE and eosinophils against helminths; Treg (FoxP3) secrete IL-10 and TGF-beta to maintain tolerance. CD8 CTLs kill by two routes: perforin/granzyme exocytosis and FasL-Fas ligation, both converging on caspase-driven apoptosis.

  • IFN-gamma that initiates Th1 differentiation comes from NK cells, not from the T cell itself; the T cell amplifies it later.
  • The three cells bearing Fc-epsilon receptors for IgE (mast cell, basophil, eosinophil) drive type I hypersensitivity; eosinophils also perform IgE-mediated ADCC against helminths.

9. Immunological Memory & Tolerance

Two properties make adaptive immunity both powerful and safe: memory, which lets the system respond faster and harder on re-exposure, and tolerance, which prevents it from attacking self. Memory T and B cells generated during a first encounter underlie the accelerated, higher affinity secondary response that vaccine boosters exploit. Tolerance is built first in the generative organs (central tolerance) and reinforced in the periphery (peripheral tolerance); when these fail, autoimmunity results, which sets up the hypersensitivity and autoimmune disease sections that follow.

High-Yield Notes

On first exposure, the primary response is slow (days to weeks), low in magnitude, and IgM dominant before any class switching. Re-exposure triggers the secondary response: faster onset, higher titers, predominantly IgG, with much higher affinity from somatic hypermutation. This depends on long-lived memory B cells (roughly a decade) and memory T cells (often decades), and is the molecular rationale for booster vaccination. Note class switching to IgG requires T cell help, so it occurs only for protein antigens.

Central tolerance happens during development. In the thymus, T cells undergo positive selection in the cortex (only cells that bind self-MHC survive, enforcing MHC restriction) and negative selection in the medulla (cells that bind self-peptide too avidly are deleted, enforcing self-tolerance). The transcription factor AIRE forces thymic display of tissue-restricted self antigens so autoreactive clones are purged; its mutation causes autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome. B cells achieve central tolerance in the bone marrow by clonal deletion or receptor editing. Cells escaping central tolerance are caught peripherally by anergy (signal 1 without costimulation), Treg suppression (FoxP3, IL-10, TGF-beta), and clonal deletion via Fas.

10. Immunoassays & Serological Tests

Serological tests exploit the exquisite specificity of antibody for antigen to detect an unknown antigen, detect an unknown antibody, or quantify how much antibody is present (the titer). The exam tests interpretation far more than technique: you must read a positive versus negative result, decide whether a test belongs in screening or confirmation, and reason from sensitivity and specificity. The recurring clinical template is the two step diagnostic pairing, a highly sensitive screen followed by a highly specific confirmatory test, as in the HIV ELISA then Western blot algorithm.

High-Yield Notes

Sensitivity is the ability to correctly identify those who have disease, so a highly sensitive test has few false negatives and is ideal for screening; a negative result on a sensitive test reliably rules disease out. Specificity is the ability to correctly identify those without disease, so a highly specific test has few false positives and is ideal for confirmation; a positive result on a specific test reliably rules disease in. The classic algorithm screens with the sensitive ELISA (high sensitivity) and confirms a positive with the more specific Western blot. ELISA uses a horseradish peroxidase labeled antibody to produce a color change; in the indirect format the secondary antibody must be raised in a species different from the primary, so a human primary antibody requires an anti human secondary. ELISA comes in three formats: the direct ELISA detects antigen with a single enzyme-linked antibody, the indirect ELISA detects patient antibody using an enzyme-linked anti-immunoglobulin secondary (the HIV screen), and the sandwich (capture) ELISA traps antigen between a coating capture antibody and a labeled detection antibody (used to quantify antigens and cytokines).

A titer is the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution still giving a positive reaction, and following a titer across acute and convalescent samples tracks disease progression: a rising or fourfold IgG titer signals an active or recent response. Seroconversion is going from no detectable antibody to a significant titer during illness, classically in HIV. A counterintuitive trap is the prozone phenomenon: excess antibody prevents lattice formation and yields a false negative precipitation test (for example VDRL for syphilis), which is corrected by diluting the serum.

11. Immunization

Immunization is one of the most heavily tested topics in this block, so master the logic rather than memorizing lists. The first decision is active versus passive immunity: active means the host makes its own antibody and memory after exposure to an antigen, giving slow but long lasting protection, while passive means preformed antibody is given for immediate but short lived protection. The second axis is the vaccine type, because each type drives a characteristic kind of immunity, has its own safety profile, and pairs with classic disease examples that the exam returns to again and again.

High-Yield Notes

Active immunity is acquired naturally through infection or artificially through vaccination, and its mediators are both antibody and T cells, so memory makes protection last for years. Passive immunity is acquired naturally as maternal IgG across the placenta and IgA in breast milk, or artificially as antitoxin, antivenin, immunoglobulin, or monoclonal antibodies; its mediator is antibody only, so it acts fast but fades. Several exposures demand both arms at once: rabies (vaccine plus rabies immunoglobulin), hepatitis B (vaccine plus HBIG, as in a needlestick or a newborn of an HBsAg positive mother), and tetanus in a patient with unknown immunization status (toxoid plus antitoxin). The passive antibody works immediately while the vaccine builds lasting immunity.

Vaccine type predicts the response. Live attenuated vaccines replicate, drive strong cellular plus humoral lifelong immunity, but can revert to virulence and are contraindicated in pregnancy and immunocompromise (MMR, varicella, oral Sabin polio, intranasal influenza, yellow fever, rotavirus). Killed, subunit, toxoid, and polysaccharide vaccines are safe in those patients but give a mostly humoral, weaker response needing boosters. Conjugate vaccines link a capsular polysaccharide to a protein carrier, converting a T independent (IgM only) response into a T dependent one with class switching and memory, which is why infants under two can respond to encapsulated organisms (Hib, PCV13, MCV4); unconjugated PPSV23 is the T independent adult counterpart.

12. Transplantation & Transfusion Immunology

Graft survival turns on how closely the donor and recipient MHC (HLA) proteins match, because HLA molecules are alloantigens that differ between members of the same species and are read as foreign by recipient T cells. The host recognizes graft alloantigens by two routes: a direct pathway, in which recipient T cells see intact donor MHC on donor APCs within the graft, and an indirect pathway, in which recipient APCs process donor antigen and present it to CD4 T cells. Rejection is then sorted by timing and mechanism into hyperacute, acute, and chronic, with graft versus host disease as the mirror image when grafted donor T cells attack the host. Transfusion immunology applies the same alloantigen logic to red cell ABO and Rh groups and to the reactions that follow a mismatch.

High-Yield Notes

Match the rejection to its timing and effector: minutes means preformed antibody (hyperacute), weeks means a primed T cell response (acute), and months to years of vascular narrowing means graft arteriosclerosis (chronic). Preformed anti-HLA antibody comes from prior transfusion, pregnancy, or a previous transplant, which is why a pretransplant crossmatch (recipient serum mixed with donor cells) is mandatory. Three loci predict success most strongly: HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-DR (a six of six match). The microcytotoxicity test types class I HLA serologically, while the mixed lymphocyte reaction tests class II: high tritiated thymidine uptake by recipient responder cells means the donor class II is foreign and the graft is likely to be rejected.

13. Immunodeficiency Diseases & HIV/AIDS

Immunodeficiencies are split into primary (inherited, usually present early in life and most often X linked, so boys predominate) and secondary (acquired, from HIV, chemotherapy, steroids, or malnutrition). The single most powerful exam strategy is pattern recognition by the arm that has failed: encapsulated pyogenic bacteria point to a B cell or antibody defect, viral, fungal, and protozoal organisms point to a T cell defect, severe early life infection with failure to thrive points to a combined defect, recurrent catalase positive abscesses with poor wound healing point to a phagocyte defect, and recurrent Neisseria points to a terminal complement defect. Map age, the organism, and the immunoglobulin pattern onto one of the disorders below.

High-Yield Notes

B cell defects surface only after about 6 months, once maternal IgG wanes, and produce sinopulmonary infections plus Giardia. T cell defects show up earlier with intracellular and fungal organisms. Phagocyte defects all share recurrent Staphylococcus aureus and fungal infection but separate cleanly on three bedside tests: the NBT / DHR respiratory burst assay (negative only when NADPH oxidase is gone), the presence of pus (absent in LAD), and the smear (giant granules in Chediak-Higashi). The catalase logic for chronic granulomatous disease is the recurring trap: catalase positive organisms destroy their own H2O2, so a phagocyte with no NADPH oxidase has no peroxide left to feed myeloperoxidase and cannot kill them. Complement defects are cross listed here because terminal MAC loss mimics a humoral pattern but is actually a lysis problem.

14. Hypersensitivity Reactions

A hypersensitivity reaction is an exaggerated or misdirected immune response that injures host tissue. The Gell and Coombs scheme sorts these reactions into four types by the dominant effector mechanism. Type I is immediate and IgE driven; Type II is antibody directed against a fixed cell surface or matrix antigen; Type III is mediated by circulating antigen antibody complexes that deposit in vessels; and Type IV is delayed and entirely T cell mediated with no antibody at all. The single most testable idea is matching a disease to its type by reading the timing, the mediator, and whether antibody or T cells do the damage. This section covers the four types and the classic disease examples used to illustrate each one.

High-Yield Notes

Types I through III are all antibody mediated (B cell limb); only Type IV is purely T cell mediated. Type I uses IgE on mast cells and runs in minutes, with a late phase at 2 to 24 hours driven by eosinophils; the sensitizing cytokines are Th2 derived IL-4 and IL-13 (IgE class switching) and IL-5 (eosinophils). Type II antibody (IgG or IgM) binds a fixed antigen and kills by three routes: complement and ADCC lysis, opsonized phagocytosis, or receptor modulation that stimulates (Graves) or blocks (myasthenia) function. Type III complexes form in antigen excess, lodge in vessel walls and glomeruli, fix complement, and recruit neutrophils via C5a, classically 7 to 10 days after exposure with low serum C3 and C4. Type IV needs no antibody: sensitized CD4 Th1 cells (releasing IFN gamma) and CD8 cytotoxic cells produce induration at 48 to 72 hours.

  • Type IV has no antibody and no complement: PPD, contact dermatitis, T1DM, and MS are driven by T cells, so a positive Coombs or low C3 argues against it.
  • Hyperacute rejection (minutes, preformed antibody) is Type II; chronic rejection (months to years, T cell vascular injury) is Type IV; do not merge the two.
  • One drug, all four types: penicillin is the classic agent that can trigger every Gell-Coombs reaction — anaphylaxis (I), immune hemolytic anemia (II), serum sickness (III), and contact dermatitis / interstitial nephritis (IV) — so the reaction type is read from the timing and effector, not from the drug.

15. Autoimmune Diseases

Autoimmunity is the failure of self-tolerance, the normal state in which the immune system ignores its own tissues. Tolerance is built in two stages. Central tolerance deletes strongly self-reactive T cells in the thymus and self-reactive B cells in the bone marrow; in the thymus the AIRE protein forces medullary epithelium to display peripheral tissue antigens so that thymocytes recognizing them are clonally deleted. Peripheral tolerance mops up cells that escape, using anergy (antigen seen without costimulation, with CTLA-4 opposing the B7 signal), Fas and Fas ligand activation-induced apoptosis, and suppression by regulatory T cells (CD4+, CD25+, FOXP3+, secreting IL-10 and TGF-β). When tolerance breaks, several forces combine: inherited HLA susceptibility alleles, environmental triggers (infection, drugs, ultraviolet light, and release of sequestered antigen from immunologically privileged sites such as the eye and testis), and molecular mimicry, in which antibody raised against a microbe cross-reacts with self. Each disease below is defined for the exam by a hallmark autoantibody, the tolerance-failure mechanism behind it, and a classic clinical clue.

High-Yield Notes

The most testable distinction is systemic versus organ-specific autoimmunity, which tracks with the underlying hypersensitivity mechanism. Systemic connective-tissue diseases are driven mainly by deposited immune complexes (type III), whereas organ-specific diseases are usually antibody directed at a receptor or cell-surface target (type II). ANA is the sensitive but non-specific screen for the connective-tissue group; the specific antibody then names the disease.
