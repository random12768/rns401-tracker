Ky's FBS2 Pharmacology Study Guide

This guide is built around active recall, not passive review. The old study cycle, reading a textbook-style chapter end to end and only then testing yourself, feels productive but is weak for long-term retention: most of the effort goes into recognizing material rather than retrieving it. This guide flips that. Each section is a UWorld-style question bank that forces you to commit to an answer first, then learn from the full explanation, which is where the real encoding happens.

How to use it well: keep your lecture slides open as the primary teaching source. For each section, work one question at a time, read the vignette carefully, and choose an answer before revealing anything, even when you are unsure: a wrong guess you then correct is remembered far better than a fact you simply read. After submitting, study the whole answer panel, not just the green header: confirm why the correct option is right, read why each distractor is wrong, absorb the teaching block, and use the slide reference to flip back to the deck for anything that did not land. Treat a missed question as a signal to revisit that slide, then return later and attempt it again. Work through every section; the goal is to be able to retrieve, explain, and apply each concept, not just recognize it.

1. Introduction to Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the science of how chemicals interact with living systems. This section orients you to the vocabulary you will use all year: the branches of pharmacology, the difference between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, drug nomenclature, prescription versus over-the-counter status, orphan drugs, the natural and synthetic sources of drugs, and the routes by which drugs enter the body. Master the route comparisons and the first-pass concept, since they recur in every later topic.

High-Yield Notes

Two phrases anchor the whole discipline: pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion); pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body (receptor binding, mechanism, effect). Every other "pharmaco-" branch is a specialized lens layered on top of these two.

Routes of administration trade off speed, bioavailability, and patient compliance. The first-pass effect is the central concept: oral drugs traverse intestinal wall and portal liver before reaching systemic circulation, so a substantial fraction can be metabolized before arrival. Routes that bypass the portal system (IV, IM, SC, SL, rectal lower-half, transdermal, inhaled) avoid first-pass.

2. Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. This section drills the membrane-transport mechanisms, the factors that govern absorption and bioavailability, the meaning of the apparent volume of distribution and plasma protein binding, the CYP450 system with enzyme induction and inhibition, the renal handling of drugs, and the kinetics that determine half-life, steady state, and clearance.

High-Yield Notes

The four-letter ADME framework runs the whole section. Absorption is the fraction of dose that reaches systemic circulation (bioavailability, F): IV F = 100% by definition; PO F is variable and reduced by first-pass metabolism, food, gastric pH, and gut transit. Distribution is described by the apparent volume of distribution (Vd): low Vd (3 to 5 L) means the drug stays in plasma (heparin, warfarin); intermediate (12 to 16 L) suggests extracellular distribution (gentamicin); high Vd (>42 L) means tissue sequestration (digoxin, lipophilic agents). Metabolism happens predominantly in the liver (Phase I oxidation, then Phase II conjugation). Excretion is mostly renal, with bile and feces important for some drugs.

Plasma protein binding matters because only the free fraction is pharmacologically active. Albumin binds acidic drugs (warfarin, phenytoin, salicylates, NSAIDs); α1-acid glycoprotein binds basic drugs (lidocaine, propranolol, quinidine). Drug interactions from displacement are clinically important for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (warfarin displaced by phenylbutazone or sulfonamides). Hypoalbuminemia (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) raises free fraction.

Henderson-Hasselbalch and ion trapping: weak acids (aspirin, phenobarbital) are non-ionized and absorbed in the acidic stomach; alkalinizing the urine (sodium bicarbonate) ionizes them in the tubule, trapping and accelerating excretion. Weak bases (amphetamine, TCAs) are trapped by urinary acidification (rarely used now because of acidosis risks).

Steady state and half-life: after a constant-rate infusion or repeated dosing, 4 to 5 half-lives are required to reach steady state and to eliminate a drug. Loading dose = Vd × target concentration (used when rapid effect needed). Maintenance dose = clearance × target concentration (matches elimination at steady state). Renal or hepatic failure reduces clearance and prolongs half-life: adjust the maintenance dose, not the loading dose.

3. Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body: the mechanisms by which drugs produce their effects. This section covers receptors and the agonist, antagonist, partial agonist, and inverse agonist classes; the four receptor superfamilies and their signaling; the graded and quantal dose-response curves and what their position, height, and slope reveal about potency and efficacy; the therapeutic index and therapeutic window; and the synergistic and antagonistic interactions of combined drugs.

High-Yield Notes

Almost every drug acts at a receptor; four superfamilies cover the vast majority. Memorize the timescale and signaling output of each because the receptor type predicts onset and second-messenger consequences.

Potency vs efficacy is the most commonly confused pair. Potency reflects the dose needed (EC₅₀); a left-shifted curve = more potent (smaller dose gives same effect). Efficacy reflects the maximum effect achievable (Emax); a taller plateau = greater efficacy. Morphine has greater efficacy than codeine. Loop diuretics have greater efficacy than thiazides (higher peak natriuresis) regardless of potency.

4. Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction is any noxious, unintended effect occurring at doses normally used in humans. This section covers the severity grades and the Type A to F classification, the distinction between dose-related augmented reactions and bizarre immunologic or idiosyncratic reactions, the four hypersensitivity types, photosensitivity, drug dependence and withdrawal, teratogenicity and the classic teratogens, and the pharmacovigilance system that detects and reports these reactions.

High-Yield Notes

The most testable dichotomy is Type A vs Type B. Type A (Augmented) reactions are dose-related, predictable extensions of pharmacology, common, and reversible by dose reduction (warfarin bleeding, β-blocker bradycardia, aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity). Type B (Bizarre) reactions are dose-independent, idiosyncratic or immunologic, uncommon but often severe, and require drug withdrawal (penicillin anaphylaxis, halothane hepatitis, malignant hyperthermia, Stevens-Johnson). The full A through F system extends this for chronic and end-of-treatment effects.

5. Cholinergic Pharmacology

The autonomic nervous system has two arms. The parasympathetic (PANS) branch is craniosacral (CN III, VII, IX, X and S2–S4), uses long preganglionic and short postganglionic fibers, and drives the rest-and-digest functions. The sympathetic (SANS) branch is thoracolumbar (T1–L3), uses short preganglionic and long postganglionic fibers, and energizes fight-or-flight. Acetylcholine (ACh) is the transmitter at every autonomic ganglion (nicotinic Nn), at the adrenal medulla (Nn), at the skeletal neuromuscular junction (nicotinic Nm), at all parasympathetic postganglionic terminals (muscarinic), and at sympathetic postganglionic fibers innervating sweat glands. Master the receptor map before any drug name; every cholinergic and anticholinergic agent is a switch on this circuit, and exam questions almost always hinge on the receptor subtype hit, not the drug name itself.

High-Yield Notes

Two rules unlock most cholinergic pharmacology questions: odd muscarinic receptors (M1, M3, M5) are Gq (IP₃/DAG → ↑Ca²⁺ → smooth-muscle contraction, glandular secretion); even muscarinic receptors (M2, M4) are Gi (↓cAMP → slows the heart). Nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels, not G-protein coupled; they depolarize. Tonic parasympathetic predominates at the SA node, GI tract, bladder detrusor, and ciliary muscle, which is why anticholinergic drugs release these from PANS control (tachycardia, ileus, urinary retention, cycloplegia).

Organophosphate poisoning (parathion, malathion, sarin, VX, echothiophate): irreversibly phosphorylates AChE → massive ACh accumulation at all cholinergic synapses. Memorize the DUMBBELSS toxidrome: Diarrhea, Urination, Miosis, Bronchospasm/Bradycardia, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation, Sweating. Nicotinic skeletal effects add fasciculations and paralysis. Time-to-pralidoxime is the single most testable detail.

Depolarizing Neuromuscular Blockade: Succinylcholine

Succinylcholine is the only depolarizing NM agonist in clinical use. Mechanism: two ACh molecules fused tail-to-tail bind NM, open the channel, depolarize the end plate (brief fasciculations), then produce a Phase I block; sustained depolarization with flaccid paralysis that AChE inhibitors actually worsen. With prolonged exposure, channels desensitize into a Phase II block which then resembles a competitive (nondepolarizing) block and can be reversed with neostigmine. Three boxed catastrophes; every one tested: malignant hyperthermia (autosomal-dominant RYR1 mutation; same trigger as halogenated volatile anesthetics → dantrolene), hyperkalemia in burns, crush, denervation, or Guillain-Barré (avoid SCh entirely), and prolonged apnea in pseudocholinesterase deficiency or with concurrent AChE-i therapy (donepezil, neostigmine).

6. Anticholinergic Pharmacology

Anticholinergic pharmacology is the mirror image of section 5; every receptor on the cholinergic map can be silenced. Antimuscarinic agents block M1–M5 (the dominant tone on heart, glands, smooth muscle, and CNS) producing a predictable toxidrome: tachycardia, dry mucosa, mydriasis, bronchodilation, urinary retention, constipation, sedation or delirium. Ganglionic blockers hit NN at all autonomic ganglia (largely historical). Nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers competitively antagonize NM at the skeletal motor end plate. Drug choice in each scenario is driven by which organ you want to hit and which CNS or systemic effect you can tolerate; route (inhaled, topical, transdermal) and lipid solubility (quaternary vs tertiary) determine selectivity more than receptor subtype.

High-Yield Notes

Muscarinic antagonists share the toxidrome "blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone, full as a flask, the heart runs alone"; mydriasis & cycloplegia, delirium, flushed skin, hyperthermia (no sweating), dry mucosa, urinary retention, and tachycardia. The drug you pick is dictated by which organ you want to spare or hit; route determines selectivity (inhaled ipratropium stays in the lung; topical tropicamide stays in the eye).

Nondepolarizing Neuromuscular Blockade

Nondepolarizing blockers (rocuronium, vecuronium, cisatracurium, atracurium, tubocurarine, pancuronium) are competitive NM antagonists; paralysis with no fasciculations and no MH/hyperkalemia risk. Reverse with neostigmine + glycopyrrolate (boosts ACh; glycopyrrolate blocks the muscarinic side effects), or with sugammadex for rocuronium/vecuronium (encapsulates the drug; fastest, cleanest reversal). Two named adverse profiles: tubocurarine → histamine-mediated hypotension; pancuronium → vagolytic tachycardia. Atracurium and cisatracurium undergo Hofmann elimination (independent of liver/kidney); preferred in organ failure.

Ganglionic Blockers

Hexamethonium and mecamylamine block NN at autonomic ganglia, paralyzing both PANS and SANS. Clinically obsolete but tested for the experimental concept: knocking out the dominant tone at each organ. At the heart (vagal tone dominates) → tachycardia. At arterioles (sympathetic tone dominates) → vasodilation and orthostatic hypotension. At the gut and bladder → ileus and urinary retention. At sweat glands → anhidrosis.

7. Adrenergic Pharmacology

The adrenergic system runs on three endogenous catecholamines; norepinephrine (NE) at postganglionic sympathetic nerve terminals, epinephrine from adrenal medulla (80%) plus NE (20%), and dopamine as a CNS transmitter and a renal/mesenteric vasodilator. Synthesis runs tyrosine → DOPA → dopamine → NE → epinephrine, with tyrosine hydroxylase as the rate-limiting step. NE is taken back up by NET (cocaine and TCAs block this), packaged into vesicles by VMAT (reserpine blocks this), and degraded by MAO (intraneuronal) and COMT (extraneuronal). Every sympathomimetic and sympatholytic drug acts on a specific receptor subtype or a specific step of this circuit.

High-Yield Notes

The two rules: α1 / α2 / β1–3 all couple to G-proteins; D1 too. α1 = Gq (↑IP₃/DAG/Ca²⁺ → contract), α2 = Gi (↓cAMP; presynaptic auto-inhibition), β1 = β2 = β3 = D1 = Gs (↑cAMP). Memorize tissue dominance: α1 vasoconstricts, β2 vasodilates, β1 drives the heart, β2 dilates bronchi, β3 lipolyzes fat, D1 dilates renal/mesenteric vessels. The exam-killer concept is Dale's vasomotor reversal: a small dose of epinephrine raises MAP via α1, but if you have already given a nonselective α-blocker, epinephrine's residual β2 vasodilation drops MAP.

Pheochromocytoma: Catecholamine Biology

Catecholamine-secreting tumor (most often adrenal medulla; "10% tumor": 10% bilateral, 10% extra-adrenal, 10% malignant, 10% familial; MEN 2A/2B, NF1, VHL). Tumor cells synthesize and release massive NE/epi via the standard catecholamine pathway. Classic triad: episodic headache, palpitations, diaphoresis with paroxysmal hypertension. Confirm with elevated plasma free metanephrines or 24-h urinary fractionated metanephrines. Treatment is surgical resection; the antiadrenergic preoperative drug sequencing is covered in §8 and is the highest-yield tested concept.

8. Antiadrenergic Pharmacology

Antiadrenergic agents silence the sympathetic system at four distinct points: α-blockers at α1/α2 on smooth muscle and presynaptic terminals; β-blockers at β1/β2/β3 on heart, kidney, lungs, and metabolic tissue; mixed α + β blockers (labetalol, carvedilol) for combined load; and indirect adrenolytics that disrupt catecholamine synthesis (metyrosine), storage (reserpine), release (guanethidine), or metabolism (MAOIs). Three exam-killer traps recur: (1) α1 blockade before β-blockade in pheochromocytoma; give the β-blocker first and unopposed α-mediated vasoconstriction can drive a hypertensive crisis. (2) Cocaine + β-blocker is the same disaster: cocaine raises NE, β-blockade leaves α1 unopposed → coronary vasospasm + severe HTN. (3) Epinephrine reversal; after a nonselective α-blocker, epinephrine's β2 vasodilation drops BP instead of raising it.

High-Yield Notes

Four classes by mechanism: α-blockers (selective vs nonselective; reversible vs irreversible); β-blockers (cardioselective β1 vs nonselective; with or without intrinsic sympathomimetic activity; lipid-soluble vs hydrophilic); mixed α + β (labetalol, carvedilol); and indirect adrenolytics that block synthesis (metyrosine), vesicular storage (reserpine), release (guanethidine), or degradation (MAOIs). The drug you pick is dictated by the receptor target and the comorbidity profile; selectivity, duration, lipid solubility, and route are how examiners distinguish two otherwise-similar agents.

Pheochromocytoma: Drug Sequencing Trap

Catecholamine-secreting tumor (most often adrenal medulla; "10% tumor": 10% bilateral, 10% extra-adrenal, 10% malignant, 10% familial; MEN 2A/2B, NF1, VHL). Episodic headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, hypertension. Workup = plasma free metanephrines or 24-h urinary metanephrines. Treatment is surgery, but preop sequencing is high-yield: phenoxybenzamine first (irreversible α-blockade, 10–14 days, volume expansion) → then add a β-blocker (typically propranolol) for tachycardia. Reversing the order leaves α-vasoconstriction unopposed by β2 vasodilation, and a tumor manipulation surge produces a hypertensive catastrophe. Intraoperative HTN spikes are managed with IV phentolamine or nitroprusside.
