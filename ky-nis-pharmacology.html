1. Autacoids: Histamine & Antihistamines

Autacoids are local hormones with intense biologic activity that act near their site of synthesis. Histamine is formed by decarboxylation of L-histidine and stored in mast cell and basophil granules, with non mast cell pools in the brain (a neurotransmitter) and in gastric enterochromaffin like (ECL) cells that drive acid secretion. It is released by immunologic (IgE crosslinking) and by chemical or mechanical triggers; certain drugs such as morphine, tubocurarine, and vancomycin displace it directly. This section covers the four histamine receptors, the actions histamine produces through each, and the antihistamines that block them.

High-Yield Notes

The exam hinges on linking each receptor to its messenger and effect. The H1 receptor raises calcium through Gq and produces vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, bronchoconstriction, and sensory nerve stimulation (itch and pain). The H2 receptor raises cAMP through Gs and drives parietal cell acid secretion plus modest cardiac stimulation. The presynaptic H3 brain autoreceptor inhibits histamine release, and the H4 receptor on eosinophils, neutrophils, and CD4 T cells mediates chemotaxis. Intravenous histamine lowers blood pressure by vasodilation, triggering a baroreflex tachycardia with flushing, warmth, and headache; intradermal histamine gives the triple response (red spot, wheal, flare).

The antihistamines below are H1 antagonists grouped by sedation. First generation agents are lipid soluble, cross the blood brain barrier, and add antimuscarinic activity, so they sedate and treat motion sickness, vomiting, and vertigo. Second generation agents stay out of the brain and are non sedating, the choice when alertness matters, but are poor antiemetics. Betahistine, by contrast, is a histamine analogue used for Meniere vertigo.

Exam pearls:
  • H1 = calcium/Gq (vasodilation, permeability, bronchoconstriction, itch); H2 = cAMP/Gs (gastric acid).
  • Triple response of Lewis: red spot (capillary dilation), wheal (permeability), flare (axon-reflex arteriolar dilation).

  • Betahistine, a histamine analogue, treats Meniere vertigo by inner-ear vasodilation; avoid in asthma and peptic ulcer.

2. NSAIDs

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs all share one mechanism: inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX), the enzyme that converts arachidonic acid into prostaglandins, prostacyclin, and thromboxane. Membrane phospholipids are first cleaved by phospholipase A2 to release arachidonic acid, which then feeds two arms: the COX pathway (targeted by NSAIDs) and the lipoxygenase pathway (leukotrienes, spared by NSAIDs). Two COX isoforms matter: COX-1 is constitutive and protective (gastric mucus, platelet thromboxane, renal blood flow), while COX-2 is inducible at sites of inflammation. Most NSAIDs block both reversibly; aspirin is the exception, irreversibly acetylating COX. Their shared profile is analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory, and their shared toxicities follow directly from loss of the protective COX-1 prostaglandins.

High-Yield Notes

The slide deck classes NSAIDs into non-selective (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen, indomethacin, ketorolac, piroxicam), preferential COX-2 (nimesulide, meloxicam, diclofenac, aceclofenac, nabumetone), selective COX-2 coxibs (celecoxib, etoricoxib, parecoxib), and the para-aminophenol paracetamol. Aspirin is the only irreversible COX inhibitor, acetylating platelet COX so thromboxane A2 is lost for the platelet lifespan (basis of low-dose antiplatelet use); its effects are dose dependent and it is avoided in children with viral illness (Reye syndrome). Salicylate toxicity is triphasic: respiratory alkalosis first, then anion gap metabolic acidosis; tinnitus is the early clue and urinary alkalinization with bicarbonate speeds excretion of the weak acid.

Blocking constitutive COX-1 produces the shared toxicities: gastric ulceration and bleeding plus renal impairment from loss of prostaglandin mediated afferent arteriolar dilation (worst in hypovolemia and heart failure). The selective COX-2 agents (celecoxib) spare the stomach and platelets but suppress endothelial prostacyclin while leaving platelet thromboxane intact, shifting toward thrombosis. Paracetamol is the outlier, with essentially no anti-inflammatory action (inactivated by tissue peroxides) and no antiplatelet effect, but dose dependent hepatotoxicity via NAPQI (glutathione depletion, worse with alcohol via CYP2E1), treated with N-acetylcysteine.

3. Autacoids: Serotonin & Migraine

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is an autacoid synthesized from the amino acid L-tryptophan by hydroxylase then decarboxylase, and degraded by MAO to 5-HIAA. About 90 percent of body 5-HT sits in enterochromaffin cells of the gut wall, with smaller stores in platelets (taken up by SERT, stored by VMAT-2) and in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem; it is also the precursor of pineal melatonin. It signals through four receptor families and 14 subtypes, all G-protein coupled except 5-HT3, which is a ligand gated cation channel. A handful of subtypes drive the high-yield pharmacology: the triptans that abort migraine through 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D agonism, the ergot alkaloids, the setrons that block 5-HT3 to control emesis, buspirone at 5-HT1A, and the agents given to prevent migraine.

High-Yield Notes

Receptor identity drives every drug choice here. 5-HT1A in the raphe nuclei and hippocampus governs mood and is the target of the anxiolytic partial agonist buspirone. 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D on cranial vessels mediate the vasoconstriction that aborts a migraine, which is exactly where the triptans (sumatriptan, naratriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan) act as selective agonists. 5-HT2A on vascular smooth muscle and platelets causes vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation; LSD is its nonselective agonist and ketanserin and cyproheptadine block it. 5-HT3 is a ligand gated cation channel in the area postrema and on vagal afferents; the setrons (ondansetron, granisetron, tropisetron) block it to stop chemotherapy and radiotherapy emesis. 5-HT4 agonism (metoclopramide, cisapride) is prokinetic.

Two cross-cutting traps: triptans and ergots both vasoconstrict, so they are contraindicated in coronary artery disease and are never combined or used within 24 hours of each other; and excess serotonergic drugs cause serotonin syndrome (altered mental status, autonomic instability, clonus and hyperreflexia), treated by stopping the drug and giving the 5-HT2 antagonist cyproheptadine.   • Propranolol is first line prophylaxis; amitriptyline if depression or insomnia; topiramate or valproate as alternatives.

4. Antirheumatoid & Antigout Drugs

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic, progressive, crippling autoimmune disease in which IgM immune complexes activate complement and release cytokines, mainly TNF-alpha and IL-1, driving joint inflammation, synovial proliferation, and destruction of articular cartilage. The goals of therapy are to relieve pain, swelling, and stiffness, to prevent cartilage damage and bony erosions, and to preserve joint function. NSAIDs are the first-line drugs for symptomatic relief but do not arrest the disease process. The agents that suppress the rheumatoid process, induce remission, and retard progression are the disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs); they lack nonspecific anti-inflammatory or analgesic action and are added to NSAIDs. Gout, by contrast, is a metabolic disorder of hyperuricemia (normal plasma urate 2 to 6 mg/dl) in which sodium urate crystals precipitate in tissue and trigger inflammation; its therapy splits cleanly into agents that abort the acute attack and agents that lower urate for chronic control. This section emphasizes each drug's mechanism, signature toxicity, and the scenario in which it is preferred.

High-Yield Notes

Methotrexate is the DMARD of first choice and standard drug: it inhibits AICAR transformylase, thymidylate synthase, and dihydrofolate reductase, has a relatively rapid onset (about 4 weeks) on low weekly oral dosing, and is the agent every new patient is measured against. Its two exam hooks are that probenecid and aspirin raise its levels and that it is contraindicated in pregnancy, lactation, liver disease, leucopenia, active infection, and peptic ulcer. Among the other nonbiologic agents, azathioprine is a 6-mercaptopurine prodrug (via thiopurine methyltransferase), steroid-sparing but not combined with methotrexate; sulfasalazine (active moiety sulfapyridine) and hydroxychloroquine, preferred over chloroquine, treat milder disease, the latter recognized by retinal damage; and leflunomide, whose active metabolite blocks dihydroorotate dehydrogenase and pyrimidine synthesis (raises hepatotoxicity with methotrexate). Gold salts and d-penicillamine are obsolete.

The biologic agents bind and inhibit cytokines and are reserved for severe refractory RA, usually with methotrexate; all are parenteral. The key distinction is that etanercept is a soluble TNF-receptor-IgG1 Fc fusion protein, whereas infliximab (chimeric) and adalimumab are anti-TNF monoclonal antibodies; infliximab can worsen heart failure. Among non-TNF biologics, anakinra blocks the IL-1 receptor, tocilizumab blocks IL-6, rituximab depletes B cells, and abatacept inhibits T-cell activation and must not be combined with a TNF inhibitor. For gout, the acute attack is aborted by colchicine (binds tubulin, blocks granulocyte migration; dose-limiting diarrhea, maximum 6.0 mg per course), NSAIDs, or corticosteroids, while chronic urate-lowering uses the xanthine oxidase inhibitor allopurinol (drug of choice; metabolite alloxanthine is long-acting), the non-purine febuxostat, and the uricosuric probenecid.

5. Skeletal Muscle Relaxants

Skeletal muscle relaxants divide into peripherally acting agents that work at the neuromuscular junction or directly on muscle, and centrally acting spasmolytics that depress spinal reflexes without loss of consciousness. The peripheral junctional blockers are nondepolarizing (competitive) antagonists of the Nm nicotinic receptor and the single depolarizing agent succinylcholine, used mainly as anesthetic adjuvants for intubation and surgery. Dantrolene is the one directly acting agent and uniquely targets the muscle itself. The centrally acting drugs (baclofen, tizanidine, diazepam, mephenesin congeners) treat chronic spasticity and painful muscle spasm. This section emphasizes each agent's site of action, signature adverse effect, and the classic exam scenarios that pin one drug to one mechanism.

High-Yield Notes

The central comparison is nondepolarizing vs depolarizing block. Nondepolarizing agents (rocuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium, pancuronium, d-tubocurarine) are competitive Nm antagonists, so raising acetylcholine with an anticholinesterase (neostigmine, edrophonium) reverses them; sugammadex is the selective antidote for rocuronium and vecuronium. Succinylcholine, the lone depolarizing agent, resembles two acetylcholines joined together, causes persistent depolarization (transient fasciculations then flaccid paralysis), and is hydrolyzed by pseudocholinesterase in about 5 minutes. Its exam hooks are malignant hyperthermia (worst with succinylcholine plus a volatile anesthetic, treated with dantrolene), hyperkalemia (avoid in crush, burn, denervation), and prolonged apnea from inherited atypical pseudocholinesterase.

6. Toxicology: Principles, Antidotes & Heavy Metals

Toxicology is the study of the adverse effects of chemicals on living organisms; as Paracelsus framed it, the dose differentiates a poison from a remedy. An antidote counteracts a toxin and comes in three flavors: chemical (reacts to form a harmless compound, e.g. chelators for heavy metals), mechanical (prevents absorption, e.g. activated charcoal), and physiologic (opposes the effect, e.g. atropine for organophosphates). The deck's core is heavy metals, which are toxic because they bind reactive ligands, especially the sulfhydryl (SH) groups of metabolic enzymes; chelators compete for the metal and form a stable excretable complex. The exam rewards three reflexes: recognize the classic clinical signature of each toxin, pair it to the correct antidote, and remember the contraindication traps, above all that dimercaprol is contraindicated in iron and cadmium poisoning.

High-Yield Notes

Lead is the heaviest tested metal. It has affinity for SH groups and poisons zinc-dependent enzymes of heme synthesis, producing a microcytic anemia with basophilic stippling; it adds a motor neuropathy (wrist and foot drop), abdominal colic, CNS encephalopathy in children, gingival lead lines, and radiodense metaphyseal lead lines. Lead accumulates in bone where it replaces calcium, giving a roughly 30-year half-life. Treat with calcium EDTA, add dimercaprol (BAL) when encephalopathy is present or the level exceeds 70 mcg/dL, give oral succimer (DMSA) for milder asymptomatic elevations, control seizures with diazepam, and relieve colic with calcium gluconate.

Arsenic binds adjacent SH groups on metabolic enzymes, cutting cellular ATP; acute poisoning gives garlic breath with rice-water stools, and chronic exposure leaves Mees lines (transverse white nail bands) and rain-drop skin pigmentation. First-line antidote is dimercaprol (BAL in oil); activated charcoal does not adsorb arsenic well. Mercury binds SH groups; the lipid-soluble organic (methyl) form from fish crosses the placenta and causes a delayed neurologic syndrome, while inorganic salts cause proximal tubular necrosis and anuria; treat with BAL, succimer, or penicillamine. Iron overdose (a classic pediatric tablet ingestion) causes bloody vomiting, bloody stool, and metabolic acidosis, treated with deferoxamine (turns urine reddish-brown). Copper (Wilson disease, low ceruloplasmin, Kayser-Fleischer rings) is the drug-of-choice indication for penicillamine. Cadmium appears on the deck roster; like iron it forms a toxic complex with dimercaprol, so dimercaprol is contraindicated for both.

7. Anticancer I: Classes & Mechanisms

Anticancer drugs are organized first by whether they act only on dividing cells (cell-cycle specific, CCS) or in any phase including the resting fraction (cell-cycle nonspecific, CCNS), and then by mechanistic class. CCS drugs (antimetabolites in S, vinca alkaloids and taxanes in M, bleomycin in G2) work best against rapidly proliferating tumors such as leukemias, while CCNS drugs (alkylating agents, platinum compounds, most antitumor antibiotics, hormonal agents) reach quiescent G0 cells and are the backbone for bulky slow-growing solid tumors. The exam tests where each class acts in the cycle and the molecular mechanism that defines it, so the two are learned together.

High-Yield Notes

Cells in G0 are quiescent and resist phase-specific drugs, so the cell-cycle position a class acts in predicts which tumors it cures. The first table maps each mechanistic class to its cell-cycle specificity, prototype drug, and defining molecular action; the second separates the entire roster into CCS versus CCNS exactly as the deck does. Learn the prototype first, then fill in the congeners.

8. Anticancer II: Specific Agents, Toxicities & Rescue

Once the classes are learned, the exam turns to the signature dose-limiting toxicity of the headline drugs and the matched protective or rescue agent. Most stems are a vignette in which one organ fails after chemotherapy, and the single best answer is either the offending drug or the antidote that prevents the injury. The high-value triplets are doxorubicin and cardiotoxicity rescued by dexrazoxane, cyclophosphamide and hemorrhagic cystitis prevented by mesna, methotrexate rescued by leucovorin, cisplatin nephro- and ototoxicity reduced by amifostine, plus the toxicities that have no antidote (bleomycin lung fibrosis, vincristine neuropathy). Memorize the drug, the organ, and the rescue together and the vignettes answer themselves.

High-Yield Notes

Each headline drug carries one classic dose-limiting toxicity, and a subset has a specific rescue or protective agent that the exam pairs against it. 9. Immunomodulators & Therapeutic Cytokines

Immunomodulators divide into drugs that dampen the immune response (immunosuppressants, used to prevent transplant rejection and treat autoimmune disease) and drugs that boost it (immunostimulants and therapeutic cytokines, used mostly against cancer). The exam concentrates on the transplant immunosuppressants: the molecular target of each, the shared nephrotoxicity of the calcineurin inhibitors, and the single toxicity that distinguishes one agent from the next. A second cluster of questions pairs each biologic antibody to the receptor it blocks, and each cytokine or stimulant to its tumor indication.

High-Yield Notes

The calcineurin to NFAT to IL-2 axis is the spine of transplant pharmacology. Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin and tacrolimus binds FKBP, but both complexes inhibit calcineurin, so NFAT never reaches the nucleus and IL-2 is never transcribed. Sirolimus also binds FKBP yet blocks mTOR instead, which is why it spares the kidney. Everything else is learned as a target-to-toxicity or target-to-use pair.

  • Both sirolimus and tacrolimus bind FKBP; the target is the discriminator (mTOR versus calcineurin), and only sirolimus spares the kidney and raises triglycerides.
  • Two interaction traps: never add allopurinol to full dose azathioprine, and never combine sirolimus with cyclosporine.
  • Suppressants prevent rejection; the stimulants on this deck (BCG, levamisole, interferon, thalidomide) are aimed at tumors.
