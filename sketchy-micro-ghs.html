1. Herpes Simplex Virus Types 1 & 2 (Herpesviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Painful grouped vesicles on an erythematous base (cold sore, genital ulcers), recurring in the same dermatome
  • HSV-1: gingivostomatitis, herpes labialis, keratitis, temporal lobe encephalitis; HSV-2: genital herpes, neonatal herpes, aseptic meningitis
  • Diagnosis: PCR (CSF PCR for encephalitis/meningitis) or viral culture; Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells but is nonspecific
  • Treatment: acyclovir/valacyclovir (needs viral thymidine kinase); foscarnet for acyclovir-resistant strains
  • Buzzwords: Cowdry A bodies, dendritic corneal ulcer, temporal lobe hemorrhagic necrosis, latency in trigeminal (HSV-1) and sacral (HSV-2) ganglia
  1. 1Hermes sprinting: HSV-1 and HSV-2 are DNA viruses of the Herpesviridae family. They are highly contagious and spread mainly by direct contact with infected skin, mucosa, or body fluids (including saliva and genital secretions), and also vertically from mother to baby (neonatal herpes, usually at delivery).
  2. 2Cool colors: HSV-1 and HSV-2 are DNA viruses (the cool colors group DNA viruses). Most DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus, which is why HSV produces intranuclear inclusions.
  3. 3Enveloping toga: HSV-1 and HSV-2 are enveloped viruses. The envelope (acquired from the host nuclear membrane) makes them fragile outside the body and explains why transmission needs close contact.
  4. 4Double road lines: HSV-1 and HSV-2 are double-stranded, linear DNA viruses.
  5. 5Cow spots: Intranuclear eosinophilic Cowdry type A inclusions are characteristic of cells infected with HSV (also seen with VZV and CMV). Infected cells (keratinocytes or neurons) show a clear nuclear halo, multinucleation, nuclear molding, and chromatin margination.
  6. 6Torch: HSV is part of the TORCHeS group of infections transmitted vertically across the placenta: T = Toxoplasma; O = Other (varicella-zoster virus, parvovirus B19, Zika virus, HIV); R = Rubella; C = CMV; H = HSV; S = Syphilis. Neonatal HSV, usually acquired at delivery from maternal genital infection, causes skin/eye/mouth lesions, encephalitis, or disseminated disease.
  7. 7Red lips: Primary HSV-1 infection often presents as gingivostomatitis, with painful vesicles and ulcerations inside the mouth and around the lips. It is most common in children.
  8. 8Lip ulcers: Herpes labialis (cold sores) presents as clusters of small, painful vesicles on a red base at the vermilion border of the lip. They rupture into shallow ulcers and heal with a yellow crust. It is most commonly caused by HSV-1.
  9. 9Serpent caduceus: Herpes keratoconjunctivitis, mainly HSV-1, causes eye pain, photophobia, redness, tearing, and decreased visual acuity. Fluorescein slit-lamp exam shows branching (dendritic, 'serpiginous') corneal ulcers, which are diagnostic. Treat with topical antivirals; topical steroids alone worsen it.⭐ Dendritic corneal ulcer on fluorescein = HSV keratitis.
  10. 10Red wings on helmet near temples: HSV-1 encephalitis is the most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in the U.S. It causes hemorrhagic necrosis of the temporal lobes, producing fever, seizures, altered mental status, personality changes, and olfactory hallucinations from limbic involvement. Diagnose with CSF PCR (MRI shows temporal lobe changes) and treat empirically with IV acyclovir.⭐ HSV encephalitis = temporal lobe + hemorrhage; start acyclovir before results.
  11. 11Black helmet: HSV-1 encephalitis is the most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in the U.S. It causes hemorrhagic necrosis of the temporal lobes, producing fever, seizures, altered mental status, personality changes, and olfactory hallucinations from limbic involvement. Diagnose with CSF PCR (MRI shows temporal lobe changes) and treat empirically with IV acyclovir.check original
  12. 12Crazy eyes: HSV-1 encephalitis is the most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in the U.S. It causes hemorrhagic necrosis of the temporal lobes, producing fever, seizures, altered mental status, personality changes, and olfactory hallucinations from limbic involvement. The altered mental status and behavioral change reflect temporal lobe/limbic damage. Diagnose with CSF PCR (MRI shows temporal lobe changes) and treat empirically with IV acyclovir.
  13. 13Three helmet gems: After primary infection HSV-1 remains latent in the trigeminal ganglia (sensory neurons). Reactivation by stress, sunlight (UV), fever, or immunosuppression causes cold sores, keratoconjunctivitis, or, rarely, temporal lobe encephalitis.
  14. 14Dew drops on roses: HSV lesions are painful grouped vesicles or pustules on an erythematous base, classically likened to 'dew drops on a rose petal.' They rupture into shallow ulcers, crust over as they heal, and recur because of viral latency.
  15. 15Dew drops on finger: Herpetic whitlow is an HSV infection of the finger with painful, clustered vesicles on an erythematous base. It is classically seen in healthcare workers and dentists (contact with oral secretions) and in thumb-sucking children.
  16. 16Target stamps on hand and arm: Erythema multiforme is a hypersensitivity reaction triggered by certain drugs and infections, with HSV being a classic infectious trigger. It produces target lesions (dusky center, pale edematous zone, outer erythematous ring) that start on the backs of the hands and feet and spread centrally.⭐ HSV is the most common infectious cause of erythema multiforme.
  17. 17Ruffles by groin: Initial genital herpes, often HSV-2 (HSV-1 is increasingly common), causes painful grouped vesicles on an erythematous base in the genital or perianal region, with shallow ulcers, dysuria, and tender inguinal lymphadenopathy. It is spread by sexual contact, including by asymptomatic shedding.
  18. 18Codpiece: HSV-2 establishes latency mainly in the sacral ganglia. Reactivation causes recurrent genital or perianal lesions, usually milder and shorter than the primary episode.
  19. 19Neck brace: HSV-2 is associated with aseptic (lymphocytic) meningitis, particularly during primary genital infection. Patients have headache, fever, and neck stiffness, with lymphocytic pleocytosis on CSF analysis (diagnose with CSF PCR). It can recur (Mollaret meningitis).
  20. 20Tank + giant spots: A Tzanck smear (scraping of a lesion base) shows multinucleated giant cells in HSV infection. It is nonspecific, since VZV also produces them, and has been replaced by PCR or viral culture for specific diagnosis.
  21. 21Violet recycling bin: Acyclovir and valacyclovir are used for treatment and for prophylaxis (suppressive therapy) against recurrent outbreaks. They are guanosine analogs activated by viral thymidine kinase and terminate viral DNA synthesis by inhibiting viral DNA polymerase. Resistance (thymidine kinase mutation) is treated with foscarnet.

2. Norovirus & Norwalk Virus (Caliciviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: outbreak of acute vomiting and watery diarrhea on a cruise ship, in a school, or after shellfish or buffet food
  • Positive-sense, nonenveloped ssRNA virus (Caliciviridae); the leading cause of viral gastroenteritis in adults and of foodborne outbreaks in the U.S.
  • Incubation 12-48 hours, self-limited in 1-3 days; diagnosis is clinical (RT-PCR on stool if needed)
  • Treatment is supportive (oral rehydration); no specific antiviral and no routine vaccine
  • Prevention: hand washing with soap and water (alcohol sanitizers are less effective), surface bleach cleaning; highly contagious with a very low infectious dose
  1. 1Narwhal: Noroviruses (including the Norwalk virus) are positive-sense RNA viruses in the Caliciviridae family.
  2. 2"Cali-sea-cruise": Noroviruses belong to the Caliciviridae family; the 'Cali-sea-cruise' pun ties the family name to cruise ship outbreaks.
  3. 3Warm colors: Noroviruses are RNA viruses (the warm colors group RNA viruses). Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm.
  4. 4Positive sun: Noroviruses are positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses: the genome can act directly as mRNA and is translated by host ribosomes immediately after entry. Naked positive-sense RNA alone is infectious.
  5. 5Statue of David: Noroviruses lack an envelope (naked viruses). This makes them resistant to ethanol, detergents, and drying, so they persist on surfaces and spread easily.
  6. 6Roll of tickets: After entry the whole genome is translated into one long polyprotein, which viral proteases cleave into functional proteins (typical of positive-sense RNA viruses). Protease inhibitors are therefore a potential target.
  7. 7Cruise ship: Norovirus outbreaks occur in confined settings such as cruise ships. It is highly contagious, with a very low infectious dose, and spreads fecal-oral, via vomit aerosols, and on contaminated surfaces.
  8. 8Children: Outbreaks also occur in day care centers and schools, where close contact and poor hand hygiene allow rapid spread.
  9. 9Shellfish buffet: Outbreaks also follow consumption of contaminated shellfish (filter feeders, e.g., raw oysters) or food handled by many people at buffets and cafeterias. Infected food handlers are a classic source.
  10. 10Mud spray: Norovirus causes acute, severe, 'explosive' watery diarrhea with nausea, vomiting, cramps, low-grade fever, and malaise. Symptoms begin 12-48 hours after exposure and resolve in 1-3 days. Treatment is supportive with rehydration.⭐ Think norovirus: sudden vomiting + watery diarrhea outbreak on a cruise ship; no vaccine, alcohol gel is poor.

3. Rotavirus & Colorado Tick Fever Virus (Reoviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Rotavirus: most common cause of severe watery diarrhea and dehydration in children under 5 worldwide, peaking in winter
  • Mechanism: NSP4 enterotoxin plus villous destruction causes secretory and malabsorptive diarrhea; diagnosis by stool antigen (ELISA)
  • Reoviridae: naked, double-stranded segmented RNA viruses (rotavirus 11 segments), replicating in the cytoplasm
  • Treatment is supportive (oral rehydration); prevention is the live oral vaccine (RotaTeq, Rotarix), with a small intussusception risk
  • Colorado tick fever: Rocky Mountain tick bite (Dermacentor andersoni), biphasic fever, flu-like illness, usually no rash
  1. 1Rio Grande Prix: Reoviruses (e.g., rotavirus and Colorado tick fever virus) are RNA viruses; 'Reo' stands for Respiratory Enteric Orphan.
  2. 2Warm colors: Reoviruses are RNA viruses (the warm colors group RNA viruses).
  3. 3Statue of David: Reoviruses lack an envelope (naked viruses). They are resistant to drying and stomach acid and spread fecal-oral.
  4. 4Double-stranded rope: Reoviruses are double-stranded RNA viruses. Because they are dsRNA, they are not classified as positive- or negative-sense, and they replicate in the cytoplasm. They carry their own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase in the virion, because host cells cannot copy dsRNA.
  5. 5No positive sun + no moon with negative sign-shaped clouds: Reoviruses are not classified as positive-sense or negative-sense (no sun, no negative moon) because the genome is double-stranded RNA. Each segment is transcribed by the viral polymerase to make mRNA in the cytoplasm.
  6. 611 boats: Reoviruses have segmented genomes (about 10-12 dsRNA segments); rotavirus has 11 segments. Segmentation allows reassortment between strains, which can generate new strains.⭐ Rotavirus = 11 segments, which can reassort.
  7. 7Boat rotor: Rotavirus is a double-stranded RNA virus in the Reoviridae family. Its wheel-like appearance on electron microscopy gives the name (Latin rota = wheel).
  8. 8Brown water: Rotavirus causes gastroenteritis with profuse watery diarrhea (non-bloody), often with vomiting and fever, and can lead to dehydration in infants. Diagnosis is by stool antigen detection (ELISA).
  9. 9"Nine SPeed 4 stroke": Rotavirus watery diarrhea is mediated by the NSP4 (nonstructural protein 4) enterotoxin, which increases Cl- permeability and causes Cl- secretion into the intestinal lumen. Na+ follows to maintain electroneutrality, water follows Na+ osmotically, and destruction of villous epithelium worsens fluid and electrolyte loss.check original
  10. 10"Chlo-rider" boat: NSP4 enterotoxin increases chloride permeability, so Cl- is secreted into the lumen and Na+ and water follow. Destruction of mature villous enterocytes adds malabsorptive loss of fluid and electrolytes.check original
  11. 11Snow-capped mountains: Rotavirus incidence peaks in winter in temperate climates, because the virus survives longer in cold, low-humidity conditions and indoor crowding promotes fecal-oral spread.
  12. 12Kids: Children, especially those under 5 years old, are most susceptible to rotavirus. Day care outbreaks are common.
  13. 13"#1" foam finger: Rotavirus is the leading cause of severe diarrhea in young children worldwide. Vaccination has dramatically reduced hospitalizations.
  14. 14"Welcome to Colorado" + tick: Colorado tick fever virus is a dsRNA virus in the Reoviridae family. It is transmitted by the Rocky Mountain wood tick (Dermacentor andersoni).
  15. 15Vomiting + sweating: Colorado tick fever presents with flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, headache, myalgias, fatigue, anorexia, vomiting), often with a biphasic ('saddleback') fever, in high-elevation areas of the western U.S. (Rocky Mountain states). Unlike Rocky Mountain spotted fever, it typically has no rash.⭐ Tick-borne fever without rash in the Rockies = Colorado tick fever.
  16. 16Water bottle: Treatment of rotavirus and Colorado tick fever is supportive. Oral rehydration therapy is the mainstay for rotavirus (IV fluids if severe dehydration).
  17. 17"Live" + syringe microphone: The rotavirus vaccine is a live attenuated oral vaccine: RotaTeq (pentavalent, 3 doses at 2, 4, and 6 months) or Rotarix (monovalent, 2 doses at 2 and 4 months). The first dose must be given before 15 weeks of age and the series completed by 8 months, to reduce intussusception risk. It is contraindicated in severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) and prior intussusception.
  18. 18Telescope: The rotavirus vaccine can rarely cause intussusception (bowel 'telescoping' into itself): viral replication in the gut causes Peyer's patch hypertrophy, which can act as a lead point. Presents with colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, and 'currant jelly' stool.

4. Giardia lamblia

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: hiker or camper who drank stream or lake water with foul-smelling, greasy, non-bloody diarrhea, bloating, and flatulence
  • Cysts are the infectious form (fecal-oral, contaminated water); trophozoites attach via a ventral sucking disc and cause malabsorption
  • Diagnosis: stool O&P (pear-shaped trophozoites with two nuclei, or cysts) or stool Giardia antigen ELISA
  • Treatment: metronidazole (also tinidazole, nitazoxanide)
  • Associations: backpackers, day care outbreaks, IgA deficiency/CVID; cysts survive chlorination, so boil or filter water
  1. 1"Giardia jungle ride": Giardia intestinalis (also called G. lamblia or G. duodenalis) is an intestinal protozoan with a flagellated trophozoite stage and a cyst stage.
  2. 2Poopy water: Giardia cysts are the infectious stage and spread by the fecal-oral route, usually through contaminated water or food. Ingesting only a small number of cysts can cause infection.
  3. 3Bubbles in water: Infected people shed cysts in feces, contaminating water and food. Cysts are hardy and resistant to chlorination at usual concentrations, so water must be boiled or filtered.
  4. 4Backpacks: Giardiasis is a risk for people drinking from natural water sources, such as hikers and campers, who may ingest contaminated water. Animals (e.g., beavers) can contaminate streams. Day care outbreaks also occur.
  5. 5Water bottle + poopy water: Poorly purified or untreated water is a common vehicle of transmission. Prevention is by filtering, boiling, or treating water before drinking.
  6. 6Campers holding noses: Giardiasis typically causes foul-smelling, greasy, non-bloody diarrhea with bloating, abdominal discomfort, and flatulence. Blood and fecal leukocytes are absent because the organism does not invade the mucosa. It may become chronic.⭐ Foul-smelling, fatty, non-bloody diarrhea in a hiker = Giardia.
  7. 7Yellow stool: Trophozoites attach to the small intestinal mucosa and disrupt villus structure and function, causing malabsorption, steatorrhea (fatty stools), diarrhea, and potential deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Secondary lactase deficiency can also occur.
  8. 8Trophozoite shields: The flagellated trophozoite is pear-shaped (broad anterior, tapering posterior) with two prominent nuclei that look like eyes (a 'face'), multiple flagella for motility, and a ventral adhesive (sucking) disc that attaches to the intestinal mucosa. Trophozoites do not invade the tissue.
  9. 9Shields in water: Finding Giardia cysts or trophozoites in stool confirms the diagnosis. Trophozoites are seen in fresh watery stool and cysts in formed stool.
  10. 10"O&P": Stool ova and parasite (O&P) examination is a reliable method for diagnosing giardiasis. Multiple samples may be needed because shedding is intermittent.
  11. 11"ELISA" boat: Stool ELISA for Giardia antigen is a sensitive diagnostic test, alongside O&P. Duodenal aspirate or the string test is an option if stool studies are negative but suspicion is high.
  12. 12Metro: Effective treatments for giardiasis are metronidazole (first line), tinidazole, and nitazoxanide. Metronidazole is activated by anaerobic organisms to form free radicals that damage DNA.

5. Entamoeba histolytica

Exam snapshot
  • Presentation: bloody diarrhea (dysentery) after travel or fecal-oral exposure; RUQ pain/fever from liver abscess.
  • Diagnosis: stool O&P with trophozoites containing ingested RBCs; serology/antigen/PCR help, and serology is key for liver abscess.
  • Histology: flask-shaped colonic ulcers; liver abscess aspirate looks like anchovy paste (usually right lobe, solitary).
  • Treatment: metronidazole or tinidazole for invasive disease, THEN a luminal agent (paromomycin or iodoquinol) to clear cysts.
  • Transmission: fecal-oral via cyst-contaminated food/water; also sexual (oral-anal) contact. Cysts have 4 nuclei, trophozoites eat RBCs.
  1. 1"Do not ENTer - HiSTorical site": Entamoeba histolytica is an intestinal protozoan that causes amebiasis (amebic dysentery and liver abscess). Mnemonic: the 'histolytica' name means tissue-lysing, so it invades tissue.
  2. 2Bubbles in water: The trophozoite is the motile, invasive, feeding stage; the cyst is the infectious, environmentally resistant form passed in stool. Cysts survive in water, which is why contaminated water is the main source.
  3. 3Drinking bubbly water: Amebiasis is acquired mainly by ingesting food or water contaminated with E. histolytica cysts (fecal-oral route). It is most common in areas with poor sanitation and in travelers returning from endemic regions.
  4. 4Holding hands: Transmission can also be sexual through oral-anal contact, since spread is fecal-oral. This makes it a consideration in men who have sex with men.
  5. 5Right lobe dig site: Hepatic amebiasis, a liver abscess, is the most common extraintestinal manifestation. Trophozoites reach the liver via the portal vein, and the abscess is typically solitary and in the right lobe.
  6. 6Clutching right side: Liver abscess presents with RUQ pain, fever, hepatomegaly, malaise and nausea; labs may show leukocytosis and elevated liver enzymes. Diarrhea is often absent at the time the abscess presents.
  7. 7Anchovy paste truck: Abscess aspirate is thick, reddish-brown 'anchovy paste' from necrotic hepatocytes (liquefactive necrosis). Trophozoites are usually absent from the aspirate and sit at the abscess periphery.⭐ Anchovy paste = amebic liver abscess. Treat with metronidazole; drainage is rarely needed.
  8. 8Colon drainage pipe with rust spots: Colonic infection produces ulcers with a narrow neck at the mucosal surface and a broad base in the submucosa (flask shape), most often in the cecum and ascending colon but anywhere in the large bowel.
  9. 9Flask water bottles: Flask-shaped ulcers on histology are the hallmark of invasive intestinal amebiasis. The trophozoites lyse the mucosa and undermine the submucosa.
  10. 10Flask in colon pipe: Colon biopsy showing flask-shaped ulcers with trophozoites confirms intestinal amebiasis when stool studies are unrevealing. Colonoscopy/biopsy is used when the diagnosis is uncertain.
  11. 11Red stools: Bloody diarrhea (dysentery) results from trophozoite invasion of the mucosa and submucosa with tissue necrosis and ulceration. Stool is typically bloody and mucoid with relatively little fever, unlike bacterial dysentery.
  12. 12"O&P" pipe: Diagnosis uses stool O&P: cysts (round, up to 4 nuclei, mostly in formed stool) or trophozoites (motile, mostly in liquid stool of acute infection). Multiple samples may be needed; antigen tests or PCR can distinguish E. histolytica from non-pathogenic E. dispar.
  13. 13Trophozoite puddle + red cups: Trophozoites containing ingested RBCs (erythrophagocytosis) in stool or tissue are diagnostic of invasive amebiasis, and this separates E. histolytica from non-pathogenic amebae.⭐ Ingested RBCs inside the trophozoite = invasive E. histolytica.
  14. 14Metro: Metronidazole (or tinidazole) is first-line for invasive disease and liver abscess because it kills tissue-invading trophozoites. It does not reliably clear luminal cysts, so a luminal agent must follow.
  15. 15Pair of mice: A luminal agent (paromomycin or iodoquinol) is given after metronidazole/tinidazole to eradicate residual cysts in the gut lumen and prevent relapse and spread. Paromomycin is a poorly absorbed aminoglycoside.
  16. 16"Queen Iodo's Tomb": Iodoquinol is the other luminal amebicide used to clear cysts after tissue-active therapy. Either paromomycin or iodoquinol completes the treatment course.

6. Cryptosporidium spp

Exam snapshot
  • Presentation: watery, non-bloody diarrhea; self-limited in normal hosts but severe and chronic in AIDS (CD4 <100).
  • Diagnosis: oocysts in stool, acid-fast (modified Ziehl-Neelsen) pink-red on blue; antigen or PCR also used.
  • Transmission: fecal-oral; contaminated water (pools, lakes, municipal supply) or animal contact; oocysts resist chlorine.
  • Treatment: nitazoxanide for immunocompetent patients; in HIV, restore immunity with ART plus supportive care.
  • Classic association: outbreaks at swimming pools/daycares and in HIV patients with chronic watery diarrhea.
  1. 1Flooded crypt: Cryptosporidium spp. are intestinal protozoa that infect the small-bowel epithelium and cause watery diarrhea. Mnemonic: 'crypt' for Cryptosporidium, flooded because of the profuse watery diarrhea.
  2. 2Bubbles: Infectious oocysts are shed in stool and spread fecal-orally, typically by oocyst-contaminated water or food. They are environmentally hardy and survive for long periods.
  3. 3Multifaceted amethyst gems scattered in water: Oocysts contain four sporozoites and are small (about 4-6 micrometers). They are acid-fast or partially acid-fast on modified Ziehl-Neelsen stain (pink-red on a blue background), which allows stool microscopy diagnosis.
  4. 4Pink acid-fast poncho: Modified acid-fast staining of stool is the classic diagnostic test: pink-red oocysts stand out against the blue counterstain. Antigen detection or PCR can also be used.⭐ Acid-fast oocyst in stool + watery diarrhea = Cryptosporidium.
  5. 5Cowboy hat + wading through dirty water: Risk factors include drinking or swimming in contaminated water (pools, lakes, untreated water) and occupational or recreational contact with animals, especially cattle and calves. Daycare and swimming pool outbreaks are classic.
  6. 6Brown water: Cryptosporidium causes watery, non-bloody diarrhea. In immunocompetent hosts it is self-limited, usually resolving in 1-2 weeks.
  7. 7Immunocompromised cane: In immunocompromised patients, particularly advanced HIV (CD4 below about 100), it causes severe, chronic, profuse watery diarrhea with dehydration and weight loss, and may involve the biliary tract.
  8. 8Broken pipe + leaking brown water: After ingestion, sporozoites invade the epithelial cells of the GI tract (the apical enterocyte, within the brush border), leading to diarrhea through direct cellular damage and malabsorption/secretory changes. The infection is non-invasive beyond the epithelium, so stool is not bloody.
  9. 9Knitted sock: Nitazoxanide is approved for cryptosporidiosis in immunocompetent patients and offers variable benefit in immunocompromised patients.
  10. 10Sock filter: Prevention depends on water filtration, because oocysts are resistant to chlorine and standard chemical disinfection. Hand hygiene and avoiding swimming while having diarrhea also reduce spread.⭐ Chlorine-resistant oocysts explain pool and municipal water outbreaks.
  11. 11Spirit crows: Spiramycin (a macrolide) may relieve diarrhea in some immunocompromised patients, but the key to resolution is immune restoration (ART in HIV) plus supportive care and rehydration. Paromomycin is another agent sometimes used.check original

7. Cestodes

Exam snapshot
  • Taenia solium (pork): eggs cause cysticercosis/neurocysticercosis (seizures, 'Swiss cheese' brain CT); undercooked pork gives intestinal taeniasis.
  • Taenia saginata (beef): intestinal tapeworm only, no hooks, no cysticercosis; praziquantel treats the adult worm.
  • Diphyllobothrium latum (fish): raw freshwater fish, B12 deficiency with megaloblastic anemia, up to 10 m long.
  • Echinococcus granulosus (dog): hydatid liver cyst with eggshell calcification; spillage causes anaphylaxis; albendazole plus surgery.
  • Treatment: praziquantel for adult intestinal tapeworms; albendazole (plus steroids and antiepileptics) for neurocysticercosis and hydatid cysts.
  1. 1Tents: Taenia species are cestodes (tapeworms) that infect humans. Two species matter: T. saginata (beef) and T. solium (pork).
  2. 2Sagging tent: Taenia saginata is the beef tapeworm. Its scolex has suckers but no hooks, and humans get only the adult intestinal worm (taeniasis) from undercooked beef.
  3. 3Cow: Cattle are the intermediate hosts for T. saginata and harbor the larval cysts (cysticerci) in muscle. Humans are the definitive host, harboring the adult worm in the intestine.
  4. 4Sun + "SOLD": Taenia solium is the pork tapeworm. It causes intestinal taeniasis (adult worm) and, if eggs are ingested, cysticercosis.
  5. 5Pig: Pigs are the intermediate hosts for T. solium and carry the larval cysts (cysticerci) in muscle. Eating undercooked pork gives humans the adult intestinal worm.
  6. 6Meat hooks: The scolex of T. solium has a rostellum with two rows of large and small hooks plus suckers, which anchor it to the intestinal wall. T. saginata lacks hooks.
  7. 7Pigs on carousel: Ingestion of T. solium eggs (not the cysts in meat) causes cysticercosis, including neurocysticercosis, an infection of the CNS. It is the most common cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic areas.
  8. 8Poop water: Humans ingest eggs from feces of a person carrying the adult worm, via contaminated food or water (fecal-oral), producing cysticercosis. Eating cyst-containing undercooked pork instead causes intestinal taeniasis. A tapeworm carrier can autoinfect.
  9. 9Motion lines: Neurocysticercosis commonly presents with seizures from cysts in brain parenchyma. It may also cause headache, hydrocephalus or raised intracranial pressure.
  10. 10Swiss cheese: CT/MRI shows multiple cystic lesions in the brain ('Swiss cheese'), well-defined, round and hypodense, some with a hyperdense dot (the scolex). Surrounding edema or ring enhancement occurs in the degenerating stage, and old lesions calcify.⭐ Multiple ring-enhancing cysts with a central dot + seizures = neurocysticercosis.
  11. 11Egg-shaped cheese in poop water: Eggs hatch in the intestine, releasing larvae (oncospheres) that penetrate the gut wall, enter the bloodstream and disseminate to the brain, muscle and eye, forming cysticerci.
  12. 12Pretzel tent: Praziquantel treats intestinal taeniasis (T. saginata and T. solium adult worms) by increasing membrane permeability to calcium, causing paralysis and death of the worm.
  13. 13Strong man named ‘Al' + bendy metal bar: Neurocysticercosis is treated with albendazole (often with praziquantel), along with corticosteroids to blunt inflammation from dying cysts and antiepileptics for seizures. Without steroids, killing cysts can worsen symptoms.
  14. 14Bathroom: Diphyllobothrium latum is the fish tapeworm, found in cold-water regions (e.g., Scandinavia, Great Lakes, Alaska).
  15. 15Fish: Humans are infected by eating undercooked or raw freshwater fish (e.g., pike, salmon) containing larvae (plerocercoids). Think sushi or gefilte fish.
  16. 16"Must B12 to buy fireworks": The worm competes for vitamin B12 in the gut, causing B12 deficiency and megaloblastic anemia. This is the key complication of D. latum.⭐ Raw fish + megaloblastic anemia = Diphyllobothrium latum.
  17. 17Cobalt blue firework: The cobalt-blue firework is cobalamin (B12). Deficiency can also cause neurologic findings such as subacute combined degeneration, with elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine.
  18. 18Red firework blasts: The red blasts represent the resulting megaloblastic (macrocytic) anemia, with large RBCs and hypersegmented neutrophils.
  19. 19Long toilet paper trail: D. latum is the longest human tapeworm, reaching up to about 10 meters, with a very large number of proglottids.
  20. 20Toilet paper segments in mud: Diagnosis is by stool exam showing the characteristic operculated eggs and/or proglottid segments, which are wider than long.

9. Intestinal Nematodes Enterobius, Ancylostoma, Necator, Ascaris, Strongyloides, and Trichinella

Exam snapshot
  • Pinworm (Enterobius): nocturnal perianal itching in a child; Scotch tape test; pyrantel pamoate (treat household).
  • Hookworms (Ancylostoma, Necator): larvae penetrate bare feet, then lungs, then gut; iron deficiency anemia; eggs in stool; albendazole/pyrantel.

  1. 1Vermin lady entering wall: Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm) is an intestinal nematode (roundworm). It is the most common helminth infection in the US, especially in school-age children and daycare or household settings.

  9. 9Rings around neck: Necator americanus is the New World hookworm (the Americas and sub-Saharan Africa). It is an intestinal nematode with cutting plates in its mouth.

  15. 15Bilobed slingshot kid + pink granules: Eosinophilia is common, especially during larval migration through the tissues. Eosinophils are bilobed with bright red-orange granules.
  16. 16"PAM!" + America dude: Pyrantel pamoate treats hookworm infection (nicotinic agonist causing spastic paralysis of the worm). Albendazole or mebendazole are alternatives, and iron is given for anemia.
  17. 17Lumbering tree man: Ascaris lumbricoides is the giant human roundworm, the largest intestinal nematode. Infection occurs by ingesting embryonated eggs from soil-contaminated hands, vegetables, or water (fecal-oral).
  18. 18Arrow symbol on lumbering tree man: Ingested eggs hatch in the small intestine, and larvae penetrate the gut wall and travel via the bloodstream to the lungs. They ascend the trachea, are swallowed at the pharynx, and mature into adults in the small intestine; eggs pass in stool.

  23. 23Strong guy: Strongyloides stercoralis (threadworm) is an intestinal nematode. Unlike the other worms in this video, it can complete its life cycle within the host, which allows persistence for decades.

10. Hepatitis A Virus (Picornaviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Picornavirus: naked, positive-sense ssRNA; acid-stable; fecal-oral spread (shellfish, contaminated water, daycare, travel).
  • Presentation: fatigue, nausea, jaundice, dark urine; kids often anicteric; adult smoker loses taste for cigarettes.
  • Dx: anti-HAV IgM (acute); IgG = past infection or vaccination; very high ALT/AST.
  • Self-limited (about 2 months), no chronic infection or carrier state, no cirrhosis/HCC.
  • Prevention: inactivated vaccine; immune globulin for post-exposure; treatment is supportive.
  1. 1"A"-tagged hippos: Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus in the Picornaviridae family (genus Hepatovirus). It causes acute viral hepatitis.
  2. 2Warm colors: HAV has a single-stranded RNA genome of positive polarity. Warm colors on the picture represent this positive-sense genome, which can be translated directly by host ribosomes (naked RNA is infectious).
  3. 3Positive sun: The positive-sense genome acts as its own mRNA, so it is translated directly into a polyprotein that includes the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.
  4. 4Baby hippo: HAV belongs to the Picornaviridae (pico = small, RNA): small, naked, icosahedral RNA viruses. Other picornaviruses are poliovirus, coxsackievirus, echovirus, and rhinovirus.
  5. 5Statue of David: HAV is a naked (non-enveloped) virus. The lack of an envelope makes it resistant to ether, detergents, and heat, so it persists in the environment.⭐ Naked + acid-stable = fecal-oral.
  6. 6Hippos protected from acid: Being non-enveloped, HAV is acid-stable and survives gastric acid, so it can reach the intestine and be transmitted by the fecal-oral route.
  7. 7Poop: HAV is transmitted fecal-oral: contaminated food or water, or contact with an infected person or object. Outbreaks occur in daycares and in close-contact settings.
  8. 8Water cup: In resource-limited settings with poor sanitation, infection is commonly acquired from contaminated drinking water.
  9. 9Water purification methods: Chlorination, UV irradiation, boiling, and bleach (sodium hypochlorite) inactivate HAV in water. Hand-washing is the key prevention step.
  10. 10USA shellfish stand: In the US, a common source is raw or undercooked shellfish from sewage-contaminated or enclosed waters. Less often, cases arise from sewage-contaminated private wells or close contact with infected individuals.
  11. 11Pulling shellfish from poop water: Shellfish (e.g., oysters, clams) are filter feeders that concentrate virus from sewage-contaminated water. Eating them raw or lightly cooked transmits HAV; contaminated produce is another source.
  12. 12Traveler's backpack: Travelers to areas with high HAV prevalence (parts of Asia, Africa, Mexico, Central and South America, the Caribbean) are at increased risk and should be vaccinated before travel.
  13. 13Enlarged liver spot: Infection is often subclinical, but acute hepatitis can cause tender hepatomegaly and less often splenomegaly. Liver injury is immune-mediated (cytotoxic T cells), with markedly elevated ALT/AST (often thousands) and ALT usually higher than AST.
  14. 14Vomiting: Prodromal and acute symptoms: fatigue, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and low-grade fever.
  15. 15Adult in yellow: Most adults develop jaundice from impaired bilirubin processing by the inflamed liver; dark urine and pale stools may occur. Adults usually have more symptomatic disease than children.
  16. 16Child in tan: Infants and young children usually have anicteric (no jaundice) or asymptomatic infection, so they act as an unrecognized source of spread in outbreaks.
  17. 17Putting out cigarette: Distaste for cigarettes is a classic early prodromal sign in smokers, reflecting hepatic inflammation and nausea before jaundice appears.⭐ Classic buzzword: smoker suddenly dislikes cigarettes.
  18. 18"ONE MONTH only": HAV infection is self-limited. Incubation lasts 2-6 weeks (average about 1 month), followed by a 1-2 week prodrome of fatigue, nausea, and anorexia.
  19. 19"LIMIT one per customer": A jaundice phase lasts 1-2 weeks, and symptoms usually resolve within about 2 months. Acute infection is diagnosed by anti-HAV IgM; anti-HAV IgG indicates past infection or vaccination (lifelong immunity).⭐ IgM anti-HAV = acute; IgG = immune.
  20. 20"NO CARRYING out of food": HAV does not cause a chronic carrier state or chronic liver disease (no cirrhosis or HCC). Fulminant hepatitis is rare, though risk is higher with older age or underlying liver disease.
  21. 21Syringe tranquilizer gun: Prevention is with the inactivated (killed) HAV vaccine, which gives lasting immunity. It is recommended for children, travelers to endemic areas, people with chronic liver disease, men who have sex with men, people experiencing homelessness, and injection drug users. Post-exposure prophylaxis is HAV vaccine and/or immune globulin.

11. Hepatitis B Viral Basics & Infectious Diseases (Hepadnaviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • HBV: enveloped, partially dsDNA, circular; replicates via reverse transcriptase (Hepadnaviridae).
  • Spread: sex, blood, vertical; neonatal infection becomes chronic about 90% of the time vs under 10% in adults.
  • Extrahepatic: serum sickness-like prodrome, polyarteritis nodosa, membranous nephropathy and MPGN.
  • Serology: HBsAg first and persists in chronic; window period = IgM anti-HBc only; vaccine = anti-HBs only; ground-glass hepatocytes.
  • Complications: cirrhosis and HCC. HDV is a defective virus needing HBsAg; superinfection is worse than co-infection.
  • Treatment: NRTIs (lamivudine, entecavir, tenofovir) or pegylated IFN-alpha; newborns get HBIG plus vaccine.
  1. 1Hippies in blue: Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a DNA virus in the Hepadnaviridae family and a major cause of viral hepatitis.
  2. 2Hippo van: HBV is a cause of viral hepatitis (acute and chronic), and is a leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma worldwide.
  3. 3"Hippie pad": HBV is the only member of the Hepadnaviridae (hepatotropic DNA viruses) that causes human disease.
  4. 4Cool colors: HBV is a DNA virus. It is unusual among DNA viruses because it replicates through an RNA intermediate.
  5. 5Hippie clothing: HBV is an enveloped virus; its envelope contains HBsAg (surface antigen). It is also the target of the vaccine, which is made of recombinant HBsAg.
  6. 6Hippies inside dome: HBV has an unusual replication process spanning the nucleus and cytoplasm. In the nucleus the genome becomes cccDNA and is transcribed into RNAs, including the pregenomic RNA.
  7. 7Hippies outside of dome: In the cytoplasm, pregenomic RNA is packaged into the core with the polymerase and reverse-transcribed back into DNA, completing the cycle (see the life-cycle video).
  8. 8Kumbaya circle + half circle: The HBV genome is a small, partially double-stranded circular DNA (one full-length strand and one incomplete strand).
  9. 9"Reverso Transcriptum" spellbook: HBV polymerase has reverse transcriptase activity (an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase), converting pregenomic RNA into DNA. This enzyme is the target of the nucleos(t)ide antiviral drugs.
  10. 10"Sex, drugs, rock & roll": HBV is transmitted by sexual contact, blood exposure (injection drug use, needlestick injuries, shared razors), and transfusions (now rare because of screening). HBV is present in blood and body fluids.⭐ HBV is far more infectious than HIV by needlestick.
  11. 11Red tie-dye outfits of mother and baby: Vertical (perinatal) transmission occurs mainly at childbirth through exposure to maternal blood and secretions; it is the major route of transmission worldwide. Risk is highest when the mother is HBeAg-positive.
  12. 12Tiki torch: HBV is grouped with the TORCHeS infections (T: Toxoplasma; O: Other, including varicella-zoster virus, parvovirus B19, Zika virus, HIV, HBV; R: Rubella; C: CMV; He: HSV; S: Syphilis), which can be transmitted vertically.
  13. 13Baby + 90% of liver cookie: Neonatal infection leads to chronic infection in about 90% of cases because of immune tolerance in the immature neonatal immune system. Chronic carriers have a high lifetime risk of cirrhosis and HCC.
  14. 14Adult + 10% of liver cookie: In adults, fewer than 10% (about 5%) develop chronic infection; most mount an effective immune response and clear the virus.
  15. 15Knee pain lines: Early (prodromal) HBV can cause a serum sickness-like syndrome from circulating immune complexes (type III hypersensitivity), with symmetric arthralgias or arthritis of small joints (hands, wrists, knees), often resolving when jaundice begins.
  16. 16Body henna: The same immune-complex process can cause a maculopapular or urticarial rash with fever and malaise, which may progress to a purpuric, non-blanching rash from small-vessel vasculitis.
  17. 17Red beads: HBV is associated with polyarteritis nodosa (PAN): necrotizing inflammation of medium-sized arteries, causing aneurysms with a beads-on-a-string appearance on angiography. Pulmonary vessels are spared.⭐ PAN: medium vessels, spares the lungs, hepatitis B association.
  18. 18Kidney box: HBV-associated PAN commonly involves the kidneys (renal arteries), causing reduced GFR, hypertension, microscopic hematuria, and proteinuria.
  19. 19Thinner glomerular knot: Chronic HBV can cause immune-complex glomerular disease. Membranous nephropathy shows a thickened glomerular basement membrane due to subepithelial immune complex deposits ('spike and dome' on silver stain), and presents as nephrotic syndrome.check original
  20. 20Thicker glomerular knot: Chronic HBV can also cause membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), with mesangial proliferation and expansion into the capillary walls, producing the classic 'tram-track' double-contour appearance due to immune complex deposition.check original
  21. 21Rising ALT volleyball: ALT rises during acute HBV and typically falls once the symptomatic phase passes; in viral hepatitis ALT is greater than AST. Acute-phase histology may show Councilman bodies (eosinophilic, apoptotic hepatocytes) and lobular inflammation.
  22. 22Baby + deflated ALT volleyball: Acute neonatal HBV often shows normal or only mildly elevated ALT because the immature immune system does not mount a strong response, which allows persistence (immune-tolerant phase).
  23. 23Antigen stickers on front of hippo van around 'S' & 'E': Know three antigens: HBsAg (surface; first marker of infection), HBcAg (core; found in infected hepatocytes, not detectable in serum, and targeted by anti-HBc antibodies), and HBeAg (marker of active replication and high infectivity).
  24. 24Spiky "S": HBsAg is the first serologic marker to appear (about 1-10 weeks after exposure) and indicates current infection, acute or chronic. Persistence for more than 6 months defines chronic infection.⭐ HBsAg > 6 months = chronic infection.
  25. 25Spiky "E": HBeAg is a secreted protein from the precore region and marks active viral replication and high infectivity. It is a marker of infectivity, and anti-HBe appears as replication declines.
  26. 26Flat tire under "S" and "E": In symptomatic acute infection, patients are HBsAg-positive and HBeAg-positive (active replication, high infectivity). Anti-HBc IgM is also present.
  27. 27"C" on window: In the window period, HBsAg has cleared and anti-HBs has not yet appeared, so anti-HBc IgM may be the only detectable marker. This is why anti-HBc IgM is key to diagnosing acute infection in this gap.⭐ Window period = IgM anti-HBc only.
  28. 28Smooth "E": Anti-HBe indicates declining viral replication and lower infectivity (HBeAg seroconversion).
  29. 29Smooth "S": Anti-HBs (surface antibody) signals recovery with immunity (with anti-HBc IgG) or vaccine-induced immunity.
  30. 30"S" line around syringe: Vaccinated people (recombinant HBsAg) are anti-HBs-positive only: negative HBsAg, anti-HBc, and anti-HBe. Anti-HBc positivity with anti-HBs implies natural infection, not vaccination.⭐ Vaccinated = anti-HBs alone.
  31. 31Serologic rainbow decal lines: Serologic timeline: HBsAg appears first, then HBeAg; anti-HBc IgM rises in acute infection (and fills the window period); anti-HBc IgG follows and persists for life; anti-HBe rises as HBeAg falls; anti-HBs appears last. Chronic infection: HBsAg and IgG anti-HBc persist, with no anti-HBs.
  32. 32Liver stone: Chronic HBV can lead to cirrhosis. Histology shows ground-glass hepatocytes (finely granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm) from cytoplasmic accumulation of HBsAg.
  33. 33Crab tarot card: HBV is a major risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which can occur even without cirrhosis because viral DNA integrates into the host genome. Screen chronic carriers with ultrasound and AFP.⭐ HBV can cause HCC without cirrhosis.
  34. 34Kid hippies in orange: Hepatitis D virus (HDV, delta agent) is a negative-sense RNA virus in the Deltaviridae (genus Deltavirus).
  35. 35Orange hippie clothing: HDV is an enveloped virus with a circular, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome; it is a defective (satellite) virus.
  36. 36Circular headbands: HDV has a small, circular ssRNA genome, the smallest of the human viruses. It encodes hepatitis delta antigen (HDAg).

12. Hepatitis B Viral Life Cycle (Hepadnaviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Entry via heparan sulfate proteoglycans and the NTCP receptor into hepatocytes.
  • Partially dsDNA is repaired to cccDNA in the nucleus: stable, transcribed by host RNA polymerase II, not eliminated by antivirals.
  • pgRNA serves as the mRNA for core and polymerase and as the template for reverse transcription inside the capsid.
  • HBsAg is made in excess as noninfectious subviral particles (vaccine antigen); virions bud with an HBsAg-containing envelope.
  • Reverse transcriptase is the target of lamivudine, entecavir, tenofovir; HDV borrows HBsAg for its envelope.
  1. 1Sticky entrance: HBV attaches to hepatocytes by binding heparan sulfate proteoglycans on the cell surface, then enters through the NTCP receptor (sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide), which explains its liver tropism.
  2. 2Magically repairing hula hoop: After entry the partially double-stranded DNA goes to the nucleus, where host enzymes repair it into covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA). cccDNA persists as a stable minichromosome, which is why antivirals rarely cure infection.
  3. 3Orange silly string: Host RNA polymerase II transcribes cccDNA into four main RNA transcripts, including the pregenomic RNA (pgRNA), the surface-protein mRNAs, and the X mRNA.
  4. 4Photo booth + photo strip: Two classes of RNAs matter most: subgenomic RNAs, which are translated into the surface proteins, and pgRNA, which is both the mRNA for core protein and polymerase and the template for the new viral genome. (Sketchy's legend attributes core and polymerase to subgenomic RNA; standard teaching assigns them to pgRNA.)check original
  5. 5Bee antenna: HBsAg is translated from the subgenomic (surface) mRNAs and processed in the ER and Golgi. Excess HBsAg is secreted alone as noninfectious subviral particles, which absorb neutralizing antibody and is the antigen used in the vaccine.
  6. 6Chain bib: HBcAg (core protein) is translated from pgRNA (per standard teaching) and forms the viral capsid. It remains within hepatocytes and is not detectable in serum.check original
  7. 7Reverso Transcriptum: Viral polymerase, which has reverse transcriptase activity, is also translated from pgRNA (per standard teaching). It is the target of nucleos(t)ide analogs.check original
  8. 8Chain link playpen + baby hippo bee: In the hepatocyte cytoplasm, pgRNA and the viral polymerase are encapsidated by core protein into an immature viral core.
  9. 9Reverso transcriptum + broken blue ring: Inside the capsid, reverse transcriptase copies pgRNA into DNA, forming the partially double-stranded circular genome. This RNA-to-DNA step is the target of lamivudine, entecavir, and tenofovir.
  10. 10Blue ring thrown on stage: Some immature cores deliver their new DNA to the nucleus, where it is converted to more cccDNA (amplifying the pool) and transcribed again.
  11. 11Hooded hippo bee: Mature cores acquire their envelope containing HBsAg by budding through host membranes (ER/Golgi) and are released as infectious virions (Dane particles).
  12. 12Delta satellites hijacking party: Hepatitis delta virus (HDV) hijacks the HBV surface antigens (HBsAg) as its own envelope, which it needs for entry, assembly, and exit from hepatocytes.

13. Hepatitis C Virus (Flaviviridae)

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: bloodborne (IV drug use, needlestick, transfusion before 1992); usually silent acute infection that becomes chronic in ~75-85%.
  • Dx: anti-HCV antibody screen, then HCV RNA PCR to confirm active infection; antibody can be negative early.
  • Complications: cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma; extrahepatic: mixed cryoglobulinemia, membranoproliferative GN, porphyria cutanea tarda.
  • Tx: direct-acting antivirals (e.g., sofosbuvir, an NS5B polymerase inhibitor) cure >95%; no vaccine.
  • Buzzwords: enveloped (+)ssRNA Flaviviridae (Hepacivirus); error-prone RdRp lacking proofreading means antigenic variation, so no vaccine and no lasting immunity.

  2. 2Five Flavors Forest Cafe: HCV belongs to the Flaviviridae family, along with dengue, yellow fever, Zika, and West Nile viruses. Unlike most of these, HCV is not arthropod-borne.
  3. 3Warm colors: HCV is an RNA virus with a non-segmented, single-stranded genome, so it does not undergo reassortment.
  4. 4Positive sun: HCV is positive-sense, so its genomic RNA acts directly as mRNA and is translated on entering the cell. Naked genomic RNA is therefore infectious.

  7. 7Rainbow robe: The varied (rainbow) envelope antigens reflect HCV's high mutation rate, which produces many quasispecies and multiple genotypes. Hypervariable envelope proteins allow immune escape and persistent infection.

  9. 9Red ink: Less commonly, HCV is spread by blood transfusion or organ transplant (rare since donor screening began in 1992), vertically from mother to child at birth, or by sharing contaminated tattoo or razor equipment. Sexual transmission is uncommon.

  11. 11Older hippo: Chronic HCV is often silent for years but causes progressive liver damage with fatigue, jaundice, and abdominal discomfort. Persistent inflammation drives fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis.

  14. 14Strandy orange seaweed: HCV RNA PCR (strands of RNA) is the confirmatory test for active infection. It also quantifies viral load, which is used to monitor treatment response and to determine the genotype.
  15. 15Hippo playing in orange seaweed: A positive HCV RNA test means active infection, because it directly detects viral genetic material in blood, showing that the virus is currently replicating and not just a past exposure.
  16. 16Hippo hiding in orange seaweed: A negative antibody with positive HCV RNA suggests very early (acute, window-period) infection before antibodies develop, or an immunocompromised patient who cannot mount a detectable antibody response. The hippo is hiding because the antibody test misses it.

  19. 19Animal Filming Permit: In chronic HCV, especially with cirrhosis, screen routinely for hepatocellular carcinoma with liver ultrasound plus serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), typically every 6 months.
  20. 20Megaphone: The megaphone represents AFP, the tumor marker announcing HCC. Elevated AFP with a liver mass in a patient with chronic HCV suggests HCC, which is a major reason for long-term surveillance even after viral cure of cirrhotic patients.

19. Bacillus anthracis & cereus

Exam snapshot
  • Anthrax: cutaneous (painless black eschar with edema), inhalational (woolsorters' disease with hemorrhagic mediastinitis and widened mediastinum), and a bioterrorism agent
  • B. anthracis: large gram-positive rod in chains (boxcar/bamboo), spore-forming, non-motile, with a unique poly-D-glutamate capsule
  • Toxin = protective antigen + edema factor (calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase, raises cAMP) and lethal factor (protease)
  • B. cereus: reheated fried rice, preformed toxin, vomiting in 1-6 h (self-limited)
  • Anthrax treatment: ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin or doxycycline; inhalational disease needs combination therapy and antitoxin
  1. 1King Anthra's axe: Bacillus anthracis, the cause of anthrax, is a large gram-positive spore-forming rod. It is non-motile and non-hemolytic on blood agar.
  2. 2Purple hues: B. anthracis and B. cereus are both gram-positive bacilli (purple on Gram stain).
  3. 3Ring of fire: Cutaneous anthrax (the most common form) begins as a painless papule or vesicle that undergoes central necrosis, leaving a black eschar with surrounding edema. It is typically painless, and with treatment mortality is low.⭐ Painless black eschar = cutaneous anthrax.
  4. 4Chain of ships: B. anthracis and B. cereus are gram-positive bacilli that grow in long chains (boxcar-shaped, bamboo-like in B. anthracis).
  5. 5"D" buckles + leather armor: B. anthracis has a unique capsule made of poly-gamma-D-glutamic acid (a polypeptide, not a polysaccharide). The capsule is antiphagocytic.⭐ Only bacterial capsule composed of D-amino acids (polypeptide).
  6. 6Aerobic bellows: B. anthracis and B. cereus are aerobic or facultatively anaerobic, which differentiates them from the obligate anaerobic Clostridium.
  7. 7Spored walnuts: Both organisms form endospores, which are metabolically inactive and resistant to heat, radiation, desiccation, and chemicals. Anthrax spores persist in soil for years, which makes them usable as a bioweapon.
  8. 8"EF" shield: Edema factor (EF) is a calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase that converts ATP to cAMP. Raised cAMP causes tissue edema and impairs neutrophil function, helping the infection become established.
  9. 9Camps: Edema factor (EF) raises intracellular cAMP (calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase), causing local edema and impaired phagocyte function. EF and LF enter cells through pores formed by protective antigen (PA); PA is the target of the anthrax vaccine.
  10. 10"LF" shield: Lethal factor (LF) is a zinc protease that cleaves MAP kinase kinases (MAPKK/MEK), disrupting MAPK signaling pathways and the host immune response, which leads to cell death.
  11. 11"LF" viking burning map: Lethal factor (LF) cleaves MAP kinase kinases, disrupting MAPK signaling and leading to impaired immunity, macrophage death, and shock. Together with EF, it is responsible for systemic toxicity.
  12. 12Sheep wool: Inhalational anthrax was historically called woolsorters' disease, from inhaling spores in animal wool, hides, and hair. It is now rare in developed countries but is a bioterrorism concern.
  13. 13Bloody axe: Inhalational anthrax causes hemorrhagic mediastinitis (and often hemorrhagic pleural effusions), followed by rapid sepsis and shock. It has high mortality, and some patients also develop hemorrhagic meningitis.
  14. 14Widened mast: Inhalational anthrax shows a widened mediastinum on chest X-ray, from hemorrhagic mediastinal lymphadenitis, with little true pneumonia.⭐ Widened mediastinum + wool/hide exposure or bioterror = inhalational anthrax.
  15. 15Flower shield: Treatment of anthrax is a fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) or doxycycline. Systemic disease needs combination therapy, and antitoxin monoclonal antibodies can be added.
  16. 16Bicycle tire shield: Post-exposure prophylaxis and treatment are with ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin or doxycycline (prolonged course, ~60 days after inhalational exposure), plus the anthrax vaccine.
  17. 17Rice bowl: Bacillus cereus is a gram-positive spore-forming rod that causes food poisoning, classically linked to rice dishes (fried rice).
  18. 18Vomiting: Emetic B. cereus food poisoning, from a preformed toxin, presents with nausea and vomiting about 1-6 h after ingestion, with abdominal cramps. It is self-limited, and a diarrheal form (toxin produced in the gut, onset 6-15 h) also exists.
  19. 19Reheating rice: Cooked rice left warm lets surviving heat-resistant spores germinate and produce a preformed heat-stable toxin, and reheating or flash-frying does not destroy it. Short incubation, so early-onset food poisoning.⭐ Reheated fried rice = B. cereus; mayo/custard = S. aureus.

20. Clostridium perfringens

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: dirty wound (trauma, war injury) with severe pain, crepitus, and rapidly spreading myonecrosis (gas gangrene)
  • Large gram-positive rod, spore-forming, anaerobic; box-car shaped; alpha toxin (lecithinase/phospholipase C) is the key virulence factor
  • Blood agar shows a double zone of hemolysis (inner theta toxin, outer alpha toxin)
  • Treatment: surgical debridement plus penicillin G (often with clindamycin)
  • Also causes food poisoning (meat/gravy; watery diarrhea, self-limited)
  1. 1Private Ringen: Clostridium perfringens is a large gram-positive rod, spore-forming and non-motile.
  2. 2Purple hues: C. perfringens is gram-positive (purple on Gram stain); it is a bacillus.
  3. 3Wounded soldier: C. perfringens infection occurs in wounds contaminated with soil or fecal matter, historically in battlefield injuries, and also with trauma, surgical wounds, or ischemic limbs.
  4. 4Spored walnuts: C. perfringens forms endospores, which are metabolically inactive and resistant to heat, radiation, and chemicals, allowing survival in harsh environments and in poorly cooked food.
  5. 5Soil: Spores persist in soil (and in the human and animal GI tract), resisting heat, radiation, desiccation, and disinfectants, which is how wounds and food get contaminated.
  6. 6Anaerobic gas mask: C. perfringens is an obligate (aerotolerant) anaerobe, so it grows in devitalized, poorly oxygenated tissue such as necrotic wounds.
  7. 7Gas vapor: C. perfringens causes necrotizing soft tissue infection with myonecrosis and gas formation (gas gangrene): severe pain, crepitus on palpation, foul discharge, and rapid spread with systemic toxicity.⭐ Crepitus + dirty wound = gas gangrene.
  8. 8Alpha flag + phospholipid clothespins: Alpha toxin is a lecithinase (phospholipase C) that cleaves lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) in cell membranes, causing myonecrosis and red cell hemolysis. Hemolysis and tissue destruction impair oxygen delivery.
  9. 9Lysed tomatoes: Alpha toxin (lecithinase) lyses red cells, causing intravascular hemolysis that impairs oxygen delivery to tissues; this contributes to anemia, jaundice, and renal failure in severe cases.
  10. 10"DOUBLE fine zone": On blood agar, C. perfringens produces a characteristic double zone of hemolysis: complete hemolysis in the inner zone (theta toxin) and incomplete hemolysis in the outer zone (alpha toxin).⭐ Double zone of hemolysis = C. perfringens.
  11. 11Purple pencil: Treatment is urgent surgical debridement (amputation if needed) plus antibiotics; penicillin G is preferred, usually with clindamycin (to suppress toxin production).
  12. 12"Slow" Sign in Muddy water: C. perfringens enterotoxin causes food poisoning from improperly cooked or stored foods (meats, gravy), with watery diarrhea and cramps and little vomiting or fever. Sketchy says onset is slow, within 2-12 h (classically 8-16 h); it is self-limited.⭐ Spores survive cooking, and enterotoxin is released in the gut during sporulation.

21. Escherichia coli (ETEC & EHEC)

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: UTI (cystitis/pyelonephritis), neonatal meningitis (K1), gram-negative sepsis; EHEC = bloody diarrhea + HUS; ETEC = traveler's watery diarrhea.
  • Lactose fermenter: pink on MacConkey, metallic green sheen on EMB; oxidase negative, indole positive.
  • EHEC O157:H7: sorbitol-negative (colorless on sorbitol-MacConkey), Shiga-like toxin (inactivates 60S ribosome), undercooked beef.
  • HUS triad: thrombocytopenia, MAHA with schistocytes, acute kidney injury; do NOT give antibiotics for EHEC.
  • ETEC: LT (like cholera, increases cAMP) and ST (increases cGMP) cause watery diarrhea; treat with fluids.
  1. 1E. cola: Escherichia coli is a gram-negative bacillus (pink rod on Gram stain) and a normal member of gut flora, but it is also the leading cause of several infections. It is a facultative anaerobe in the Enterobacteriaceae family.
  2. 2Red hues: Red/pink coloring reminds you that E. coli is gram-negative: it has a thin peptidoglycan layer with an outer membrane containing LPS (endotoxin), which stains pink with safranin counterstain.
  3. 3Pink milk carton: E. coli ferments lactose, forming pink colonies on MacConkey agar (acid from lactose fermentation turns the pH indicator pink). Other lactose fermenters include Klebsiella and Enterobacter.
  4. 4Glass capsule: Many strains of E. coli are surrounded by a polysaccharide capsule, which helps them evade phagocytosis.
  5. 5K cake + glass capsule: Some strains express K capsule (K antigen), a key virulence factor in extraintestinal infections (e.g., sepsis, meningitis, UTI) because it resists phagocytosis.
  6. 6Green coaster: E. coli typically forms distinctive metallic green colonies on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar; the metallic sheen is a classic buzzword for strong lactose fermentation.
  7. 7Catalase cat: E. coli is catalase positive: catalase converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, protecting the bacterium from oxidative killing. Catalase-positive organisms are particularly a problem in chronic granulomatous disease.
  8. 8Fimbriae ribbons: Fimbriae (pili) of E. coli mediate adhesion to host cells, an important virulence factor. P fimbriae bind uroepithelium and are linked to pyelonephritis, preventing the bacteria from being washed out by urine flow.
  9. 9"1" bladder cup: E. coli is the most common cause of urinary tract infections (cystitis and pyelonephritis), usually by ascending spread from the gut flora. It is the most common cause of uncomplicated UTI.
  10. 10Blood red shake: E. coli is a major cause of gram-negative sepsis. LPS (endotoxin, lipid A) triggers release of TNF-α and IL-1, leading to fever, hypotension and septic shock; urinary tract and GI/biliary sources are common.
  11. 11K cake + baby + meningitis helmet: Strains with the K1 capsular antigen cause neonatal meningitis, acquired from the maternal birth canal.⭐ Neonatal meningitis: think group B Strep, E. coli (K1), and Listeria.
  12. 12Hamburger: Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) is classically transmitted through undercooked ground beef (also unpasteurized juice and raw produce). The infectious dose is very low.⭐ Undercooked burger + bloody diarrhea = EHEC (O157:H7).
  13. 13Red stool: EHEC causes bloody diarrhea (hemorrhagic colitis) by damaging the intestinal lining through Shiga-like toxin production. Fever is typically absent or low-grade, and fecal leukocytes are scant.⭐ Antibiotics and antimotility agents are avoided in EHEC because they can raise the risk of HUS.
  14. 14Sorbitol free soda: O157:H7 does not ferment sorbitol, so it forms colorless colonies on sorbitol-MacConkey agar, distinguishing it from most other E. coli strains, which are sorbitol fermenters.
  15. 15She-gorilla toy: EHEC produces Shiga-like toxin (Shiga toxin 1/2, phage-encoded), nearly identical to the Shiga toxin of Shigella dysenteriae. It inactivates the 60S ribosomal subunit (cleaves 28S rRNA) in host cells, disrupting protein synthesis and killing the cell.
  16. 16Glomerular straw: Shiga-like toxin damages endothelial cells of glomerular capillaries and causes hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). This endothelial injury leads to acute kidney injury; HUS usually follows the diarrheal illness by about a week, mainly in children.
  17. 17Plate aggregation: Endothelial damage exposes the surface to platelets, triggering platelet aggregation and microthrombi. Platelet consumption causes thrombocytopenia (the classic HUS triad is thrombocytopenia, MAHA and acute kidney injury).⭐ HUS: low platelets but normal PT/PTT (unlike DIC); schistocytes on smear.
  18. 18Popped red balloon: In microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), red cells are mechanically sheared into schistocytes as they pass through small vessels filled with fibrin/platelet clots, lowering the red cell count. Labs show elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and a negative Coombs test.
  19. 19"Only $1.57": E. coli O157:H7 is the classic enterohemorrhagic serotype: Shiga-like toxin causes bloody diarrhea and HUS. The "$1.57" in the picture is a mnemonic for O157. Management is supportive (fluids); avoid antibiotics.
  20. 20Water truck: Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) is a common cause of traveler's diarrhea, acquired by ingesting contaminated water or food. It causes watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping, nausea and vomiting; it colonizes the small bowel via fimbriae (colonization factors) and does not invade.
  21. 21eL Agua: Heat-labile toxin (LT, mnemonic: eL = Labile) ADP-ribosylates Gs, increasing adenylyl cyclase activity, so cAMP rises, inhibiting sodium/chloride absorption and promoting chloride and water secretion, causing secretory (watery) diarrhea. It works like cholera toxin.
  22. 22San Gabriel: Heat-stable toxin (ST, mnemonic: San Gabriel = Stable, Guanylate) activates guanylate cyclase, raising cGMP, increasing chloride secretion and reducing sodium absorption, causing secretory (watery) diarrhea.⭐ LT = cAMP (like cholera); ST = cGMP. Remember: LT is Labile (heat-inactivated), ST is Stable.
  23. 23Spilling water: ETEC causes traveler's diarrhea: watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping, nausea and vomiting, with no blood and no significant inflammation. It is usually self-limited and treated with oral rehydration; antibiotics (e.g., rifaximin, azithromycin, fluoroquinolone) are used in more severe cases.

22. Vibrio spp

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: profuse rice-water diarrhea (cholera) from contaminated water in poor sanitation; dehydration, hypovolemic shock.
  • Comma-shaped, oxidase-positive gram-negative rods; grow on TCBS agar (yellow, sucrose-fermenting) and in alkaline media.
  • Cholera toxin permanently activates Gs (ADP-ribosylation), raising cAMP and opening CFTR, causing massive Cl-/Na+/water secretion.
  • Treatment: aggressive oral/IV rehydration; antibiotics (doxycycline or azithromycin) shorten severe disease.
  • V. parahaemolyticus: raw seafood gastroenteritis; V. vulnificus: raw oysters/seawater wound infections, hemorrhagic bullae, sepsis, especially in liver disease or iron overload.
  1. 1Red canoe: Vibrio species are comma-shaped (curved), gram-negative, oxidase-positive bacilli. They are motile by a single polar flagellum (darting motility).
  2. 2Blue rings: Vibrio are oxidase positive: they produce cytochrome c oxidase, an enzyme in the aerobic respiratory chain, which turns the oxidase reagent blue/purple. Oxidase positivity distinguishes Vibrio (and Campylobacter, Pseudomonas) from Enterobacteriaceae such as E. coli.
  3. 3Bags of rice: Vibrio cholerae causes profuse watery "rice-water" diarrhea (flecks of mucus in clear fluid) with no blood and little inflammation. Patients can lose liters per hour, leading to severe dehydration, hypovolemic shock and electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis).
  4. 4Outhouse: V. cholerae is transmitted fecal-orally via contaminated water or food, especially where sanitation is poor and during disasters or crowding. Humans and aquatic environments (shellfish, plankton) are the reservoirs.
  5. 5BASE: Vibrio grow preferentially in alkaline (basic) media, e.g., alkaline peptone water; the selective plate is TCBS agar, where V. cholerae forms yellow colonies (ferments sucrose).
  6. 6Yellow ducks with lemon shaped bodies: Vibrio are extremely acid-labile and are killed by stomach acid, so a high infectious dose is needed. Patients with reduced gastric acidity (e.g., PPI use, pernicious anemia, achlorhydria) are more susceptible.
  7. 7Raft rope attached to river shore: The toxin-coregulated pilus (TCP) of V. cholerae allows attachment to the intestinal mucosa (without invasion). It is also the receptor for the CTXφ phage that carries the cholera toxin genes.
  8. 8"Toxic" symbol: Cholera toxin (an A-B toxin) permanently activates Gs protein via ADP-ribosylation, overactivating adenylate cyclase and raising cAMP in intestinal epithelial cells, which activates CFTR channels and increases Cl- secretion into the lumen. The lumen becomes negatively charged, so Na+ follows the electrochemical gradient, and water follows osmotically, producing profuse watery rice-water diarrhea.
  9. 9GaS stove: The toxin permanently activates Gs (the stimulatory G protein), keeping adenylate cyclase on. Mechanism: Gs overactivation raises cAMP, activates CFTR, increases Cl- secretion, which draws Na+ and then water into the lumen, causing rice-water diarrhea.⭐ Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gs (locks it on); pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates Gi.
  10. 10cAMP: Elevated cAMP in intestinal epithelial cells activates protein kinase A and CFTR channels, causing chloride secretion into the lumen (cAMP is the central second messenger of the toxin). The lumen becomes negatively charged, Na+ follows, and water follows Na+ osmotically.
  11. 11Salty peanut water: The net result is massive loss of Cl- and Na+ with water into the lumen, producing profuse watery diarrhea; the stool is isotonic and electrolyte-rich. Chloride secretion drives the sodium and water loss, which causes dehydration, with hypokalemia and metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate loss).
  12. 12Drinking water: Oral rehydration is the primary treatment of cholera; it works because glucose-coupled Na+ uptake (SGLT1) remains intact. Antibiotics (doxycycline, azithromycin) and IV fluids are reserved for severe cases.⭐ Treat cholera by replacing fluids; the toxin effect is irreversible, so symptoms last until epithelial cells turn over.
  13. 13Eating oysters: V. parahaemolyticus and V. vulnificus are marine bacteria that infect humans through ingestion of contaminated seafood, especially raw oysters, or via open wounds exposed to seawater. V. parahaemolyticus is the most common food-related Vibrio infection in the United States, typically causing self-limited gastroenteritis. V. vulnificus is the most deadly Vibrio species, causing severe wound infections and sepsis with fever, chills, hypotension, and large fluid-filled bullae that can become necrotic and progress to necrotizing fasciitis.⭐ V. vulnificus is worst in patients with liver disease (cirrhosis) or iron overload (hemochromatosis); treat with doxycycline plus a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftazidime) and surgical debridement.

23. Campylobacter jejuni

Exam snapshot

  • Curved (comma/S or seagull-wing), oxidase-positive gram-negative rods; microaerophilic and grow at 42°C on Campylobacter (Skirrow) agar.

  2. 2Red rod-shaped cooler: C. jejuni is a gram-negative bacillus (pink rods on Gram stain), with a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane containing LPS. It is motile with polar flagella.
  3. 3Fire: C. jejuni is thermophilic: it grows optimally at about 42°C (selective culture is incubated at 42°C under microaerophilic conditions on Campylobacter agar). The heat explains its association with the intestinal tract of warm-bodied birds (poultry body temperature is about 41-42°C).⭐ Remember Campylobacter grows at 42°C, microaerophilic, on Campy (Skirrow) agar.

  5. 5Red stool: Symptoms include cramping, abdominal pain, fever, and diarrhea, which is often bloody. If bacteremia occurs, patients may develop systemic signs such as high fever, chills, malaise, hypotension, and confusion. The illness is usually self-limited (about a week); treat supportively, with a macrolide such as azithromycin for severe disease.
  6. 6Curved mustache: C. jejuni is a curved bacillus: comma-shaped or S-shaped, often described as a "seagull wing" on microscopy, with darting corkscrew motility.
  7. 7Blue oxidase ring: C. jejuni is oxidase positive: it produces oxidase, an enzyme in the aerobic respiratory chain. Together with the curved shape, this resembles Vibrio, but Campylobacter is microaerophilic and grows at 42°C. It is also catalase positive.

25. Clostridioides difficile

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: hospital-acquired watery diarrhea after antibiotics (clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, penicillins) disrupting normal flora.
  • Gram-positive, spore-forming, obligate anaerobic rod; spores resist heat, alcohol-based sanitizers, and drying.
  • Toxin A (enterotoxin) and Toxin B (cytotoxin; depolymerizes actin) cause pseudomembranous colitis with yellow-grey plaques.
  • Diagnosis: stool NAAT/PCR for toxin genes or EIA for toxins A/B (often GDH + toxin algorithm).
  • Treatment: oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin first-line; IV metronidazole only adjunctive in fulminant disease (with PO/PR vancomycin).
  • Prevention: soap-and-water handwashing (not alcohol gel), contact precautions, stop the offending antibiotic.
  1. 1Walnuts: Spores are metabolically inactive and highly resistant to heat, radiation, and chemical agents (including alcohol-based hand sanitizers). They persist on hospital surfaces and drive transmission.⭐ Alcohol gel does not kill spores; use soap and water.
  2. 2"Clean" sign with washing hands over chocolate machine: C. difficile is primarily nosocomial; spores spread patient to patient via contaminated hands and surfaces (fecal-oral). Antibiotics, especially clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, and penicillins, disrupt normal gut flora, letting C. difficile overgrow and produce toxins that cause diarrhea.
  3. 3Workstation A and workstation B: C. difficile produces two major exotoxins, Toxin A (TcdA) and Toxin B (TcdB), which are responsible for disease. Both are glucosyltransferases that inactivate Rho GTPases in colonocytes.
  4. 4"A - Apples" sign: Toxin A is the enterotoxin: it binds the brush border of intestinal epithelial cells, causing fluid secretion, inflammation, and mucosal damage with watery diarrhea.
  5. 5Employee picking up the apple and brushing chocolate on them: Toxin A acts on the intestinal lining (apples brushed with chocolate): epithelial cell damage and inflammation lead to cell death and secretory watery diarrhea.
  6. 6Chocolate filled machine: Toxin A is mainly enterotoxic: it drives fluid secretion and mucosal injury, producing the watery diarrhea of early disease.
  7. 7"B - Black Licorice" sign: Toxin B is the cytotoxin: it depolymerizes actin filaments in enterocytes, causing cell rounding and death.   8. 8Black Licorice: Toxin B (black licorice) disrupts the enterocyte actin cytoskeleton by depolymerizing actin, causing cell rounding and death.
  9. 9Little kid chewing them apart: Toxin B damage to the cytoskeleton (kid chewing licorice apart) causes enterocyte rounding, loss of the cell layer, and mucosal necrosis.
  10. 10Worker packing the licorice in sheets of yellowish plastic: Mucosal necrosis from toxin B yields the yellowish-grey pseudomembrane (yellow plastic sheets) of fibrin, mucus, and dead cells that defines pseudomembranous colitis. Severe disease can progress to toxic megacolon.

  12. 12Gas mask: C. difficile is an obligate anaerobe (cannot grow in oxygen-rich environments), consistent with infection in the anaerobic colon.
  13. 13Violet: C. difficile is a gram-positive bacillus (violet on Gram stain) and a spore former.
  14. 14Van with the mouth like opening: Oral vancomycin is first-line therapy (fidaxomicin is an equally accepted first-line option). It stays in the gut lumen with minimal systemic absorption, directly reaching bacteria and toxins.⭐ IV vancomycin does not reach the colonic lumen and is useless for C. difficile.
  15. 15Train: IV metronidazole is used for severe or fulminant infection alongside vancomycin, as it achieves systemic levels that reach inflamed colonic tissue, complementing local gut-directed therapy.

26. Salmonella Intro to Salmonella & NTS

Exam snapshot
  • Gram-negative, motile (peritrichous flagella), facultative intracellular rod; H2S producer (black colonies on Hektoen agar), non-lactose fermenter.
  • NTS (S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium) acquired from poultry, eggs, and reptiles/amphibians (turtles).
  • Usual presentation: self-limited gastroenteritis (fever, cramps, diarrhea); no antibiotics, supportive care only.
  • Bacteremia risk and osteomyelitis in sickle cell disease (also young infants, elderly, immunocompromised): treat with fluoroquinolone or ceftriaxone.
  • Invasion via M cells of Peyer's patches; low gastric acid (PPIs, antacids) lowers the infectious dose.
  1. 1Red rod salmon: Salmonella is a gram-negative rod. It is a non-lactose fermenter in the Enterobacterales and ferments glucose.
  2. 2Swimming salmon: Salmonella is motile via peritrichous flagella (H antigen), unlike Shigella, which is non-motile.⭐ Salmonella motile, Shigella non-motile.
  3. 3Fishbowl capsule: Salmonella produces a protective polysaccharide capsule (as given in the Sketchy picture). The best-known capsule is the Vi antigen of S. Typhi, which helps evade host defenses; most NTS lack it.
  4. 4Open cage: Salmonella is a facultative intracellular organism: it can survive and replicate inside host macrophages as well as outside cells.
  5. 5Salmo crossing M window: Invasive serotypes (all typhoidal and some NTS) cross the intestinal barrier via enterocytes and M cells, the specialized epithelial cells overlying lymphoid tissue of Peyer's patches.
  6. 6Lemons: Salmonella is acid-labile, so gastric acid is a major host defense. Low-acid states (PPIs, antacids, achlorhydria) reduce the infectious dose.
  7. 7Farting salmon: Salmonella produces hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which gives black colonies on certain media (e.g., hydrogen sulfide-detecting agars).
  8. 8Black plates: H2S production gives black colonies on Hektoen enteric agar, where Salmonella is distinguished from Shigella (colorless/green colonies, no H2S).
  9. 9"Thai Food": Typhoidal Salmonella (TS) serotypes are S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi, which cause enteric (typhoid) fever.
  10. 10"Auntie Rita's" Turmeric: Nontyphoidal Salmonella (NTS) comprises the many serotypes other than S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi; S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium are the most common.
  11. 11Smorgasbord: NTS causes foodborne and waterborne illness, spread through a wide variety of fresh and processed foods.
  12. 12Chicken and eggs: NTS is zoonotic; poultry (chicken, eggs) are the primary reservoir, so undercooked poultry and raw eggs are classic sources.
  13. 13Turtle handler: Animal contact, especially reptiles and amphibians (e.g., pet turtles), can transmit NTS.
  14. 14Queasy kid with flame bandana: The most common presentation of NTS is self-limited gastroenteritis: fever, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea (can be bloody and inflammatory).
  15. 15Red river: NTS can cause extraintestinal bloodstream infection (bacteremia), leading to severe sepsis or death, especially in the immunocompromised, infants, and elderly.
  16. 16Aorta tree: Bacteremic NTS can disseminate, causing endovascular infection (e.g., infected aortic aneurysm/atherosclerotic plaques), meningitis, and osteomyelitis.
  17. 17Glowing brain tree: Disseminated NTS can seed the CNS and cause meningitis, particularly in infants and immunocompromised patients.
  18. 18Fish bones: Disseminated NTS can seed bone and cause osteomyelitis.
  19. 19Sickling guy: Osteomyelitis is a classic complication of invasive NTS in patients with sickle cell disease (functional asplenia) and other underlying conditions.⭐ Sickle cell + osteomyelitis: Salmonella and S. aureus.
  20. 20"Don't touch" Abx: NTS gastroenteritis is self-limited; manage with supportive care and hydration. Antibiotics are generally not indicated, and may prolong carriage.
  21. 21Flowers: Fluoroquinolones are the preferred antibiotic for NTS when treatment is indicated (to prevent invasive disease in high-risk patients and to treat invasive infection).
  22. 223 axes: Third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone) are an alternative for NTS, used to prevent invasive disease when indicated and to treat invasive infection, including in children.

27. Salmonella Typhoidal Salmonella

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: traveler to South-Central/East Asia or Southern Africa with stepwise fever, relative bradycardia, rose spots, and abdominal pain.
  • S. Typhi/Paratyphi: humans are the only reservoir; fecal-oral spread; chronic gallbladder carriers (Typhoid Mary).
  • Vi capsule blunts neutrophil response; bacteria multiply inside macrophages of Peyer's patches.
  • Week 3 complications: sepsis, hepatosplenomegaly, intestinal bleeding, and perforation.
  • Treatment: antibiotics (typically a fluoroquinolone; ceftriaxone or azithromycin where resistance exists).
  • Vaccines: oral live attenuated (Ty21a), injectable Vi polysaccharide, and Vi-conjugate (to tetanus toxoid) vaccine.
  1. 1Thai Food sign: S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi are the typhoidal Salmonella (TS) serotypes; they cause enteric (typhoid) fever in humans.
  2. 2Parrot: S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi are the typhoidal Salmonella (TS) serotypes, which cause enteric (typhoid) fever.
  3. 3"No animals" sign: Humans are the exclusive reservoir of TS; there is no animal reservoir, unlike NTS.⭐ Humans only: TS vs. poultry/reptiles for NTS.
  4. 4Outhouse: TS is acquired by consuming food or water contaminated with human feces (fecal-oral route), so poor sanitation drives spread.
  5. 5Parrots in papaya tree: TS can colonize the gallbladder, leading to a chronic, asymptomatic carrier state that sheds bacteria in feces and perpetuates transmission.⭐ Gallstones raise carrier risk; chronic carriage predisposes to gallbladder cancer.
  6. 6Thai mountains: TS predominantly occurs in developing countries with poor sanitation; endemic regions include South-Central and East Asia and Southern Africa.
  7. 7Vi-olet punch: TS possesses the Vi polysaccharide capsular antigen, which helps resist host defenses by blunting the neutrophilic response (inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis, opsonization, and oxidative burst).
  8. 8MacroCAGE: TS triggers a monocytic rather than neutrophilic response: monocytes/macrophages are recruited to sites harboring the bacteria, such as Peyer's patches.
  9. 9Caged parrots: TS survives and extensively proliferates within macrophages, which carry the organism through the reticuloendothelial system.
  10. 10Flaming lanterns: Fever is a classic early sign of TS infection and the hallmark of enteric fever.
  11. 11Stepwise rock island: Enteric fever begins with a stepwise rising fever that reaches a high plateau, typically over the first week.
  12. 12Fallen heart watch: Relative bradycardia (pulse lower than expected for the degree of fever, Faget sign) is a characteristic feature of enteric fever, typically developing in the first week.
  13. 13Red algae: Flat, rose-colored macules ("rose spots") may develop on the chest and abdomen, typically during the second week. Constipation, headache, and abdominal pain are also common.
  14. 14Dugong with liver and spleen spots: If untreated, sepsis, hepatosplenomegaly, and intestinal bleeding and perforation may develop in the third week.
  15. 15Leaking intestine engine: Peyer's patch necrosis from the monocytic infiltrate causes intestinal bleeding and perforation in the third week of untreated enteric fever.⭐ Typhoid perforation typically involves the terminal ileum.
  16. 16Flowers: Enteric fever requires antibiotic treatment, typically a fluoroquinolone (ceftriaxone or azithromycin where resistance is common).
  17. 17"LIVE music" sign + syringe mic: A live attenuated oral vaccine (Ty21a) protects against enteric fever. It is contraindicated in immunocompromised patients and cannot be given with concurrent antibiotics.
  18. 18Skull and Syringe shirt + Vi-olet punch: The Vi capsular polysaccharide vaccine is given by injection to protect against enteric fever; it is a purified polysaccharide vaccine (T-cell independent).
  19. 19Rhesus + Vi-olet punch: The conjugate vaccine (Vi capsular polysaccharide linked to tetanus toxoid protein) is given by injection and gives more robust, T-cell dependent protection against enteric fever.

28. Shigella spp

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: bloody (dysenteric) diarrhea with fever and abdominal cramps, with fecal leukocytes; very low infectious dose (10-100 organisms), spread person to person fecal-oral.
  • Gram-negative, non-motile, non-lactose-fermenting, non-H2S-producing rod (colorless/green colonies on Hektoen agar, unlike black Salmonella).
  • Invades M cells and spreads cell to cell via actin rockets; Type III secretion system injects effector proteins.
  • Shiga toxin (S. dysenteriae type 1) inactivates 60S ribosomal subunit (28S rRNA) and causes HUS (anemia, thrombocytopenia, AKI).
  • Treatment: fluoroquinolone or azithromycin (ceftriaxone), but mainly supportive; avoid antimotility drugs.
  • Buzzwords: day-care outbreaks, men who have sex with men, travelers, and HUS in children.
  1. 1She-gorilla: Shigella spp. are gram-negative bacilli and belong to the Enterobacterales; they are non-lactose fermenters.
  2. 2Red hues: Shigella spp. are gram-negative bacilli (red/pink on Gram stain).
  3. 3Green tutu: On Hektoen enteric agar, Shigella forms blue-green (colorless) colonies because it does not produce H2S. Salmonella makes black colonies on HE due to H2S.
  4. 4Chained she-gorilla: Shigella spp. are non-motile (no flagella), unlike Salmonella, which is motile.⭐ Shigella non-motile, Salmonella motile.
  5. 5Protected from acid: Shigella is relatively resistant to gastric acid, so very small inocula (10-100 organisms) can cause infection, which facilitates person-to-person spread.
  6. 6Motion tail: Shigella induces apoptosis in host cells and polymerizes host actin into tails ("actin rockets") to move through the cytoplasm and spread cell to cell, making it a facultative intracellular bacterium.
  7. 7"M" pad: Shigella invades M cells overlying Peyer's patches of the gut mucosa, then infects the colonic epithelium from the basolateral side.
  8. 8Red stool: The main sign of shigellosis is diarrhea, which is often bloody (dysentery), usually with fever and abdominal cramps.
  9. 9Inflammatory flames: Shigellosis diarrhea is inflammatory, with leukocytes (and often blood) in stool, reflecting invasion of the colonic mucosa.
  10. 10Glomerular knot: In rare cases, Shiga toxin from S. dysenteriae type 1 causes hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) by damaging endothelial cells of glomerular capillaries. This triggers platelet aggregation, causing thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), and acute kidney injury.⭐ HUS triad: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI.
  11. 11Popped red balloon: In MAHA (part of HUS), red blood cells are mechanically sheared into schistocytes as they pass through small vessels occluded by platelet-rich clots, lowering the RBC count.
  12. 12Aggregation of plates: In HUS, platelet aggregation in damaged glomerular capillaries consumes platelets (thrombocytopenia) and impairs renal function, causing acute kidney injury.
  13. 13Young ringmaster: HUS occurs more commonly in children under 5 years of age.
  14. 1460S sand timer: Shiga toxin of S. dysenteriae (similar to the Shiga-like toxin of enterohemorrhagic E. coli O157:H7) inactivates the 60S ribosomal subunit by removing an adenine from 28S rRNA, inhibiting protein synthesis.
  15. 15Turkey baster: Shigella has a type III secretion system, a needle-like apparatus that injects effector proteins into host cells, helping the bacteria invade, evade immune responses, and survive intracellularly.
