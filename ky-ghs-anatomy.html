1. Introduction to Abdomen

The abdomen is the portion of the trunk between the thorax and the pelvis. It is not a closed cylinder but a dynamic space defined by rigid bony boundaries above and below, and muscular walls laterally. Its internal content — the majority of the digestive and urogenital systems — is mapped using two complementary grid systems: the nine-region system for precise anatomical localisation and the four-quadrant system for rapid clinical communication.

1.1 Anatomical Boundaries

The superior boundary is the inferior thoracic aperture, sealed by the diaphragm. Inferiorly, the abdominal cavity is continuous with the pelvic cavity at the pelvic inlet (superior pelvic aperture). The posterior boundary is formed by the vertebral column with quadratus lumborum, psoas major, and iliacus muscles on each side. The anterior wall is the rectus abdominis, and the lateral walls are the three flat muscles. Key palpable bony landmarks for physical examination are the ASIS, pubic tubercle, and pubic symphysis.

1.2 Nine-Region System

Four planes create the nine-region grid: two vertical midclavicular planes (mid-clavicle to midinguinal point), a horizontal subcostal plane (inferior border of the 10th costal cartilage), and a transtubercular plane (through the iliac tubercles and the body of L5). The clinically vital transpyloric plane (lower border of L1) cuts through the pylorus, neck of pancreas, hilum of both kidneys, and the SMA origin.

Region Key Contents Clinical Pathologies
Right Hypochondriac Liver (R lobe), gallbladder, right kidney, small intestine Hepatitis, gallstones, cholecystitis, liver abscess
Epigastric Stomach, liver (L lobe), pancreas, duodenum, spleen, adrenal glands Peptic ulcer, oesophagitis, pancreatitis, gastric cancer
Left Hypochondriac Spleen, colon (splenic flexure), left kidney, pancreas (tail) Splenic abscess, rupture, infarct
Right Lumbar (Flank) Gallbladder, liver, ascending colon, right kidney Renal colic, pyelonephritis
Umbilical Umbilicus, small intestine, duodenum, transverse colon Early appendicitis, mesenteric adenitis, Meckel's diverticulitis
Left Lumbar (Flank) Descending colon, left kidney Renal colic, pyelonephritis

Hypogastric (Pubic) Urinary bladder, sigmoid colon, female reproductive organs UTI, urinary retention, testicular torsion

Several of the organs listed in the nine-region table are retroperitoneal — positioned posterior to the parietal peritoneum against the posterior abdominal wall, covered by peritoneum on their anterior surface only. Knowing these structures is clinically essential: retroperitoneal pathology (bleeding, inflammation, abscess) does not freely communicate with the peritoneal cavity, making signs more subtle and delayed than with intraperitoneal pathology.

SAD PUCKER — retroperitoneal structures:
  • S — Suprarenal (adrenal) glands
  • A — Aorta and inferior vena cava
  • D — Duodenum (D2–D4; the first 2.5 cm "cap" is intraperitoneal)
  • P — Pancreas (head, neck, and body; the tail is intraperitoneal within the splenorenal ligament)
  • U — Ureters
  • C — Colon — ascending and descending (secondarily retroperitoneal)
  • K — Kidneys
  • E — (o)Esophagus — abdominal segment
  • R — Rectum (upper two-thirds; lower rectum is primarily retroperitoneal, i.e., never had a mesentery)

1.3 Four-Quadrant System

The vertical median plane and a horizontal transumbilical plane (through the L3–L4 disc) intersect at the umbilicus to create four quadrants used for rapid clinical communication, operative notes, and imaging reports.

1.4 Referred Pain — Embryological Basis

Visceral pain signals are conducted via bilateral sympathetic afferents and perceived at the somatic dermatomes sharing the same spinal cord segments as the developing gut segment. This produces predictable midline pain that shifts to somatic (parietal) pain only when the overlying parietal peritoneum becomes inflamed.

Visceral Pain Referral by Gut Origin:
  • Foregut (esophagus → duodenum D2, liver, pancreas, spleen) → Epigastric region
  • Midgut (distal D2 → proximal 2/3 transverse colon, appendix) → Umbilical region
  • Hindgut (distal 1/3 transverse colon → rectum) → Hypogastric (pubic) region
Classic exam pattern: appendicitis begins as periumbilical pain (midgut visceral referral via T10) then localises to McBurney's point as parietal peritoneum becomes inflamed.

1.5 Anterior Abdominal Wall — Layers and Muscles

The anterior abdominal wall is a layered "sandwich" that protects the viscera while allowing significant changes in intra-abdominal pressure for micturition, defecation, vomiting, parturition, and forced expiration. Understanding its fascial planes is essential because they dictate how fluid (urine, blood, infection) spreads following trauma.

  1. Skin
  2. Superficial fascia (subcutaneous tissue)
  3. Deep fascia (investing fascia)
  4. Muscles
  5. Extraperitoneal fascia (containing extraperitoneal fat)
  6. Parietal peritoneum

1.6 Superficial Fascia — Camper's and Scarpa's

Below the umbilicus, the superficial fascia bifurcates into two named layers with distinct clinical behavior. Camper's fascia is the fatty layer; it continues over the inguinal ligament into the thigh's fascia lata, and in males loses its fat over the penis to become the smooth-muscle-containing Dartos fascia of the scrotum. Scarpa's fascia is a thin, membranous layer that attaches firmly to the fascia lata just inferior to the inguinal ligament, and continues into the perineum as Colles' fascia.

Urinary Extravasation — Fascial Boundaries: Rupture of the bulbar urethra tears Buck's fascia, allowing urine into the superficial perineal space. Because Scarpa's fascia attaches firmly to the fascia lata, urine is excluded from the thighs and instead spreads: into the scrotum (deep to Dartos), around the penis, and superiorly into the lower anterior abdominal wall deep to Scarpa's fascia.

1.7 Muscles of the Anterior Abdominal Wall

Three flat muscles with obliquely crossing fibres provide lateral and rotational support. Two vertical muscles (rectus abdominis and pyramidalis) run along the midline, encased in the rectus sheath.

Key Fibrous Structures:
  • Inguinal (Poupart) Ligament: The folded inferior border of the external oblique aponeurosis — spans ASIS to pubic tubercle.
  • Linea Alba: Tendinous midline raphe from xiphoid to pubic symphysis. Becomes pigmented in pregnancy (Linea Nigra).
  • Conjoint Tendon (Falx Inguinalis): Union of internal oblique and transversus abdominis aponeuroses; reinforces the medial posterior wall of the inguinal canal.

1.8 Rectus Sheath — The Arcuate Line Rule

The rectus sheath is an aponeurotic container formed by contributions from all three flat muscles. Its arrangement changes critically at the arcuate line (midway between umbilicus and pubic symphysis), which marks the inferior limit of the posterior sheath.

Level Anterior Sheath Posterior Sheath
Above costal margin External oblique aponeurosis only None (muscle lies on costal cartilages)
Costal margin → Arcuate line External oblique + anterior layer of internal oblique Posterior layer of internal oblique + transversus abdominis
Below arcuate line All three aponeuroses pass anterior to rectus None — rectus lies directly on transversalis fascia

1.9 Neurovascular Architecture

Segmental thoracic nerves provide both motor supply to the flat muscles and cutaneous sensation in a predictable dermatomal pattern. The umbilicus serves as a watershed for both venous drainage and lymphatics.

Nerve Level Cutaneous Territory
T7–T9 Xiphoid to just above umbilicus
T10 Umbilicus (exact level)
T11, T12, L1 Below umbilicus to pubic region
Umbilicus as Clinical Watershed:
  • Venous drainage above umbilicus → axillary vein (via lateral thoracic vein)
  • Venous drainage below umbilicus → femoral vein (via superficial epigastric vein)
  • Portal connection: Paraumbilical veins link both systems to the portal vein → dilate as Caput Medusae in portal hypertension
  • Lymphatics above umbilicus → axillary nodes; below → superficial inguinal nodes

1.10 Iliopectineal Arcus — Space Deep to Inguinal Ligament

The iliopectineal arcus divides the space posterior to the inguinal ligament into two compartments that transmit distinct structures.

Compartment Position Contents
Muscular lacuna Lateral Iliopsoas muscle
Vascular lacuna Medial Femoral sheath, femoral vessels, femoral branch of genitofemoral nerve

2. Abdomen — Inguinal Region

The inguinal canal is an oblique, intramuscular passage approximately 4 cm long in adults, directed inferomedially through the lower anterolateral abdominal wall. It is the direct anatomical consequence of gonadal descent during embryological development — the testes pass through this passage between weeks 9 and 12, dragging peritoneal (processus vaginalis) and fascial coverings along with them. Because this passage represents a structural weakness, inguinal hernias are the most common hernia, occurring overwhelmingly more in males (86%).

2.1 Boundaries of the Inguinal Canal

Wall Structure
Anterior wall Aponeurosis of external oblique (entire length)
Posterior wall Transversalis fascia — reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon (inguinal falx)
Superior wall (roof) Internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles (arch over the canal)
Inferior wall (floor) Inguinal ligament

2.2 Rings of the Canal

The canal opens at two ends. The deep (internal) inguinal ring is an evagination of the transversalis fascia, located superior to the midpoint of the inguinal ligament and lateral to the inferior epigastric artery. The superficial (external) inguinal ring is a triangular gap in the external oblique aponeurosis, located superior to the pubic tubercle, with a medial crus, lateral crus, and apex directed superolaterally.

2.3 Contents of the Inguinal Canal

In males, the canal transmits the spermatic cord. In females, it transmits the round ligament of the uterus and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (both sexes).

2.4 Spermatic Cord — Fascial Layers and Contents

As the testis descends, it picks up three concentric fascial sleeves derived from the corresponding layers of the abdominal wall.

Fascial Layer Abdominal Wall Derivative
External spermatic fascia External oblique aponeurosis / fascia
Cremasteric fascia Internal oblique fascia
Internal spermatic fascia Transversalis fascia
Spermatic Cord Contents — "Partying Doesn't Contribute To A Good Social Life":
  • Pampiniform plexus (8–12 veins)
  • Ductus deferens
  • Cremasteric artery (from inferior epigastric)
  • Testicular artery (from abdominal aorta, below renal arteries)
  • Artery of the ductus deferens (from inferior vesical artery)
  • Genital branch of genitofemoral nerve (motor to cremaster)
  • Sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve fibers
  • Lymphatic vessels (drain to lumbar/para-aortic nodes)
  • Plus: vestige of the processus vaginalis

2.5 Direct vs. Indirect Inguinal Hernia

The relationship of the hernia sac to the inferior epigastric artery is the definitive anatomical discriminator between the two types. The inferior epigastric artery forms the lateral boundary of Hesselbach's triangle (inguinal triangle).

Feature Indirect Inguinal Hernia Direct Inguinal Hernia
Origin Congenital (patent processus vaginalis) Acquired (posterior wall weakness)
Entry point Deep inguinal ring — lateral to inferior epigastric artery Directly through the posterior wall (conjoint tendon) — medial to inferior epigastric artery
Hesselbach's triangle Lateral to triangle Within the triangle
Reduction control Reduced and controlled by pressure over deep ring Reduced but not controlled by pressure over deep ring alone; controlled at superficial ring

Hesselbach's Triangle boundaries: lateral — inferior epigastric artery; medial — lateral border of rectus abdominis; inferior — inguinal ligament.

Hernia Exam Pearls:
  • Indirect is the most common hernia overall; most common in young males
  • Femoral hernia passes below the inguinal ligament through the femoral canal — more common in females and at higher risk of strangulation
  • Spigelian hernia: protrudes at the junction of the linea semilunaris and the arcuate line; peaks at age 50, more common on the right
  • Cremasteric reflex: afferent — ilioinguinal nerve (L1); efferent — genital branch of genitofemoral nerve (motor to cremaster)

2.6 Scrotum and Testis — Clinical Anatomy

The scrotum maintains testicular temperature below core body temperature via its fat-free Dartos wall. Scrotal innervation is divided: the anterior one-third is supplied by the ilioinguinal nerve (L1), and the posterior two-thirds by the pudendal nerve (S3). Lymphatic drainage of the testis goes to lumbar/para-aortic nodes (not the groin), which is why early testicular cancer produces no palpable inguinal lymphadenopathy.

Varicocele: Dilated pampiniform plexus producing a "bag of worms" appearance. 99% occur on the left because the left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a 90-degree angle, increasing venous back-pressure. The right testicular vein enters the IVC at an acute angle, allowing easier drainage.
Hydrocele: Excess fluid in a persistent processus vaginalis, surrounding the testis (hydrocele of testis) or confined to the cord (hydrocele of cord). Diagnosed by transillumination — the fluid-filled sac transmits light; a solid mass (tumor) does not.

3. Esophagus and Stomach

The abdominal segment of the esophagus is the shortest and clinically most critical portion of the organ. Measuring only approximately 1.25 cm, it passes through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm at T10 and terminates at the esophagogastric junction at T11. Despite its brevity, it is the primary anti-reflux zone and the site of important portosystemic anastomoses.

3.1 Structural Anchoring

The phrenicoesophageal ligament (with superior and inferior segments) anchors the esophagus to the margins of the diaphragmatic hiatus. This attachment allows the diaphragm and esophagus to move independently during respiration and swallowing while maintaining the integrity of the gastro-esophageal barrier.

3.2 Z-Line — Endoscopic Landmark

The esophagogastric junction is marked internally by the irregular Z-line (squamocolumnar junction), visible on endoscopy as an abrupt transition from the stratified squamous epithelium of the esophagus to the simple columnar epithelium of the gastric mucosa.

3.3 Innervation

The right and left vagus nerves form the esophageal plexus around the lower thoracic esophagus. As they descend through the esophageal hiatus, they reorganise into the anterior gastric trunk (predominantly left vagus) and posterior gastric trunk (predominantly right vagus) to supply the stomach.

Barrett's Esophagus: Chronic GERD causes metaplastic replacement of the normal stratified squamous epithelium with intestinal-type columnar epithelium. This is a precancerous lesion — Barrett's is a high-risk precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma and requires regular endoscopic surveillance with biopsies.
Esophageal Varices: The lower third of the esophagus is a site of portosystemic anastomosis between the left gastric vein (portal) and azygos vein (systemic). Portal hypertension (most commonly from cirrhosis) causes these channels to dilate into fragile, thin-walled varices. Rupture produces massive upper GI hemorrhage — a leading cause of death in cirrhosis.
Hiatal Hernias — Two Types:
  • Sliding (Type I): The abdominal esophagus, cardia, and part of the fundus slide superiorly through the hiatus. Most common type; associated with GERD.
  • Paraesophageal (Type II): The cardia remains in place but a peritoneal pouch (often containing fundus) herniates through the hiatus alongside the esophagus. Higher risk of strangulation — the key distinguishing danger.

4. Abdomen — Stomach Relations

The stomach is the most dilated segment of the GI tract, acting as a reservoir for mechanical and chemical digestion. It occupies the epigastric, umbilical, and left hypochondrium regions, typically assuming a J-shaped morphology. Its fundus reaches the level of the 5th intercostal space — the anatomical basis for why acute gastric distension or irritation can mimic angina pectoris.

4.1 Gross Morphological Divisions

  • Cardia: Region immediately distal to the esophageal orifice
  • Fundus: Dome-shaped superior portion above the horizontal plane of the cardia
  • Body: Primary central region from fundus to pyloric antrum
  • Pylorus: Distal outflow tract — pyloric antrum → sulcus intermedius → pyloric canal → gastroduodenal junction

4.2 Interior — Gastric Rugae

The gastric mucosa is thrown into prominent longitudinal ridges called gastric rugae. These are most marked toward the pyloric part and along the greater curvature. They allow significant expansion as the stomach fills (capacity ~1500 mL in adults) and are an important endoscopic orientation landmark.

4.3 Peritoneal Attachments and the Lesser Sac

The stomach is almost entirely intraperitoneal. Its peritoneal attachments are the primary neurovascular conduits. The posterior surface of the stomach forms the anterior wall of the lesser sac (omental bursa) — a secluded retrogastric peritoneal compartment that communicates with the greater sac via the epiploic foramen of Winslow (anteriorly bounded by the hepatoduodenal ligament containing the portal triad; posteriorly by the IVC; superiorly by the caudate lobe; inferiorly by D1).

  • Lesser omentum: Lesser curvature → liver (hepatogastric + hepatoduodenal ligaments)
  • Greater omentum: Lower 2/3 of greater curvature → transverse colon (four layers)
  • Gastrosplenic ligament: Upper 1/3 of greater curvature (fundus) → splenic hilum; contains short gastric and gastroomental vessels
  • Gastrophrenic ligament: Superior fundus → inferior diaphragm
Omental Bursa — Why It Matters: The pancreas forms the posterior wall of the lesser sac. A perforated posterior gastric ulcer or pancreatic laceration releases fluid/enzymes into this compartment first, explaining why these conditions present with pain radiating to the back and collections localized to this space initially.

4.4 Visceral Relations — The Stomach Bed

The anterior surface of the stomach is related on its right to the liver's left and quadrate lobes, and on its left to the diaphragm. Its inferior aspect is in direct contact with the anterior abdominal wall. The posterior surface rests upon the "stomach bed".

Stomach Bed — "Dr S3 Kills Patients Mercilessly":
  • Diaphragm
  • Splenic artery, Suprarenal gland (left), Splenic flexure (left colic flexure) + Spleen (lateral boundary)
  • Kidney (left)
  • Pancreas (body)
  • Mesocolon (transverse)
Clinical correlate: a posterior gastric ulcer can erode into the pancreas (→ acute pancreatitis) or splenic artery (→ catastrophic haemorrhage).

4.5 Blood Supply

All gastric arteries are branches of the celiac trunk (L1). The stomach receives a rich, redundant blood supply along both curvatures:

  • Lesser curvature: Left gastric artery (directly from celiac) + right gastric artery (from hepatic artery proper)
  • Greater curvature: Right gastroomental (from gastroduodenal) + left gastroomental (from splenic artery)
  • Fundus: Short gastric arteries (from splenic artery)

Venous drainage follows the same named veins → portal system.

4.6 Autonomic Innervation

Parasympathetic (vagal) fibres increase gastric motility and secretion. Sympathetic fibres originate at T5–T9 and reach the stomach via the greater splanchnic nerves and celiac ganglion, inhibiting motility and causing vasoconstriction.

4.7 Surgical Anatomy — Vagotomy and Gastrectomy

Vagotomy reduces acid secretion by denervating the parietal cells. The key surgical pitfall during truncal vagotomy is the Nerve of Grassi (criminal nerve) — a branch of the posterior vagal trunk supplying the posterior gastric fundus. It branches early, often still within the mediastinum before the posterior trunk reaches the abdomen, making it easy to overlook. If missed during vagotomy, the posterior fundus remains innervated and parietal cell acid secretion persists, producing recurrent PUD despite apparent complete vagotomy.

Peptic Ulcer Disease: Erosion of the gastric or duodenal mucosa by acidic digestive juices. >80% are associated with H. pylori infection. A perforated duodenal or gastric ulcer releases free gas into the peritoneal cavity (pneumoperitoneum), visible as air under the diaphragm on an upright CXR — the classic radiological emergency sign.

5. Small Intestine

The small intestine extends from the pylorus to the ileocecal junction, measuring 6–7 metres in total. It comprises three segments: the duodenum (25 cm, largely retroperitoneal, the convergence point for bile and pancreatic secretions), the jejunum (proximal 2/5, left upper abdomen), and the ileum (distal 3/5, right lower abdomen). The duodenum curves in a C-shape around the pancreatic head; its intimate posterior relations make it highly vulnerable during posterior ulcer perforation and pancreatic surgery.

5.1 Duodenum — The Four Parts and Relations

Part Length Anterior Relations Posterior Relations Other Relations
1st (Superior) 5 cm Quadrate lobe of liver, gallbladder Portal vein, gastroduodenal artery, CBD Sup: epiploic foramen; Inf: pancreatic head/neck
2nd (Descending) 7.5 cm Gallbladder, liver, transverse colon Right kidney and renal vessels, IVC, psoas major Medial: pancreatic head; Lateral: right colic flexure

4th (Ascending) 2.5 cm Transverse colon and mesocolon Left psoas major, sympathetic chain, inferior mesenteric vein Superior: body of pancreas

5.2 Internal Anatomy of the Second Part

The descending duodenum is the critical interface between the hepatobiliary system and the GI lumen. Two papillae open on its medial wall:

  • Major duodenal papilla (Ampulla of Vater): 8–10 cm distal to the pylorus; marks the opening of the hepatopancreatic duct (junction of CBD and main pancreatic duct/duct of Wirsung), controlled by the Sphincter of Oddi. A plica semicircularis forms a "monk's hood" over it, with a plica longitudinalis descending from it.
  • Minor duodenal papilla (Accessory Ampulla): 2 cm proximal to the major papilla; opening of the accessory pancreatic duct (duct of Santorini).
Duodenum Exam Pearls:
  • The duodenal cap (bulb) is the smooth-walled first part seen on barium meal — most frequent site of duodenal ulceration
  • SMA syndrome: The 3rd part of the duodenum can be compressed between the aorta posteriorly and SMA anteriorly following rapid weight loss (loss of the mesenteric fat pad), causing duodenal obstruction
  • Duodenal diverticula occur in the medial wall of D2, where neurovascular structures pierce the muscle — creating a point of weakness
  • Blood supply: D1–D2 → gastroduodenal artery (from hepatic artery, celiac trunk); D3–D4 → inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (from SMA) — these form the pancreaticoduodenal arcade

5.4 Surface Area Adaptations

The small intestinal wall is maximally modified for absorption. Plicae circulares (valves of Kerckring) are permanent mucosal folds that do not flatten on distension. Villi and microvilli multiply the absorptive surface area exponentially. The crypts of Lieberkühn secrete digestive enzymes. Brunner's glands are in the duodenal submucosa only — they secrete bicarbonate-rich mucus to neutralise acidic gastric chyme.

Terminal Ileum — Unique Absorptive Role: Only the terminal ileum can absorb vitamin B12–intrinsic factor complex and bile salts. Resection of the terminal ileum causes B12 deficiency (→ megaloblastic anaemia) and bile salt malabsorption (→ steatorrhoea, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency). Peyer's patches in the ileum are the primary site of typhoid ulcers and tubercular ulcers.

5.5 Neurovascular Supply of the Small Intestine

Arterial supply is from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) via jejunal and ileal branches forming vascular arcades. Venous drainage parallels the SMA into the superior mesenteric vein → portal vein. Sympathetic innervation (T10–T11) reaches the gut via lesser splanchnic nerves and the superior mesenteric ganglion; these fibres are inhibitory to the gut wall and excitatory to sphincters. Post-surgical sympathetic dominance causes paralytic ileus. Parasympathetic supply is via the vagus, stimulating peristalsis.

Meckel's Diverticulum: A true diverticulum — a remnant of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct, present in ~2% of the population. Found within 2 feet (60 cm) of the ileocecal valve on the antimesenteric border. Contains ectopic gastric or pancreatic tissue in 50% of symptomatic cases. Rule of 2s: 2% incidence, within 2 feet of ileocecal valve, 2 inches long, 2x more symptomatic in males, presents before age 2 most commonly. Ectopic gastric mucosa produces acid → ulceration → painless rectal bleeding.

6. Large Intestine

The large intestine extends approximately 1.5 metres from the ileocecal valve to the anus. It is distinguished from the small intestine at laparotomy by three reliable gross anatomical features: taeniae coli, haustra, and omental appendices.

6.1 Large Intestine vs. Small Intestine — Distinguishing Features

The large intestine is distinguished from the small intestine by three gross anatomical features visible at laparotomy: taeniae coli (three longitudinal muscle bands), haustra (sacculations between the taeniae), and omental appendices (fatty peritoneal projections). Microscopically, the large intestine is characterised by abundant goblet cells and an absence of plicae circulares, villi, and Peyer's patches — all of which are present in the small intestine.

Feature Small Intestine Large Intestine
Diameter Smaller Larger
Taeniae coli Absent Present (3 bands)
Haustra Absent Present
Omental appendices Absent Present
Standard pathologies Worm infestation, typhoid, tubercular ulcers Amebiasis, carcinoma
Irritation outcome Diarrhoea Dysentery

6.2 Taeniae Coli — Three Bands

  • Mesocolic taenia: At the site of mesocolon attachment
  • Omental taenia: At the site of omental appendix attachment
  • Free taenia: Bearing no attachments

6.3 Caecum and Ileocecal Valve

The caecum is the blind-ended initial pouch of the large intestine, approximately 7 cm long, located in the right lower quadrant. It is intraperitoneal, giving it mobility within the iliac fossa. The terminal ileum enters the caecum at an oblique angle, invaginating to form the ileocecal valve, which prevents retrograde flow of bacteria-rich colonic contents into the small bowel.

6.4 Vermiform Appendix — Anatomy and Positions

The appendix arises from the posteromedial aspect of the caecum, just inferior to the ileocecal junction. Its base is fixed; its tip position is highly variable. All three taeniae coli converge on the base of the appendix — the most reliable surgical guide to finding it during laparotomy.

Position Clock-Face Prevalence Clinical Significance
Retrocecal 11 o'clock 64% (most common) Psoas sign positive; tenderness atypically posterior
Pelvic 5 o'clock 32% Obturator sign positive; may mimic pelvic pathology
Subcecal 6 o'clock 2% Directly below the caecum
Pre-ileal 1–2 o'clock 1% Anterior to terminal ileum

The appendix is suspended by the mesoappendix, which contains the appendicular artery (branch of ileocolic artery). Venous drainage is via the superior mesenteric vein. Sensory innervation follows sympathetic pathways to the T10 dermatome — explaining early periumbilical pain in appendicitis before somatic localisation to the RLQ.

6.5 McBurney's Point

McBurney's point localises the base of the appendix at the surface. It is located one-third of the way along the oblique line from the right ASIS to the umbilicus (the spinoumbilical line). This is the site of maximal tenderness in appendicitis and the standard incision point for open appendicectomy (either a gridiron/muscle-splitting incision or the more cosmetic Lanz incision).

Acute Appendicitis — Diagnosis: Caused by lumenal obstruction (often by a fecalith), leading to increased intraluminal pressure, ischaemia, and bacterial proliferation.
  • Rovsing's sign: RLQ pain elicited by LLQ palpation — indicates parietal peritoneal irritation
  • Psoas sign: RLQ pain on passive right hip extension in left lateral decubitus — suggests retrocecal appendix irritating the iliopsoas
  • Obturator sign: RLQ pain on passive internal rotation of flexed right hip — suggests pelvic appendix irritating the obturator internus
Progression: periumbilical visceral pain (T10) → RLQ somatic pain → guarding → rigidity → perforation and peritonitis.

Autonomic Divide at the Splenic Flexure: Proximal to the left colic (splenic) flexure, parasympathetic supply is via the vagus (cranial). Distal to the splenic flexure, parasympathetic supply switches to pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2–S4). This junction also approximates the SMA–IMA vascular watershed (Griffith's point).
Watershed Zone — Griffith's Point and Sudeck's Point: The colon has two zones of tenuous collateral blood supply where SMA and IMA territories meet with minimal overlap. Griffith's point at the splenic flexure marks the junction between the SMA's middle colic territory and the IMA's left colic territory; the marginal artery is most attenuated here. Sudeck's point is a second watershed at the rectosigmoid junction, where the sigmoid branches of the IMA meet the superior rectal artery. Both zones are vulnerable to ischaemic colitis during systemic hypoperfusion (e.g., septic shock, post-cardiac surgery) or when a major mesenteric vessel is ligated or occluded. Clinically, ischaemic colitis at the splenic flexure presents with cramping left-sided abdominal pain and bloody diarrhoea; diagnosis is confirmed by flexible sigmoidoscopy showing mucosal pallor, submucosal haemorrhage, or frank necrosis at the flexure.

6.7 Colonic Motility — Gastrocolic Reflex

The most important colonic mass movement reflex is the gastrocolic reflex: distension of the stomach by a large meal triggers increased propulsive mass movements in the colon, producing an urge to defecate. This is a neurally and hormonally mediated reflex, largely via the enteric nervous system and gastrin/cholecystokinin release. It is the reason patients with irritable bowel syndrome typically experience urgency shortly after meals.

6.8 Colonic Stomas

A stoma is a surgically created opening that diverts intestinal contents through the abdominal wall. An ileostomy is fashioned from the terminal ileum and an colostomy from the colon; the level of diversion determines the anatomical site, output volume, and stool consistency. The table below compares the two, as these differences are directly testable and guide post-operative management.

Criteria Ileostomy Colostomy

7. Liver and Pancreas

The liver is the largest gland in the body (average weight 1500 g), strategically positioned in the right hypochondrium, epigastric, and left hypochondrium regions. It has a dual blood supply and serves as the central metabolic, detoxification, and bile-producing organ. The pancreas is a retroperitoneal exocrine and endocrine gland positioned posterior to the stomach, sitting in the concavity of the duodenal C-loop. The spleen is an intraperitoneal immunological and filtrative organ in the left hypochondrium, closely related to the pancreatic tail.

7.1 Liver — Surfaces and the Bare Area

The liver has two primary surfaces. The diaphragmatic surface is smooth and convex, moulded against the inferior diaphragm. The visceral surface is irregular and concave, bearing impressions from the stomach, duodenum, right kidney, and gallbladder. The bare area on the posterior diaphragmatic surface is devoid of peritoneum, allowing direct liver-diaphragm contact — it is bounded by the coronary ligaments.

7.2 Peritoneal Ligaments of the Liver

  • Falciform ligament: Two-layered fold anchoring the liver to the anterior abdominal wall; its free margin contains the ligamentum teres (round ligament) — the obliterated remnant of the fetal umbilical vein.
  • Coronary ligaments (anterior and posterior): Bound the bare area; meet laterally as the triangular ligaments.
  • Hepatogastric ligament: Part of the lesser omentum; connects liver to stomach.
  • Hepatoduodenal ligament: Part of the lesser omentum; connects liver to D1; transmits the portal triad (portal vein, hepatic artery proper, CBD).

7.3 Lobar Anatomy and Porta Hepatis

Morphologically, the liver is divided into right, left, quadrate, and caudate lobes by surface fissures. The quadrate and caudate lobes arise from the right lobe morphologically but are functionally part of the left lobe. The porta hepatis (hilum) lies on the visceral surface between the caudate and quadrate lobes. It is the gateway for all neurovascular entry/exit. Its contents are:

  • Right and left hepatic ducts (biliary exit)
  • Right and left branches of hepatic artery proper (arterial entry)
  • Portal vein (entering posterior to hepatic arteries)
  • Sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve fibres
  • Hepatic lymph nodes

7.4 Hepatic Blood Supply — Dual System

The liver receives blood from two sources: 30% from the hepatic artery proper (oxygenated, from the celiac trunk), and 70% from the portal vein (nutrient-rich venous blood from the GI tract).

7.5 Portal-Caval Anastomoses

Portal hypertension (e.g., cirrhosis) forces blood through portosystemic collateral channels. The four clinically important anastomotic sites are:

Site Portal Vein Tributary Systemic Vein Tributary Clinical Manifestation
Esophageal Left gastric vein Esophageal vein (azygos) Esophageal varices — risk of massive haemorrhage
Paraumbilical Paraumbilical vein Epigastric veins Caput Medusae — dilated veins radiating from umbilicus
Rectal Superior rectal vein (IMA → portal) Inferior/middle rectal vein (systemic) Rectal varices (distinct from haemorrhoids)
Retroperitoneal Colic veins Retroperitoneal veins Typically no overt clinical finding

7.6 Biliary System and Gallbladder

Bile flows from hepatocytes → right and left hepatic ducts → common hepatic duct (~4 cm). The common hepatic duct joins the cystic duct (~3.8 cm) to form the common bile duct (~8 cm), which descends posterior to D1 and in a groove on the pancreatic head to join the main pancreatic duct at the ampulla of Vater.

The gallbladder is a pear-shaped, 30–50 mL sac with a fundus, body, and neck. The neck contains an infundibulum (Hartmann's pouch) — the most common site for gallstone impaction. The cystic duct contains the spiral valve of Heister, a mucosal fold that keeps the lumen open for bidirectional bile flow.

Cholelithiasis (Gallstones): Gallstones composed of cholesterol and bile pigments most commonly impact at Hartmann's pouch, obstructing gallbladder emptying and causing biliary colic. Stones passing into the CBD can cause obstructive jaundice and cholangitis. Definitive management is cholecystectomy. A stone at the ampulla of Vater can simultaneously obstruct bile and pancreatic secretion, precipitating pancreatitis (gallstone pancreatitis).

7.7 Pancreatic Anatomy — Parts and Peritoneal Status

The pancreas is secondarily retroperitoneal except for its tail, which is intraperitoneal within the splenorenal ligament. Its parts are: head (seated within the duodenal C-loop), uncinate process (hooks posterior to the SMA), neck, body, and tail.

7.8 Pancreatic Ductal System

  • Main pancreatic duct (duct of Wirsung): Runs the length of the gland; joins the CBD to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla (Ampulla of Vater), which opens at the major duodenal papilla, regulated by the Sphincter of Oddi.
  • Accessory pancreatic duct (duct of Santorini): Drains the upper head and uncinate process; opens at the minor duodenal papilla, 2 cm proximal to the major papilla.
Annular Pancreas: A congenital anomaly where the ventral bud of the pancreas bifurcates and one segment wraps completely around the second part of the duodenum. This encirclement causes duodenal constriction or atresia, presenting clinically as bilious vomiting in a neonate and polyhydramnios in utero (the fetus cannot swallow sufficient amniotic fluid).
Pancreatic Relations — Why They Matter:
  • Pancreas forms the posterior wall of the lesser sac — enzyme leak from pancreatitis or trauma collects here first
  • The splenic vein runs along the posterior surface of the pancreas → chronic pancreatitis can cause splenic vein thrombosis → sinistral (left-sided) portal hypertension
  • The CBD runs in a groove on the posterior surface of the pancreatic head → pancreatic head carcinoma causes painless obstructive jaundice (Courvoisier's law)

7.9 Spleen — Anatomy and Ligaments

The spleen lies in the left hypochondrium against the diaphragm at the level of ribs IX and X. It is held in position by two primary ligaments:

  • Gastrosplenic ligament: Connects the spleen to the greater curvature (fundus) of the stomach; contains the short gastric and gastroomental vessels.
  • Splenorenal (lienorenal) ligament: Connects the spleen to the left kidney; contains the splenic vessels and the tail of the pancreas.

The splenic artery is the largest and most tortuous branch of the celiac trunk and provides the entire arterial supply. Venous drainage is via the splenic vein, which joins the SMV to form the portal vein.

7.10 Peritoneal Classification of Abdominal Organs

The peritoneal cavity is a potential space (not a real, fluid-filled space) between the parietal peritoneum (lining the abdominal wall) and the visceral peritoneum (covering the organs). It contains only a thin film of serous fluid that allows frictionless organ movement. An organ's degree of peritoneal coverage determines its surgical mobility and the route by which disease can spread from it.

Classification Definition Examples
Intraperitoneal Almost completely covered by visceral peritoneum Stomach, spleen, jejunum, ileum, cecum, appendix, transverse colon, sigmoid colon
Mesoperitoneal (partially covered) Covered on one surface only; fused to the posterior wall Liver, gallbladder, ascending colon, descending colon
Retroperitoneal (secondarily) Lost original mesentery during development; covered anteriorly only Duodenum (D2–D4), pancreas (body, head), upper/middle rectum
Retroperitoneal (primarily) Never had a mesentery; always behind peritoneum Kidneys, suprarenal glands, ureters, aorta, IVC, lower rectum

8. Rectum

The rectum begins at the rectosigmoid junction at S3 and spans approximately 13 cm. It is the portion of the hindgut that stores fecal matter prior to defecation. Its flexures, internal folds, and layered peritoneal coverage are clinically significant for endoscopy, surgery, and oncology.

8.1 Rectum — Gross Anatomy and Flexures

The rectum follows two flexures: the sacral flexure (conforming to the anterior concavity of the sacrum) and the anorectal flexure (sharply posteroinferior at the anorectal ring). The distal rectum dilates to form the rectal ampulla, which stores fecal matter prior to defecation.

Retroperitoneal Classification of the Rectum:
  • Upper rectum = secondarily retroperitoneal (lost mesentery during development)
  • Lower rectum = primarily retroperitoneal (never had a mesentery)
This dictates surgical mobilisation and the spread of rectal malignancy.

8.2 Valves of Houston (Transverse Rectal Folds)

Three permanent transverse rectal folds (not mere mucosal ridges — they incorporate the circular muscle coat) line the interior of the rectum: one on the right (intermediate) and two on the left (superior and inferior). They support the weight of the fecal load, preventing constant pressure on the anal canal. They must be gently navigated during sigmoidoscopy to avoid iatrogenic perforation.

8.3 Peritoneal Reflections and Pelvic Pouches

The peritoneum covers the upper and middle thirds of the rectum anteriorly and then reflects anteriorly to form gender-specific pouches. In females, this forms the rectouterine pouch (Pouch of Douglas) — the lowest point of the peritoneal cavity. In males, the equivalent is the rectovesical pouch.

Inferior rectal artery Internal pudendal artery Lower rectum / anal canal

9. Anal Canal

The anal canal is 3.5–4 cm long, begins at the rectal ampulla where the puborectalis muscle forms a muscular sling, and terminates at the anus. The dentate (pectinate) line is the single most important landmark in this region, dividing it into two anatomically, embryologically, and clinically distinct halves.

9.1 Anorectal Angle and Puborectalis

The anorectal angle (approximately 80 degrees) is maintained by the puborectalis — its contraction during sitting "chokes" the rectum to maintain continence. Squatting relaxes the puborectalis, straightening the rectum and facilitating defecation.

9.2 The Dentate (Pectinate) Line — Primary Landmark

The dentate line marks the junction between the endodermal and ectodermal portions of the anal canal and is formed by the inferior margins of the anal valves. Above the valves are the anal columns of Morgagni (6–10 longitudinal ridges) and anal sinuses (containing openings of anal glands). Below the dentate line, an additional landmark, the White Line of Hilton, marks the intersphincteric groove between the internal and external sphincters.

Feature Above Dentate Line (Upper Canal) Below Dentate Line (Lower Canal)
Embryological origin Endoderm (primitive anorectal canal) Ectoderm
Epithelial lining Simple/stratified columnar epithelium Stratified squamous → keratinised skin
Arterial supply Superior rectal artery (from IMA) Inferior rectal artery (from internal pudendal)
Venous drainage Superior rectal vein → Portal circulation Inferior rectal vein → Systemic (IVC)
Lymphatic drainage Para-rectal / inferior mesenteric nodes Superficial inguinal lymph nodes

9.3 Sphincteric Mechanism

Continence depends on a dual sphincter system. The internal sphincter is an involuntary thickening of the circular smooth muscle coat surrounding the upper 2/3 of the anal canal. The external sphincter is voluntary striated muscle divided into three parts: deep (encircles upper end), superficial (attached to perineal body and anococcygeal ligament), and subcutaneous (encircles lower end). The anorectal ring — formed by puborectalis, deep external sphincter, conjoint longitudinal muscle, and highest internal sphincter — is palpable digitally on its posterior and lateral aspects. Damage to the anorectal ring causes incontinence.

Internal vs External Sphincter — Clinical Distinction: The internal anal sphincter is involuntary smooth muscle (sympathetic tone keeps it contracted at rest). The external anal sphincter is voluntary striated muscle (pudendal nerve, S2–S4). If a patient loses voluntary bowel control but resting tone is preserved and the anal wink reflex is present → the external sphincter (voluntary component) is damaged while the internal sphincter is intact. Complete section of the anorectal ring (internal + external + puborectalis) causes total incontinence.
Haemorrhoids (Piles):
  • Internal: Above the dentate line; insensitive to pain (visceral innervation); sensitive only to stretch. Primary piles are enlargements of the three main radicles of the superior rectal vein at 3, 7, and 11 o'clock (left lateral, right posterior, right anterior).
  • External: Below the dentate line; covered by skin; extremely painful (somatic innervation).
Causes include portal hypertension, chronic constipation, venous incompetence (no valves in portal system), poor connective tissue support.
Anal Fissure: An elongated ulcer in the anal mucosa caused by tearing of the anal valves, almost always in the posterior midline. Exquisitely painful due to somatic innervation below the dentate line. The posterior midline is preferentially affected because the superficial external sphincter does not encircle the canal posteriorly, leaving the mucosa unsupported.

10. Posterior Abdominal Wall

The posterior abdominal wall forms the back boundary of the abdominal cavity, providing structural support and serving as the origin of several muscles that move the vertebral column and lower limb. It is also the direct anatomical floor for the retroperitoneal organs — kidneys, ureters, adrenal glands, aorta, and IVC.

The diaphragm is the musculotendinous partition forming the superior boundary of the posterior abdominal wall. It attaches to the lumbar vertebrae via crura that blend with the anterior longitudinal ligament. Two congenital diaphragmatic defects are high-yield:

Foramen of Morgagni (Retrosternal Hernia): A para-retrosternal defect caused by incomplete fusion of the septum transversum with the sternum and anterior ribs. Content herniates anteriorly into the chest. Rare but tested.
Foramen of Bochdalek (Posterolateral Hernia): The most common congenital diaphragmatic hernia. A posterolateral defect (left side > right). Abdominal viscera herniate into the thorax, compressing the developing lung → pulmonary hypoplasia. Presents at birth with respiratory distress and a scaphoid abdomen.

10.3 Lumbar Plexus

The lumbar plexus forms within the substance of the psoas major from the ventral rami of L1–L4. Key branches:

  • Iliohypogastric nerve (L1): Supplies skin of hypogastric region
  • Ilioinguinal nerve (L1): Supplies anterior scrotum/labia and medial thigh; afferent limb of cremasteric reflex
  • Genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2): Genital branch (efferent cremasteric reflex — motor to cremaster) + femoral branch (skin over femoral triangle)
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3): Passes under (or through) the inguinal ligament near the ASIS; compression causes meralgia paraesthetica
  • Femoral nerve (L2–L4): Exits via muscular lacuna beneath inguinal ligament; motor to quadriceps, sensory to anteromedial thigh and medial leg
  • Obturator nerve (L2–L4): Exits through obturator canal; motor to adductors, sensory to medial thigh

10.4 Major Retroperitoneal Vessels

The aorta enters the abdomen at T12 through the aortic hiatus and bifurcates into the common iliac arteries at L4 (level of the iliac crests — a key surface landmark). The IVC forms at L5 from the union of the common iliac veins and ascends to the right of the aorta, exiting through the caval opening in the diaphragm at T8. Key anatomical relations: the left sympathetic trunk lies to the left of the aorta; the IVC, cisterna chyli, and the beginning of the azygos vein lie to its right. Note that the left ovarian (gonadal) vein drains into the left renal vein (not directly into the IVC), whereas the right gonadal vein drains directly into the IVC.

Aortic Bifurcation at L4 — palpable in thin patients as a pulsatile midline mass slightly above and to the left of the umbilicus. An aortic aneurysm expanding toward the spine causes back pain; anterior rupture causes haemoperitoneum.
Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA): Dilation >3 cm of the infrarenal aorta (most common site). Rupture presents with sudden, severe abdominal or back pain ± haemodynamic collapse. Carries a 90% mortality rate at rupture due to massive blood loss. Surgical treatment involves placement of a prosthetic graft. Elective repair is offered when diameter exceeds 5.5 cm.

10.5 Lymphatics — Cisterna Chyli and Thoracic Duct

Lymph from the abdomen drains hierarchically: lumbar (aortic/caval) nodes receive drainage from the posterior wall, kidneys, and reproductive organs; pre-aortic nodes receive drainage from the GI tract. These converge into the intestinal trunk, which delivers lymph to the cisterna chyli.

Cisterna Chyli and Thoracic Duct: The cisterna chyli is a thin-walled lymphatic sac located anterior to L1–L2 vertebrae and marks the inferior origin of the thoracic duct. The thoracic duct ascends through the aortic hiatus (T12) and terminates at the left venous angle — the junction of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins. Disruption (e.g., thoracic surgery or trauma) causes a chylothorax.
Psoas Abscess: Infection (TB, Crohn's, discitis) tracks within the fascial compartment of the psoas, then passes inferiorly over the pelvic brim and deep to the inguinal ligament, presenting as a tender inguinal swelling. Hip is held in flexion to relieve tension on the infected muscle.

11. Review of Arteries and Veins

The abdominal gut receives its arterial supply from three unpaired anterior branches of the aorta, each supplying the territory of the corresponding embryological gut division. Understanding the precise origin levels, branches, and watershed zones between these vessels is essential for interpreting ischaemia and planning vascular surgery.

11.1 Celiac Trunk (T12/L1)

The celiac trunk arises from the anterior aorta at the level of L1 (transpyloric plane) and immediately trifurcates into three branches, supplying the foregut (distal esophagus to D2).

  • Left gastric artery: Supplies the lesser curvature of the stomach; gives esophageal branches; provides the portal side of the esophageal portosystemic anastomosis
  • Splenic artery: Largest, most tortuous branch; supplies the spleen, pancreas (via pancreatic branches), and fundus of the stomach (via short gastric arteries); runs along the superior border of the pancreas
  • Common hepatic artery: Divides into gastroduodenal artery (→ superior pancreaticoduodenal + right gastroomental) and hepatic artery proper (→ right and left hepatic + right gastric arteries)
Celiac Trunk Compression Syndrome (Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome): The median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm abnormally compresses the celiac trunk at its origin. Presents with postprandial epigastric pain (intestinal angina) in young, thin females. Diagnosed by Doppler ultrasound or CT angiography; treated by surgical division of the ligament.

11.2 Superior Mesenteric Artery — SMA (L1)

The SMA arises from the anterior aorta at L1, just behind the neck of the pancreas, and descends anterior to the uncinate process and D3 (where it can compress the duodenum in SMA syndrome). It supplies the midgut (distal D2 → proximal 2/3 transverse colon). Anatomical relations: anteriorly lie the pyloric part of the stomach, the splenic vein, and the neck of the pancreas; posteriorly lie the left renal vein and the uncinate process of the pancreas. The SMA terminates in the right iliac fossa.

  • Inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (→ anastomoses with superior pancreaticoduodenal = celiac-SMA arcade)
  • Jejunal and ileal branches (12–15 branches forming arcades)
  • Ileocolic artery (→ appendicular artery, cecal branches)
  • Right colic artery (ascending colon)
  • Middle colic artery (transverse colon)

11.3 Inferior Mesenteric Artery — IMA (L3)

The IMA arises from the anterior aorta at L3 and supplies the hindgut (distal 1/3 transverse colon → upper rectum).

  • Left colic artery (descending colon)
  • Sigmoid arteries (2–4 branches to sigmoid colon)
  • Superior rectal artery (continuation of IMA → upper rectum)
Splenic Artery Aneurysm: The most common visceral artery aneurysm, accounting for approximately 60% of all visceral aneurysms. Risk factors include portal hypertension, multiparity, and fibromuscular dysplasia. Rupture during pregnancy carries high maternal and fetal mortality — treat electively when discovered.

11.4 Anastomotic Channels — Collateral Circulation

Two longitudinal anastomoses protect against ischaemia along the colon:

  • Marginal artery of Drummond: A continuous arterial arcade running along the inner border of the entire colon, anastomosing branches of the SMA and IMA — clinically critical during colonic resection to preserve adequate blood supply to remaining colon.
  • Arc of Riolan (Meandering Mesenteric Artery): A specific, larger anastomosis in the mesentery directly connecting the left branch of the middle colic (SMA) to the left colic (IMA). Becomes prominent and identifiable on angiography when either the SMA or IMA is obstructed.
Watershed Zones — Highest Ischaemia Risk:
  • Griffith's point: Splenic flexure — watershed between SMA (middle colic) and IMA (left colic). Most vulnerable to ischaemia in hypoperfusion states.
  • Sudeck's point: Rectosigmoid junction — watershed between sigmoid arteries (IMA) and superior rectal artery.
Systemic hypotension (e.g., post-cardiac surgery, aortic surgery) preferentially infarcts these watershed zones → ischaemic colitis presenting with bloody diarrhoea.

11.5 Venous Drainage Summary

Colon Segment Primary Vein Drains Into
Ascending colon Ileocolic and right colic veins Superior mesenteric vein
Transverse colon Middle colic vein Superior mesenteric vein
Descending colon Left colic vein Inferior mesenteric vein
Sigmoid colon Sigmoid veins Inferior mesenteric vein

All venous return drains into the hepatic portal vein (formed by the SMV and splenic vein posterior to the neck of the pancreas at L2) for hepatic detoxification before reaching systemic circulation.

11.6 Portal Hypertension — Classification

Portal hypertension (portal pressure >12 mmHg) is classified by the site of obstruction relative to the liver. When pressure rises, blood is forced through portosystemic anastomoses (covered in Section 7.5), producing esophageal varices, caput medusae, and hemorrhoids.

Category Site of Obstruction Causes
Pre-hepatic Before the liver (portal vein itself) Portal vein thrombosis, splenic vein thrombosis
Hepatic Within liver parenchyma Liver cirrhosis (primary cause), schistosomiasis, hepatitis
Post-hepatic After the liver (venous outflow) Right-sided heart failure, Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis), IVC obstruction
Liver cirrhosis = primary cause of portal hypertension. Hepatic fibrosis increases intrahepatic vascular resistance → portal pressure rises → collateral channels dilate. Esophageal varices (at the gastroesophageal junction) are the most lethal consequence — rupture causes massive upper GI haemorrhage.

12. Clinicals of Abdomen

The following cases integrate anatomy with clinical reasoning in the format used on board examinations. For each scenario, identify the anatomical basis before reading the answer.

12.1 Blunt Abdominal Trauma — Pancreatic Laceration

Scenario: A patient sustains blunt abdominal trauma; a CT scan shows pancreatic laceration with fluid accumulation anterior to the pancreas. Which space does the fluid first collect in?

Answer: Omental Bursa (Lesser Sac). The pancreas forms the posterior wall of the lesser sac. Enzymes or fluid leaking anteriorly from the pancreas collect in this compartment first, which is why pancreatic pseudocysts classically appear in the lesser sac.

12.2 Hiatal Hernia Repair — Nerve at Risk

Scenario: During surgical repair of a large hiatal hernia involving the gastric fundus, which specific nerve must be identified and protected?

Answer: Nerve of Grassi. This is a branch of the posterior vagal trunk supplying the posterior gastric fundus. It branches proximally — often still within the mediastinum — and is the most commonly missed nerve during truncal vagotomy. If overlooked, parietal cell innervation persists in the posterior fundus, causing recurrent peptic ulcer disease despite apparent complete vagotomy. Its tendency to escape detection earned it the epithet "criminal nerve."

12.3 Inguinal Hernia in a Neonate — Deepest Layer

Scenario: A newborn has small intestine within a scrotal swelling. What is the deepest fascial layer covering the herniated bowel?

Answer: Transversalis fascia (internal spermatic fascia). In indirect inguinal hernia, the bowel passes through the deep inguinal ring — an evagination of the transversalis fascia. The bowel therefore acquires the internal spermatic fascia as its deepest layer.

12.4 Cremasteric Reflex — Efferent Limb

Scenario: A patient with an L1–L2 spinal cord injury has an absent cremasteric reflex. Which nerve carries the efferent limb of this reflex?

Answer: Genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2). The cremasteric reflex arc: afferent — ilioinguinal nerve (L1, stroking the medial thigh); efferent — genital branch of genitofemoral nerve (motor to cremaster muscle). Absent reflex at L1–L2 eliminates both limbs.

12.5 Diverticulosis — Most Common Site and Mechanism

The sigmoid colon is the most common site for diverticulosis (60%). Diverticula are false diverticula — only mucosa and submucosa herniate through weak points in the circular muscle layer where blood vessels penetrate. Low-fibre diets increase intraluminal pressure, driving mucosal herniation. When inflamed, diverticulitis presents with LLQ pain — "left-sided appendicitis."

12.6 Testicular Cancer Metastasis — No Groin Nodes

Testicular lymphatics drain to lumbar/para-aortic nodes (mirroring the embryological origin of the testes near the kidneys), not to the inguinal nodes. Therefore, early testicular cancer produces no palpable groin lymphadenopathy. A scrotal skin tumour, by contrast, would drain to the superficial inguinal nodes.

12.7 Varicocele — Why Almost Always Left-Sided

The left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a 90-degree angle, creating increased hydrostatic pressure and susceptibility to incompetent venous valves → dilation of the pampiniform plexus. The right testicular vein enters the IVC at an acute angle, allowing easier drainage. A new right-sided varicocele in an older male should raise concern for a right renal mass compressing the right testicular vein.

12.9 Gallbladder Clinical Signs — Murphy's and Boas'

Two examination signs localise gallbladder pathology using referred and somatic pain mechanisms.

Murphy's Sign: The examiner presses into the right subcostal region while the patient takes a deep breath. The descending liver brings the inflamed gallbladder against the examiner's fingers → sudden cessation of inspiration due to pain. Positive in acute cholecystitis. Contrast with a negative sign in choledocholithiasis (no peritoneal irritation).
Boas' Sign: Hyperaesthesia (increased skin sensitivity) in the area below the right scapula. Caused by referred pain from gallbladder inflammation via the right phrenic nerve (C3–C5 dermatome). Often accompanies cholecystitis and cholelithiasis.

12.10 Jaundice — Three Types

Jaundice results from bilirubin accumulation. Distinguishing its origin is essential for directing investigation and management.

Type Mechanism Examples
Prehepatic Excessive RBC breakdown overwhelms hepatic conjugation capacity Haemolytic anaemia, incompatible transfusion, G6PD deficiency
Hepatic Hepatocyte failure to conjugate fat-soluble to water-soluble bilirubin Hepatitis, cirrhosis, paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose
Posthepatic (Obstructive) Obstruction of bile flow in the biliary tree → conjugated bilirubin backs up Gallstones (cholelithiasis), pancreatic head cancer, cholangiocarcinoma
Obstructive jaundice = pale stools + dark urine. Bile pigments cannot reach the gut (pale stools) and conjugated bilirubin (water-soluble) is excreted in urine (dark urine). Pancreatic head cancer causes painless obstructive jaundice — no gallbladder tenderness because the pancreatic mass compresses the CBD gradually. Courvoisier's sign: palpable, non-tender gallbladder in obstructive jaundice → malignancy until proven otherwise.

12.11 Scrotal Pathologies — Torsion and Hematocele

Several acute scrotal conditions require rapid differentiation because testicular torsion is a surgical emergency.

Testicular Torsion: Twisting of the spermatic cord causes simultaneous obstruction of venous and then arterial blood supply. Presents with sudden-onset severe scrotal pain, nausea, and a high-riding testis with a horizontal lie (bell-clapper deformity). Must be surgically detorsed within 6 hours to prevent irreversible testicular necrosis. No cremasteric reflex on the affected side.
Hematocele: Collection of blood within the tunica vaginalis, typically from blunt scrotal trauma causing rupture of branches of the testicular artery. Does not transilluminate (blood blocks light), distinguishing it from a hydrocele. Management: surgical evacuation if large.

12.12 Pyloric Stenosis

Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is a congenital narrowing of the pyloric canal caused by hypertrophy of the pyloric circular muscle.

Pyloric Stenosis: Presents at ~3 weeks of age with projectile, non-bilious vomiting after feeds (the obstruction is proximal to the ampulla of Vater, so bile cannot enter the vomitus). The infant appears hungry and may develop hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis. A palpable olive-shaped mass in the epigastrium is pathognomonic. Treatment: Ramstedt pyloromyotomy.

12.13 Surgical and Procedural Pearls

Several surgical procedures have high-yield anatomical vulnerabilities that commonly appear on examinations.

Liver Biopsy: Performed at the 10th intercostal space in the midaxillary line. This site avoids the costodiaphragmatic recess and provides direct access to the right lobe. The liver is highly vascular — fractured ribs can lacerate liver parenchyma, causing significant haemorrhage.
Splenectomy — Stomach at Risk: During ligation of the splenic artery at the splenic hilum, the short gastric arteries (branches of the splenic artery supplying the gastric fundus) may be inadvertently ligated, compromising the blood supply of the fundic portion of the stomach.

12.8 Appendicitis Pain Migration — Anatomical Explanation

Initial appendicitis causes visceral inflammation sensed via sympathetic afferents at T10, referred to the periumbilical region (umbilical dermatome). As inflammation progresses and involves the parietal peritoneum overlying the RLQ, somatic afferents (well-localised) produce pain localised to McBurney's point (one-third of the distance from the right ASIS to the umbilicus along the spinoumbilical line — the base of the appendix projects to this surface landmark). This pain migration from periumbilical to RLQ is pathognomonic of appendicitis.
