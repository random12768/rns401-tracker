This guide is built around active recall, not passive review. The old study cycle, reading a textbook-style chapter end to end and only then testing yourself, feels productive but is weak for long-term retention: most of the effort goes into recognizing material rather than retrieving it. This guide flips that. Each section is a UWorld-style question bank that forces you to commit to an answer first, then learn from the full explanation, which is where the real encoding happens.

How to use it well: keep your lecture slides open as the primary teaching source. For each section, work one question at a time, read the vignette carefully, and choose an answer before revealing anything, even when you are unsure: a wrong guess you then correct is remembered far better than a fact you simply read. After submitting, study the whole answer panel, not just the green header: confirm why the correct option is right, read why each distractor is wrong, absorb the teaching block, and use the slide reference to flip back to the deck for anything that did not land. 1. Introduction to Pathology

Pathology is the bridge between the basic sciences and clinical medicine. This section covers the four aspects of disease (etiology, pathogenesis, morphologic change, clinical significance), the language of signs versus symptoms, the major diagnostic techniques (histopathology, frozen section, cytology, FNAC, autopsy), and the special stains and microscopy modalities you need to recognize on test day.

2. Cellular Adaptations & Cell Injury

This section covers how cells respond to stress. Adaptive responses (hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia, dysplasia) preserve viability when stimuli are manageable. When stress exceeds adaptive capacity, cells progress through reversible injury (hydropic swelling, fatty change) and ultimately to irreversible injury and death. Master the mechanisms: ATP depletion, calcium influx, free radical generation, and membrane damage.

High-Yield Notes

The cell moves along a continuum: homeostasis, adaptation, reversible injury, irreversible injury. The four targets of injury are membranes, mitochondria, the cytoskeleton, and DNA. Hypoxia is the most common upstream driver: oxidative phosphorylation collapses, ATP falls, ion pumps fail, water enters the cell, and rising cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates destructive enzymes.

Common confusions to avoid:
  • Reversible injury can look dramatic on light microscopy (swollen cells, fatty vacuoles) yet still fully recover. The discriminator is membrane integrity, not how alarming the cytoplasm appears.
  • Cellular swelling and fatty change are signatures of sublethal injury. Mistaking them for end-stage damage is a common error.
  • Atrophy is a cell-level shrinkage program, not a death program. There is no inflammation and no membrane rupture; lipofuscin accumulates as a benign by-product.
  • Hyperplasia and hypertrophy can coexist whenever the cell type can both divide and respond to load; some tissues do only one because of their proliferative class.
  • Subcellular pigments differ by mechanism: lipofuscin from autophagy residue, hemosiderin from iron overload, melanin from melanocytes, anthracotic from inhaled carbon.
  • Intermediate-filament inclusions across diseases are all ubiquitinated protein aggregates the cell tagged for destruction but could not clear.

3. Necrosis

Necrosis is irreversible cell death with rupture of plasma membranes and release of intracellular contents into the surrounding tissue, provoking inflammation. The morphologic pattern depends on the etiology and the affected tissue: coagulative (most solid organ infarcts), liquefactive (brain, abscesses), caseous (TB granulomas), fat (pancreatitis, breast trauma), fibrinoid (vessel walls in vasculitis and malignant hypertension), and gangrenous (limb or organ-scale). Master the gross and microscopic differences, and learn the classic clinical contexts where each pattern appears.

High-Yield Notes

Necrosis is irreversible cell death with plasma-membrane rupture, leakage of contents, and a brisk inflammatory response; this contrasts with apoptosis (intact membrane, no inflammation). After lethal injury the cytoplasm becomes deeply eosinophilic as denatured protein binds eosin and loss of RNA reduces basophilia, and the nucleus passes through a stereotyped sequence of changes. The morphologic pattern that emerges depends on the tissue and the injury.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Coagulative is the default ischemic pattern in solid organs; the brain is the major exception because of its tissue composition.
  • An infarct's color (red versus white) is determined by tissue architecture and the number of arterial supplies, not by what embolized.
  • Granulomas form when macrophages cannot clear the antigen and T cells wall it off. Caseous necrosis is the histologic centerpiece.
  • Pancreatic fat necrosis is enzyme-driven; traumatic fat necrosis is not. Both can calcify, but only the enzymatic form produces chalky calcium soaps.
  • Apoptosis is the only cell-death mode that is energy-requiring, single-cell, and non-inflammatory. The patterns in this section all trigger inflammation.
  • Necrosis lags morphologically behind biochemistry: a heart can be lethally damaged and still look normal under light microscopy in the first few hours.

4. Apoptosis

Apoptosis is the cell's regulated suicide program: orderly, energy-dependent, and silent. It carves embryos, prunes unwanted immune cells, and disposes of damaged or virus-infected cells without alerting the immune system. Failures of apoptosis underlie certain cancers; excess apoptosis drives degeneration. The lecture also folds in cellular aging and autophagy, which share the same molecular machinery and the same kind of clinical correlates.

High-Yield Notes

Apoptosis is single-cell death by an active program: the cell shrinks, condenses, fragments into membrane-bound pieces, and is silently cleared by neighbors. Two activation pathways converge on a shared set of executioner caspases that disassemble the cell from within. The same biology underwrites cellular aging and autophagy, which is why this section bundles them together.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Apoptosis vs necrosis is not just a morphologic distinction; it is a statement about the cell's energy budget. A cell that can no longer make ATP cannot apoptose.
  • The mitochondrial pathway is the cell's internal censor; the death-receptor pathway is the external one. Cytotoxic lymphocytes use both at once.
  • Diseases of apoptosis split into "too little" (cancers, autoimmunity) versus "too much" (degenerative disorders, certain viral cytopathic effects). The mechanism the test wants is usually the imbalance, not a specific named protein.
  • Cell aging has a defined molecular clock: each somatic division shortens a protective chromosomal cap, and at a threshold the cell exits the cycle. Cancers escape by reactivating the enzyme that maintains that cap.
  • Autophagy is housekeeping turned to defense: it digests damaged organelles, misfolded protein aggregates, and intracellular pathogens by delivering them to lysosomes.

5. Intracellular Accumulations & Calcification

When a cell cannot keep up with normal metabolism, makes the wrong protein, loses a degradative enzyme, or ingests indigestible material, abnormal substances build up inside or around it. Each kind of accumulation has a characteristic appearance; recognizing the substance points to a mechanism, and the mechanism points to the disease. Pathologic calcification is a separate but related topic that follows one of two distinct rules.

High-Yield Notes

Four general mechanisms drive intracellular accumulation: inadequate removal of a normal substance, accumulation of an abnormal endogenous substance, failure to degrade a metabolite, and deposition of an exogenous substance the cell cannot break down. The substances tested most heavily are lipids, proteins (collectively called "hyaline"), glycogen, pigments, and calcium.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Three rules sort an inclusion: what it is, where it sits, and whether it disappears when the stimulus stops. Reversible accumulations rarely signal end-stage disease.
  • Iron overload is a spectrum: macrophage-only deposition without organ damage at the mild end; parenchymal deposition with organ failure at the severe end. The hereditary form involves a single iron-sensing gene.
  • Several conditions produce black-looking pigment in tissue (inhaled carbon, ingested laxative metabolites, deposited tyrosine metabolites). The clinical context tells you which one.
  • Hypopigmentation can come from a missing biosynthetic enzyme, autoimmune destruction of melanocytes, or a downstream amino-acid pathway block. The pattern (congenital, patchy, or syndromic) tells you which.
  • Calcification is local when the tissue is the problem and systemic when the serum chemistry is the problem. The discriminator is not the histologic appearance but what is happening elsewhere in the patient.

6. Acute Inflammation: Features & Mechanisms

Acute inflammation is the rapid host response that delivers neutrophils and plasma proteins to a site of infection or injury. It runs as two coordinated programs: a vascular phase that brings plasma and dilates vessels, and a cellular phase that walks circulating leukocytes from the bloodstream into the interstitium. Inherited defects at each step of that walk produce recognizable diseases.

High-Yield Notes

The vascular phase causes the cardinal signs: rapid arteriolar vasodilation gives warmth and redness, leakage of plasma proteins gives swelling, and stimulation of pain fibers gives the discomfort. The cellular phase delivers neutrophils first (within roughly 24 hours), followed by macrophages and lymphocytes over the next several days. Each physical step of the cellular cascade depends on a specific molecular pair, and each molecular step has a corresponding clinical defect to recognize.

Synthesis pearls:
  • An exudate by itself is a statement that inflammation is happening; a transudate by itself is a statement that pressure or oncotic balance is off. The same patient can have both at different sites.
  • Each step of the leukocyte cascade has its own molecular pair, and the inherited defects map onto those steps cleanly. The clinical picture (rolling defect, adhesion defect, killing defect) follows the level of the block.
  • Neutrophil counts in the blood can be paradoxically high in adhesion defects because the cells cannot leave the vasculature to enter tissue.
  • The vascular and cellular phases happen together but on different time scales: vasodilation and edema in minutes, neutrophil arrival in hours, mononuclear cells over days.
  • Oxygen-dependent killing depends on a single enzyme complex; without it, organisms that produce their own protective enzyme become the dominant pathogens.

7. Acute Inflammation: Morphologic Patterns, Mediators, Complement

Acute inflammation is choreographed by chemical mediators that come from four sources: cell membranes (lipid mediators from arachidonic acid), pre-formed cytoplasmic stores (vasoactive amines), plasma protein cascades (complement, kinins, clotting), and activated leukocytes (cytokines). Drugs that modulate inflammation act at defined nodes in these pathways. The gross appearance of inflamed tissue follows a small number of morphologic patterns that pair with characteristic causes.

High-Yield Notes

The arachidonic acid pathway is the highest-yield mediator system: phospholipase A2 frees arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids, and two enzyme branches turn it into two families of mediators with opposite roles in different vascular beds. Drugs intervene at each step. The complement system, the kinin system, and the clotting cascade are linked plasma cascades that share activators and amplify each other. Vasoactive amines and cytokines complete the picture.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Lipid products from a single fatty acid split into two opposite families: one mostly vasoactive, the other mostly chemotactic and bronchoconstrictive. Drug class indicates which branch is being modulated.
  • Complement, kinin, and clotting cascades all share Hageman factor (factor XII) as an activator, which is why injury that activates one tends to activate the others.
  • Cytokines IL-1 and TNF run the systemic response (fever, leukocytosis, acute-phase protein synthesis in the liver); they also upregulate endothelial adhesion molecules.
  • The morphologic patterns are pattern-recognition shortcuts: a thin watery effusion versus a fibrin-coated serosa versus a frank pocket of pus tells you the dominant mediator and often the likely organism.
  • Two complement regulator deficiencies produce named diseases with very different presentations: one is recurrent episodic swelling, the other is morning hemoglobinuria. Both come from missing brakes on the cascade.

8. Chronic Inflammation

Chronic inflammation is inflammation of prolonged duration in which active injury, tissue destruction, and attempts at repair all proceed at the same time. It usually arises when an acute response cannot clear the offending agent. The dominant cell types shift from neutrophils to mononuclear cells, and the tissue takes on a different histologic and gross signature. A specialized form, granulomatous inflammation, organizes activated macrophages into a focal lesion that walls off the trigger.

High-Yield Notes

Chronic inflammation lasts weeks to years and is driven by persistence of the trigger (microbial, autoimmune, foreign material, or toxic). The cellular cast changes from neutrophils to macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Macrophages and T cells engage in bidirectional cross-talk that sustains the lesion: T cells secrete cytokines that activate macrophages, and macrophages secrete cytokines that activate T cells. The systemic consequences (anemia, raised ESR and CRP, sometimes amyloid deposition) often dominate the clinical picture.

Synthesis pearls:
  • The transition from neutrophil-dominant to mononuclear-dominant infiltrate is one of the earliest histologic clues that an inflammation has crossed the chronic line. The shift is usually complete by about 48 hours.
  • A granuloma is a focal sign that the host has decided "we cannot kill this; we will wall it off." Whether caseation appears reflects how toxic the organism is to the macrophage population.
  • Macrophage and T cell cross-talk is bidirectional and self-reinforcing, which is why chronic inflammation persists as long as the trigger does.
  • Several granulomatous diseases are not infections at all. Recognizing the granuloma still narrows the differential to a specific small group.
  • The systemic features of chronic inflammation (anemia, raised ESR and CRP, sometimes amyloidosis) can be the only thing the patient notices, and they prompt the search for an unsuspected chronic cause.

9. Tissue Repair

Tissue repair restores function after injury. Two complementary processes do the work: regeneration replaces lost cells with cells of the same type when the conditions allow, while connective-tissue scarring fills in the defect when they do not. Both pathways depend on a choreographed sequence of inflammation, growth factor signaling, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, and remodeling. Predictable systemic and local factors speed or impair the process, and several characteristic abnormal outcomes follow when the program goes wrong.

High-Yield Notes

Regeneration requires two things: cells that can divide AND an intact connective-tissue scaffold to climb. Lose either one and the tissue scars instead. Scarring is a stereotyped sequence: inflammation, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, then remodeling over weeks to months. Growth factors choreograph each step, and a small set of micronutrient cofactors are needed for collagen biosynthesis and remodeling. The way a wound closes (clean apposed edges versus a gaping defect) determines how visible the final scar will be.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Regeneration depends on two preconditions: cells that can divide AND an intact stromal scaffold. Lose either one and the tissue scars instead.
  • The collagen story has two phases: rapid laying-down of an early, weaker isotype, then slow conversion to a stronger mature isotype during remodeling. Eventual tensile strength reaches about 80 percent of the original.
  • Several micronutrient cofactors come up repeatedly in healing biology. Deficiency of any one yields structurally weak collagen and impaired wound closure.
  • Wound contraction depends on contractile cells specific to granulation tissue, not on shrinkage of the surrounding native tissue. When excessive, the same biology produces joint contractures after burns.
  • Healing is delayed by predictable factors: infection, foreign material, poor blood supply, malnutrition, diabetes, and corticosteroids. Each acts at a defined step in the sequence.

10. Hemodynamics: Edema, Congestion & Hemorrhage

Edema is the abnormal accumulation of fluid in interstitial tissue spaces or body cavities, and it is the product of a tug-of-war between Starling forces. Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of capillaries while plasma oncotic pressure (driven by albumin) pulls it back in; lymphatics drain whatever escapes. Tip any one of these and fluid accumulates. Congestion and hyperemia are both states of increased blood volume in a tissue, but one is an active arteriolar process and the other a passive venous backup with predictable organ signatures. Hemorrhage is extravasated blood, graded by size and judged clinically by rate, volume, and location.

High-Yield Notes

The single most tested distinction in this lecture is transudate versus exudate. A transudate is a protein-poor, cell-poor ultrafiltrate produced by altered Starling forces (heart failure, hypoalbuminemia, sodium retention) and causes pitting edema. An exudate is a protein-rich, cell-rich fluid produced by increased vascular permeability in acute inflammation; pus is the archetype. Lymphatic obstruction and excess glycosaminoglycan synthesis both yield protein-rich, non-pitting edema (lymphedema and myxedema, respectively). There are five pathophysiologic mechanisms of edema: increased hydrostatic pressure, decreased plasma oncotic pressure, lymphatic obstruction, sodium and water retention (RAAS), and increased vascular permeability, with increased GAG synthesis as a sixth special category.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Pitting versus non-pitting tells you the mechanism: transudate and sodium retention pit; protein-rich lymphedema and myxedema do not.
  • Left heart failure backs up into the lungs (pulmonary edema, then CVC lung); right heart failure backs up into the systemic veins (peripheral pitting edema, then nutmeg liver).
  • Heart failure cells are hemosiderin-laden macrophages in alveoli, the histologic hallmark of chronic pulmonary congestion.
  • Active hyperemia is arteriolar dilation producing erythema (warm, red); congestion is passive venous backup producing cyanosis (blue, cool).
  • Post-mastectomy lymphedema is the most common cause of lymphedema in the US; Wuchereria bancrofti causes elephantiasis; dermal lymphatic plugging by tumor causes peau d'orange.
  • Chronic external blood loss (menstruation, peptic ulcer) leads to iron deficiency anemia; bleeding into cavities is reabsorbed and is less likely to cause IDA.

11. Hemostasis, Thrombosis & Embolism

Hemostasis is the tightly regulated process that keeps blood fluid within intact vessels yet forms a localized plug at sites of injury. The intact endothelium is normally antithrombotic; once it is breached, subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor recruit platelets (primary hemostasis) while tissue factor triggers the coagulation cascade to lay down fibrin (secondary hemostasis). Thrombosis is the pathologic counterpart of this same machinery, activated inappropriately inside an uninterrupted vessel. When a fragment of a thrombus (or any other detached mass) is carried downstream, it becomes an embolus, and where it lodges determines the clinical catastrophe that follows.

High-Yield Notes

Primary hemostasis begins when platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen, bridged by von Willebrand factor binding the platelet receptor GpIb. Activated platelets release ADP and thromboxane A2, recruit more platelets, and aggregate by linking their GpIIb/IIIa receptors with fibrinogen. Secondary hemostasis stabilizes this fragile plug with cross-linked fibrin generated by the coagulation cascade converging on thrombin. Thrombosis is driven by Virchow's triad, and of its three arms endothelial injury is the single most important. Knowing how an arterial thrombus differs from a venous one in composition, color, location, and mechanism predicts both its cause and its complication.

Synthesis pearls:
  • vWF bridges collagen to platelet GpIb (adhesion); fibrinogen bridges platelets via GpIIb/IIIa (aggregation). Deficiency or blockade of each defines a distinct bleeding disorder.
  • Endothelial injury is the most important arm of Virchow's triad because intact endothelium is actively antithrombotic; injury both exposes collagen and tips the balance toward coagulation.
  • Factor V Leiden is the most common inherited hypercoagulable state: the mutant factor V resists cleavage by activated protein C, so anticoagulation fails. Antithrombin III, protein C, and protein S deficiencies act by losing natural anticoagulants.
  • Migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau syndrome) signals an occult visceral adenocarcinoma, classically of the pancreas, which secretes procoagulant mucins.
  • A thrombus is firm and attached at its point of origin and may show lines of Zahn; a post-mortem clot is gelatinous, unattached, with a currant-jelly red base and chicken-fat yellow top.
  • Pulmonary emboli come from deep leg veins above the popliteal vein; the lung's dual blood supply usually prevents infarction, a saddle embolus at the bifurcation can cause sudden death, and recurrent small emboli cause pulmonary hypertension.
  • Most systemic arterial emboli arise from the left heart (post-MI mural thrombus, mitral stenosis left atrial thrombus, valve vegetations) and lodge in the legs, brain, spleen, and kidney, usually causing infarction.
  • Fat embolism follows long-bone fracture by 24 to 72 hours: the triad is respiratory distress, neurologic dysfunction, and a petechial rash with thrombocytopenia. Amniotic fluid embolism during labor causes DIC (thromboplastin) with greater than 80 percent mortality.

12. Infarction & Shock

An infarct is an area of ischemic necrosis caused by occlusion of either the arterial supply or the venous drainage of a tissue. Most infarcts result from thrombotic or embolic arterial occlusion; the others follow venous obstruction in organs with a single efferent vein. The resulting lesion is classically wedge-shaped, with the occluded vessel at its apex and the organ surface at its base, and it heals by scarring. Shock, in contrast, is a global problem: systemic hypoperfusion that drops cardiac output or effective circulating blood volume to the point that tissues cannot meet their metabolic demand, producing widespread cellular hypoxia. Both conditions share a final common pathway of ischemic injury, and both are predictable from a small set of governing variables.

High-Yield Notes

The single most testable distinction in infarction is red versus white. Color is decided by the geometry of the blood supply and whether blood can re-enter the necrotic zone. Solid organs with a single end-arterial supply (heart, spleen, kidney) wall off the dead tissue and go pale; loose, dual-supplied tissues (lung, small bowel) and any infarct caused by venous occlusion or followed by reperfusion fill with blood and go red. The morphology of the dead tissue itself is coagulative necrosis everywhere except the brain, which undergoes liquefactive necrosis. Whether an occlusion actually produces an infarct depends on four variables: the anatomy of the supply (dual vs end-arterial), how fast the occlusion develops (slow occlusion lets collaterals open), the intrinsic vulnerability of the tissue to hypoxia (neurons die in minutes, fibroblasts last hours), and the oxygen content of the blood.

Shock is classified by the hemodynamic defect that drives the hypoperfusion. Hypovolemic and cardiogenic shock both present cold and clammy because intense compensatory vasoconstriction raises systemic vascular resistance while cardiac output falls. The distributive shocks (septic, neurogenic, anaphylactic) instead lose vascular tone: systemic vascular resistance collapses, so early septic shock is warm and hyperdynamic with a high cardiac output before it decompensates. Septic shock is the prototype: endotoxin (LPS) from gram-negative walls triggers a TNF and IL-1 storm that causes vasodilation, endothelial activation with DIC, and diffuse alveolar damage (ARDS). Whatever the trigger, shock evolves through three stages and leaves a stereotyped set of end-organ lesions.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Red vs white is about geometry: loose or dually supplied tissue (lung, bowel) and venous or reperfused infarcts go red; solid end-arterial organs (heart, spleen, kidney) go white.
  • Coagulative necrosis is the universal infarct morphology; the brain is the lone exception with liquefactive necrosis.
  • Four variables decide whether occlusion infarcts at all: nature of the supply, rate of occlusion, tissue vulnerability to hypoxia, and blood oxygen content.
  • Cold shock (low CO, high SVR) is hypovolemic or cardiogenic; warm shock (high CO, low SVR) is early distributive (septic, neurogenic, anaphylactic).
  • Septic shock = LPS to TNF and IL-1 to vasodilation, DIC, and ARDS; its hallmark complications are disseminated intravascular coagulation and shock lung.
  • The three stages run nonprogressive (compensated) to progressive (lactic acidosis, blunted reflexes) to irreversible (cell injury beyond rescue even if hemodynamics are restored). ATN is the classic reversible organ lesion.

13. Genetic Disorders

Genetic disorders span single-gene defects in structural proteins, whole-chromosome aneuploidies, sex-chromosome imbalances, and several non-Mendelian patterns. A handful of recurring themes carry most of the exam weight: which protein or enzyme is missing, which chromosome and gene are involved, the mode of inheritance, and the one or two pathognomonic clinical clues that separate look-alike syndromes. The professor's distilled lists stress distinguishing pairs that are easy to confuse, so the cleanest path to mastery is learning the contrasts rather than the syndromes in isolation.

High-Yield Notes

Structural-protein disorders are about the matrix scaffold. Marfan is a fibrillin-1 (FBN1, chromosome 15) defect that weakens elastic-fiber scaffolding, giving ectopia lentis, mitral valve prolapse, and cystic medial necrosis of the aorta (the dissection that is the most common cause of death). Ehlers-Danlos is a collagen defect with hyperextensible skin and joints; the dangerous vascular type IV is a type III collagen (COL3A1) defect that ruptures arteries, bowel, and the gravid uterus. Homocystinuria mimics Marfan (ectopia lentis, arachnodactyly) but adds intellectual disability and thrombosis and is autosomal recessive.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Marfan versus homocystinuria: both give dislocated lens (ectopia lentis) and arachnodactyly as an example of genetic heterogeneity, but homocystinuria (cystathionine synthase deficiency, autosomal recessive) adds intellectual disability, vessel damage with thrombosis, and osteoporosis.
  • Sex-chromosome labs follow one rule: when the gonad fails, negative feedback is lost, so LH and FSH rise in both Turner and Klinefelter. Count Barr bodies (one fewer than the X count) to settle the karyotype.
  • Imprinting is parent-of-origin: a deletion on chromosome 15 causes Prader-Willi when paternal and Angelman when maternal, because the surviving allele is silenced.
  • Mitochondrial inheritance is purely maternal; an affected mother passes the mutation to all children, while affected fathers pass it to none. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy is the classic example.
  • Cystic fibrosis (CFTR, chromosome 7, ΔF508, autosomal recessive) dehydrates all exocrine secretions, explaining the Pseudomonas pneumonias, pancreatic insufficiency, meconium ileus, infertility, and the elevated sweat chloride that confirms the diagnosis.
  • Cherry-red macula with NO hepatosplenomegaly and whorled lysosomal bodies points to Tay-Sachs; massive hepatosplenomegaly with foamy macrophages and zebra bodies points to Niemann-Pick. Both store sphingolipids and cause infantile neurodegeneration, but the liver/spleen and the macular finding split them.
  • Glycogen storage diseases hinge on the organ: Von Gierke (liver/kidney, glucose-6-phosphatase) gives fasting hypoglycemia with hepatomegaly, Pompe (lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase) gives cardiomegaly and early cardiac death, and McArdle (muscle phosphorylase) gives exercise cramps with no rise in blood lactate.
  • Lesch-Nyhan: HGPRT deficiency (X-linked) causes juvenile gout with hyperuricemia, intellectual disability, and self-mutilation.
  • Familial hypercholesterolemia: most common inherited disorder (1 in 500), an LDL-receptor mutation on chromosome 19. Look for Achilles tendon xanthomas (diagnostic), xanthelasma, and premature atherosclerosis with MI in the late teens to twenties.

14. Environmental Diseases

Environmental diseases arise when external agents in the air we breathe, the substances we ingest, and the physical forces we encounter overwhelm the body's defenses. The same handful of mechanisms recur across this topic: a toxin disables a specific enzyme or carrier protein, generates reactive free radicals, or denatures cellular machinery, and the resulting organ injury produces a stereotyped clinical picture. Heavy metals are essential to master because each one targets distinct enzymes and organs, yielding pathognomonic findings the examiner expects you to separate. Gases (carbon monoxide, air pollutants), personal exposures (tobacco, alcohol, drugs of abuse), therapeutic drug toxicities, and physical injuries (thermal, radiation) round out a high-volume, heavily tested body of fact.

High-Yield Notes

Carbon monoxide is the USMLE's favorite environmental killer: an odorless, colorless product of incomplete combustion that binds hemoglobin roughly 200 times more avidly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin. This both reduces oxygen-carrying capacity and shifts the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, crippling tissue oxygen delivery. Headache is the first symptom (~30% HbCO), progressing to loss of consciousness (~50%); the cherry-red skin is classic but unreliable. Treat with 100% oxygen, escalating to hyperbaric oxygen.

The EPA monitors six criteria air pollutants. The high-yield four each have a signature mechanism and disease, summarized below. Note that radon is the only one acting through ionizing (alpha) radiation, and that its lung-cancer risk is markedly synergistic with cigarette smoking.

Personal exposures and physical injuries account for the remaining high-yield facts. Memorize the mechanism-level pearls below; most board items are won by recalling the single denaturing or receptor-level event and its one downstream consequence.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Organophosphates irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase, flooding synapses with acetylcholine (SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI, emesis; plus miosis and fasciculations). Treat with atropine plus pralidoxime (2-PAM).
  • Tobacco is the leading single preventable cause of cancer and the MCC of premature death in the US; carcinogens drive lung (SCC, small cell), bladder, oral, esophageal, and pancreatic cancers, plus COPD/emphysema and atherosclerosis (CO-mediated endothelial injury).
  • Alcohol: hepatic steatosis (reversible) → alcoholic hepatitis (Mallory bodies) → cirrhosis. Thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke–Korsakoff. In pregnancy, acetaldehyde crosses the placenta causing fetal alcohol syndrome (microcephaly, smooth philtrum, short palpebral fissures), the MC preventable cause of mental retardation.
  • Cocaine blocks monoamine reuptake, causing vasospasm-driven MI/stroke and nasal septal perforation; acetaminophen overdose generates the free-radical metabolite NAPQI causing centrilobular hepatic necrosis, reversed by N-acetylcysteine.
  • Burns: full-thickness (3rd degree) burns are painless from nerve loss; >20% TBSA causes hypovolemic shock; infection (Pseudomonas) is the commonest cause of death; Curling ulcers form in the proximal duodenum.
  • Ionizing radiation damages DNA directly and via hydroxyl free radicals; lymphocytes are the most radiosensitive cells (lymphopenia is the first hematologic change), and late effects are fibrosis and second cancers (leukemia, thyroid, osteosarcoma) after a 10–20 year latency.

15. Nutritional & Metabolic Disorders

Nutritional pathology is driven by two questions on the boards: what does a given deficiency look like, and what biochemical step explains it. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) depend on intact fat absorption, so cholestasis, cystic fibrosis, and pancreatic disease produce combined deficiencies; water-soluble vitamins (the B complex and C) are not stored except B12, so their deficiencies appear quickly. Protein-energy malnutrition splits into kwashiorkor and marasmus along a single axis of whether calories are adequate. Obesity is increasingly an exam favorite for its neurohumoral control and its cancer associations, and a handful of trace elements (zinc especially) round out the picture. The unifying skill is matching a clinical clue to the underlying cofactor role.

High-Yield Notes

Group the vitamins first by solubility, because solubility predicts the cause of deficiency. The four fat-soluble vitamins are lost together whenever fat absorption fails, so a patient with cystic fibrosis, chronic cholestasis, or celiac disease can present with night blindness, osteomalacia, hemolysis, and easy bleeding all at once. Water-soluble vitamins are not stored (B12 is the lone exception, with hepatic stores lasting years), so dietary lack shows up fast and toxicity is rare. For each one, anchor to a single biochemical role and the deficiency falls out of it.

Synthesis pearls:
  • Fat-soluble vitamins fail together in fat malabsorption: cystic fibrosis is the classic cause of vitamin E deficiency, and any cholestasis or pancreatic insufficiency reproduces the pattern.
  • Vitamin K and vitamin C both cause bleeding, but distinguish them: K gives a coagulation defect (prolonged PT/PTT, normal vessels), while C gives a vascular defect (perifollicular hemorrhage, bleeding gums, normal PT/PTT).
  • Both B12 and folate cause megaloblastic anemia, but only B12 deficiency causes neurologic disease (subacute combined degeneration). Giving folate alone to a B12-deficient patient corrects the anemia but lets the neuropathy progress.
  • Kwashiorkor versus marasmus turns on one variable: whether calories are adequate. Adequate calories with no protein gives edema and fatty liver; deficiency of both gives wasting without edema.
  • Obesity is an endocrine organ: visceral fat drives insulin resistance and DM2, lowers adiponectin, and aromatizes androgens to estrogen, raising endometrial and breast cancer risk. Leptin resistance, not leptin lack, underlies common obesity.
