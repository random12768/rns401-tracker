1. Penicillin

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: penicillin G (IV/IM; benzathine and procaine IM depot forms) and penicillin V (oral).
  • Mechanism: beta-lactam ring binds penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases), blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking (D-Ala-D-Ala) so cell wall synthesis stops; bactericidal, cell wall autolysins activated.
  • Indications: gram-positive cocci (Streptococcus), Actinomyces, Clostridium perfringens, Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Neisseria meningitidis, Pasteurella multocida; strep pharyngitis, rheumatic fever prophylaxis, group B strep prophylaxis in labor.
  • Adverse effects: type I hypersensitivity (IgE, anaphylaxis), hemolytic anemia (type II, positive direct Coombs), drug-induced interstitial nephritis; resistance via beta-lactamase (plasmid-encoded) or altered PBPs (MRSA).
  • Buzzwords: penicillin V acid stable (oral), penicillin G acid labile (IV/IM); benzathine penicillin G for syphilis and rheumatic fever prophylaxis; Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction after syphilis treatment; renal tubular secretion blocked by probenecid.
  1. 1Purple pencil-saber: Penicillins (penicillin G, penicillin V) are the natural penicillin class, narrow-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics.
  2. 2Princess Ellen: Penicillin, the drug class represented in this video (named for the "-cillin" suffix).
  3. 3Ringed planet: Penicillin contains a beta-lactam ring in its structure, classifying it as a beta-lactam antibiotic.
  4. 4Purple coccoid space stations: Penicillins are primarily effective against gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Actinomyces) and a few gram-negative organisms.⭐ Natural penicillins do not cover MSSA (beta-lactamase) or most gram-negative rods.
  5. 5D-Ala wall: D-alanyl-D-alanine forms the terminal end of peptide chains that are cross-linked to strengthen the bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall; it is the substrate mimicked by penicillin.
  6. 6Planetary Building Project worker: Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs, transpeptidases) catalyze peptidoglycan cross-links, providing cell wall structural integrity and shape.
  7. 7Inactivated PBP worker: Penicillin and other beta-lactams kill bacteria by irreversibly binding and inhibiting PBPs, halting cell wall synthesis (bactericidal, cell lysis).⭐ Active against growing bacteria; not effective against organisms lacking a cell wall (Mycoplasma).
  8. 8V-winged ship: Penicillin V is acid-stable and well absorbed orally.
  9. 9Acid nebula: Penicillin V resists gastric acid degradation compared to other forms, so it is suitable for oral use.
  10. 10G-shaped hairdo: Penicillin G is acid-labile with low oral bioavailability, so it is given parenterally (IV or IM).
  11. 11Ivy: Penicillin G is administered IV; the depot form penicillin G benzathine is given IM for slow release.⭐ Never give benzathine penicillin IV (cardiac arrest).
  12. 12Red neck bandana: Penicillin is first-line for streptococcal pharyngitis (strep throat) caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (gram-positive coccus).
  13. 13Heart-shape + planet Sydenhaam: Benzathine penicillin G (IM), or sometimes oral penicillin V, is used for secondary prevention of rheumatic fever (Sydenham chorea is a manifestation).⭐ Treating strep pharyngitis prevents rheumatic fever but not post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  14. 14Green Emperor Viridans: Penicillin treats infections caused by viridans group streptococci (alpha-hemolytic gram-positive cocci).
  15. 15Bicuspid hat: Penicillin G treats left-sided native-valve infective endocarditis caused by viridans streptococci and Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis).⭐ Viridans strep endocarditis often occurs on previously damaged valves after dental procedures.
  16. 16A galactic baby: Intrapartum penicillin G prophylaxis prevents early-onset neonatal infection with group B Streptococcus (S. agalactiae, a gram-positive coccus).
  17. 17Israeli flag: IV penicillin G is initial therapy for Actinomyces israelii (gram-positive branching filamentous rod, "sulfur granules", cervicofacial abscess), followed by oral penicillin V.
  18. 18Perforated space suit: Penicillin G is the antibiotic of choice for Clostridium perfringens infections (gas gangrene), along with surgical debridement.
  19. 19Space dog: Penicillin (typically amoxicillin-clavulanate) treats dog and cat bite wounds infected with Pasteurella multocida (gram-negative coccobacillus).
  20. 20Red gloves and boots: Intramuscular benzathine penicillin G treats syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum (spirochete); palms and soles rash marks secondary syphilis.⭐ Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction (fever, chills) can occur after starting treatment from endotoxin-like release.
  21. 21Meningitis mohawk: Penicillin G is the drug of choice for meningitis caused by Neisseria meningitidis (gram-negative diplococcus), once susceptibility is known.⭐ Empiric meningitis treatment is typically ceftriaxone plus vancomycin.
  22. 22Beta guard: Bacteria that produce beta-lactamase (many Staph aureus, H. influenzae, Enterobacterales) are resistant to penicillin because the enzyme hydrolyzes the beta-lactam ring; MRSA instead has altered PBP2a (mecA gene).
  23. 23Circular plasmid gun: Beta-lactamases are often encoded on plasmids, allowing rapid spread of resistance among bacteria by conjugation.
  24. 24IgE missiles: Penicillins can cause type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity: urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, anaphylaxis.⭐ Cross-reactivity with cephalosporins is low (about 1-2 percent).
  25. 25Exploding RBC asteroids: Penicillins can cause hemolytic anemia (type II hypersensitivity) in which antibodies against the drug-hapten on RBCs lead to red cell destruction.
  26. 26IgG missile on surface: IgG antibodies bound to penicillin on the RBC surface cause a positive direct Coombs test (detects antibody-coated RBCs).
  27. 27Blue star clusters in kidney nebula: Penicillins can cause drug-induced (acute) interstitial nephritis: fever, rash, eosinophilia, WBC casts, pyuria with sterile urine.

2. Nafcillin, Oxacillin, Methicillin, Dicloxacillin

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: nafcillin, oxacillin (IV), dicloxacillin (oral), methicillin (no longer used clinically).
  • Mechanism: beta-lactams that bind PBPs and block peptidoglycan cross-linking; bulky side chains make them resistant to staphylococcal beta-lactamase (penicillinase).
  • Indications: methicillin-sensitive Staph aureus (MSSA) - skin and soft tissue infection, abscess, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis; not active against MRSA.
  • Adverse effects: hypersensitivity, interstitial nephritis (methicillin), hepatotoxicity/cholestasis (oxacillin, dicloxacillin), phlebitis (nafcillin); resistance in MRSA via altered PBP2a (mecA gene), treat with vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin or ceftaroline.
  • Buzzwords: penicillinase-resistant penicillins; Staph aureus is catalase and coagulase positive, ferments mannitol, protein A binds IgG Fc; TSST-1 superantigen; preformed enterotoxin food poisoning (mayonnaise, meats).
  1. 1Ringed planet: Anti-staphylococcal penicillins (nafcillin, oxacillin, dicloxacillin, methicillin) are beta-lactam antibiotics; they contain the beta-lactam ring.
  2. 2Death Coccus wall: The bacterial peptidoglycan cell wall contains repeating strands with terminal D-alanyl-D-alanine peptides that are cross-linked.
  3. 3Planetary Building Project worker: Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) form the peptidoglycan cross-links; beta-lactams inhibit them.
  4. 4Ineffective beta guard: Staphylococcal beta-lactamases (penicillinase) are ineffective against anti-staph penicillins, which therefore remain active against beta-lactamase-producing MSSA.
  5. 5Protective armor: The bulky R-groups of anti-staph penicillins sterically block beta-lactamase from binding and hydrolyzing the beta-lactam ring.⭐ This is why they are called penicillinase-resistant penicillins.
  6. 6Pencil saber staff: Nafcillin, an anti-staph penicillin given IV (hepatically cleared, no renal adjustment).⭐ Can cause phlebitis and neutropenia with prolonged use.
  7. 7Ox horns: Oxacillin, an anti-staph penicillin given IV.
  8. 8Purple coccoid space station cluster: Anti-staph penicillins are active against gram-positive staphylococci, particularly methicillin-sensitive Staph aureus (MSSA).
  9. 9Red hump: Anti-staph penicillins (nafcillin, oxacillin; dicloxacillin orally) are used for skin and soft tissue infections (folliculitis, abscesses, cellulitis) due to MSSA.
  10. 10Tricuspid pyramids: Anti-staph penicillins treat MSSA endocarditis (tricuspid valve in IV drug users).⭐ Cefazolin is an alternative.
  11. 11Fish bones: Anti-staph penicillins treat MSSA osteomyelitis.
  12. 12Altered wall builder: MRSA produces an altered PBP (PBP2a, encoded by mecA) with low affinity for beta-lactams, conferring resistance to anti-staph penicillins and other beta-lactams (except ceftaroline).
  13. 13Merciless Dark Lord MRSA: Methicillin-resistant Staph aureus (MRSA); treat with vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, or ceftaroline.

3. Ampicillin, Amoxicillin, Piperacillin, Ticarcillin

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: aminopenicillins - ampicillin (IV/PO), amoxicillin (PO); extended-spectrum (antipseudomonal) - piperacillin, ticarcillin; beta-lactamase inhibitors - clavulanate, sulbactam, tazobactam.
  • Mechanism: beta-lactams that bind PBPs and block peptidoglycan cross-linking; added gram-negative activity via better porin penetration; susceptible to beta-lactamase unless combined with an inhibitor.
  • Indications: respiratory (otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia), UTI, Listeria meningitis, Enterococcus, H. pylori (amoxicillin), endocarditis prophylaxis, Pseudomonas and anaerobes (piperacillin-tazobactam).
  • Adverse effects: hypersensitivity, rash (especially with EBV mononucleosis), SJS/TEN, hepatotoxicity (amoxicillin-clavulanate cholestasis), diarrhea, interstitial nephritis, hemolytic anemia.
  • Buzzwords: ampicillin covers HELPSS (H. influenzae, E. coli, Listeria, Proteus, Salmonella, Shigella, Enterococci); amoxicillin for strep prophylaxis and H. pylori; ampicillin-sulbactam for anaerobes; pip-tazo for Pseudomonas and anaerobes.
  1. 1Amino's Space Cantina: Amoxicillin and ampicillin are aminopenicillins (penicillins with an added amino group, improving gram-negative penetration).
  2. 2Ringed planet: Aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins are beta-lactam antibiotics; they contain the beta-lactam ring.
  3. 3Unfinished Death Coccus: Aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), halting peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis; they are bactericidal.
  4. 4Purple coccoid space stations: Aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins retain activity against gram-positive organisms (Streptococcus, Enterococcus, non-penicillinase-producing Staph).
  5. 5Red color: Aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins have improved activity against gram-negative bacteria compared with natural penicillins.
  6. 6Prism: Aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins are broad-spectrum antibiotics (gram-positive, gram-negative, and some anaerobes).
  7. 7Ammo box: Amoxicillin, an aminopenicillin.
  8. 8Open mouth: Amoxicillin has good oral bioavailability (better than ampicillin) and can be given with food.
  9. 9Red bandana: Amoxicillin and ampicillin can treat streptococcal pharyngitis (strep throat) caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, although penicillin is first-line.
  10. 10Plugged ears: Amoxicillin is first-line for acute otitis media and sinusitis (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis).⭐ Use amoxicillin-clavulanate if beta-lactamase-producing H. influenzae or M. catarrhalis is suspected.
  11. 11Rusty chest plate: Aminopenicillins treat community-acquired pneumonia caused by S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae.
  12. 12H wing ship: Aminopenicillins have activity against Haemophilus influenzae (non-beta-lactamase-producing strains).
  13. 13Helicopter: Amoxicillin is part of triple therapy (with clarithromycin and a PPI) for Helicobacter pylori infection.
  14. 14Robin of Ixodes: Amoxicillin treats Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi) in children and pregnant women, where doxycycline is avoided.⭐ Doxycycline is first-line in non-pregnant adults.
  15. 15Amp: Ampicillin, an aminopenicillin given IV or orally.
  16. 16Ivy: Ampicillin can be administered IV (useful for serious infections).
  17. 17Gas mask: Ampicillin-sulbactam treats anaerobic infections such as aspiration pneumonia.
  18. 18Diplococcus rock drums: Ampicillin is a drug of choice for Enterococcus (E. faecalis) infections.
  19. 19Knocked over amp: Beta-lactamase-producing strains of Enterococcus (and E. faecium) are resistant to ampicillin; use vancomycin or linezolid for resistant strains.
  20. 20Set LIST: Ampicillin treats meningitis caused by Listeria monocytogenes (give in neonates, elderly and immunocompromised, in addition to ceftriaxone and vancomycin).
  21. 21Intestinal tubes: Aminopenicillins have activity against GI and urinary tract gram-negative rods (E. coli, Proteus mirabilis, Salmonella, Shigella).
  22. 22Beta bouncer: Beta-lactamase-producing bacteria are resistant to aminopenicillins and extended-spectrum penicillins unless a beta-lactamase inhibitor is added.
  23. 23Distracting clarinet: Clavulanate is a beta-lactamase inhibitor combined with amoxicillin (Augmentin) and previously with ticarcillin.⭐ Amoxicillin-clavulanate can cause cholestatic hepatitis.
  24. 24Back to back tambourines: Sulbactam and tazobactam are beta-lactamase inhibitors combined with ampicillin and piperacillin respectively.
  25. 25Spleen hole: Amoxicillin is used as prophylaxis against encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae) in asplenic patients (children).
  26. 26Bloody tooth: Amoxicillin is used as prophylaxis before dental procedures in patients at high risk for infective endocarditis (prosthetic valve, prior endocarditis, certain congenital heart disease).
  27. 27Trampled liver spot: Aminopenicillins can cause drug-induced liver injury (especially cholestatic injury with amoxicillin-clavulanate).
  28. 28Red mask sloughing off: Aminopenicillins can cause Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN).
  29. 29Red spots at Ebstein's Bar: Aminopenicillins can cause a diffuse maculopapular rash in patients with a viral illness, classically Epstein-Barr virus infectious mononucleosis; it is not a true IgE-mediated allergy.⭐ Rash in mono after ampicillin or amoxicillin is a classic board scenario.
  30. 30Piper: Piperacillin, an extended-spectrum (antipseudomonal) penicillin; given IV, usually with tazobactam.
  31. 31Tiger stripes: Ticarcillin, an extended-spectrum (antipseudomonal) penicillin, previously combined with clavulanate (no longer widely available).
  32. 32Gas mask: Extended-spectrum penicillins (piperacillin-tazobactam) treat anaerobic infections (e.g. intra-abdominal infection).
  33. 33Mona Lisa: Extended-spectrum penicillins (piperacillin, ticarcillin) treat Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections.⭐ Often combined with an aminoglycoside for synergy in serious Pseudomonas infections.

4. Cephalosporins

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: 1st gen cefazolin, cephalexin; 2nd gen cefuroxime, cefoxitin, cefotetan; 3rd gen ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime; 4th gen cefepime; 5th gen ceftaroline.
  • Mechanism: beta-lactams that bind PBPs and block peptidoglycan cross-linking (bactericidal); more beta-lactamase-resistant than penicillins; MRSA PBP2a is bound only by ceftaroline.
  • Indications: 1st gen - gram-positive cocci, skin infection, surgical prophylaxis, PEcK gram-negatives; 2nd gen - HENS plus anaerobes (cefoxitin/cefotetan); 3rd gen - meningitis, gonorrhea, Lyme, Pseudomonas (ceftazidime); 4th gen - Pseudomonas; 5th gen - MRSA.
  • Adverse effects: hypersensitivity (low cross-reactivity with penicillin), disulfiram-like reaction and vitamin K deficiency (cefotetan), biliary sludge and neonatal kernicterus (ceftriaxone), increased nephrotoxicity with aminoglycosides.
  • Buzzwords: no coverage of LAME (Listeria, Atypicals, MRSA, Enterococci); ceftriaxone is the single-dose IM treatment for gonorrhea; ceftriaxone should not be mixed with calcium-containing IV solutions in neonates.

  2. 2Unfinished Death Coccus: Cephalosporins bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), halting peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis; they are bactericidal.
  3. 31st General Lex: First-generation cephalosporins include cephalexin and cefazolin.

  7. 7Puffy red gloves and patches: First-generation cephalosporins treat skin and soft tissue infections (cellulitis, abscesses) caused by Staph aureus (MSSA) and Streptococcus.

  9. 9Red bladder cup: First-generation cephalosporins treat UTIs caused by gram-negative bacteria (Proteus mirabilis, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae; the "PEcK" organisms).
  10. 10Surgical instruments: Cefazolin is the standard agent for surgical prophylaxis (skin flora coverage).⭐ Does not cross the BBB.
  11. 112nd General Fox: Second-generation cephalosporins include cefuroxime, cefotetan and cefoxitin.

  13. 13Tea cup: Cefotetan, a second-generation cephalosporin with anaerobic (Bacteroides fragilis) coverage.⭐ Can cause a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol and vitamin K deficiency bleeding.
  14. 14Fox: Cefoxitin, a second-generation cephalosporin with anaerobic coverage (B. fragilis), used for intra-abdominal and pelvic infections.

  17. 173rd General Taz: Ceftazidime is a third-generation cephalosporin with antipseudomonal activity.
  18. 18Mostly red space stations: Third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime) have extended gram-negative coverage beyond second generation, with reduced gram-positive activity.⭐ Ceftazidime has poor gram-positive coverage.
  19. 193 axes: Ceftriaxone and cefotaxime are third-generation cephalosporins that cross the BBB.⭐ Ceftriaxone is excreted in bile (biliary sludge) and can cause kernicterus in neonates; cefotaxime is preferred in neonates.
  20. 20Mohawk helmet: Ceftriaxone and cefotaxime are first-line empiric therapy for bacterial meningitis (with vancomycin, plus ampicillin in neonates, elderly and immunocompromised).
  21. 21H-wing ship: Third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime) are active against Haemophilus influenzae.
  22. 22Rusty chest plate: Third-generation cephalosporins treat community- and hospital-acquired pneumonia.
  23. 23Taz pointing to Mona Lisa: Ceftazidime is used to treat Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections (the only third-generation agent with significant antipseudomonal activity).

5. Monobactams & Carbapenems

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: monobactam - aztreonam; carbapenems - imipenem (with cilastatin), meropenem, ertapenem, doripenem.
  • Mechanism: beta-lactams that bind PBPs and block peptidoglycan cross-linking; aztreonam binds PBP3 (monocyclic ring) and is resistant to many beta-lactamases; carbapenems are very resistant to beta-lactamases including ESBLs.
  • Indications: aztreonam - aerobic gram-negative rods (including Pseudomonas) in penicillin-allergic patients; carbapenems - broad-spectrum gram-positive, gram-negative and anaerobic coverage for serious infections, ESBL organisms, meningitis (meropenem), and resistant organisms.
  • Adverse effects: aztreonam - GI upset, rash, usually safe in penicillin allergy; carbapenems - GI upset, rash, seizures (imipenem highest risk), eosinophilia; imipenem needs cilastatin because renal dehydropeptidase inactivates it.
  • Buzzwords: aztreonam has no gram-positive or anaerobic activity; ertapenem lacks Pseudomonas coverage; carbapenems lack MRSA and atypical coverage; carbapenemase-producing (CRE/KPC) bacteria are resistant.
  1. 1Ringed planet: Monobactams (aztreonam) and carbapenems are beta-lactam antibiotics; monobactams have a monocyclic beta-lactam ring while carbapenems have a fused ring.
  2. 2Unfinished Death Coccus: Monobactams and carbapenems bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), halting peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis (bactericidal).
  3. 3One eyed AZ-3M: Aztreonam, the only available monobactam; it binds PBP3 and is given IV (or inhaled for cystic fibrosis).
  4. 4Bellows: Monobactams (aztreonam) are active against aerobic gram-negative rods only (no gram-positive or anaerobic activity).⭐ Think "aerobic gram-negative rods in a penicillin-allergic patient".
  5. 5Red rod robot: Aztreonam has activity against aerobic gram-negative rods such as Enterobacterales (E. coli, Klebsiella) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
  6. 6Mona Lisa: Monobactams (aztreonam) and carbapenems (except ertapenem) treat Pseudomonas infections.⭐ Ertapenem lacks Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter activity.
  7. 7Red mohawk helmet and rusty lungs: Aztreonam treats serious systemic gram-negative infections (pneumonia, sepsis, UTI, intra-abdominal infection, and meningitis).
  8. 8Pencil wound: Aztreonam can be used in patients with a history of penicillin allergy because it does not cross-react with penicillins (sharing a side chain only with ceftazidime).⭐ Safe in penicillin allergy; no cross-reactivity because of the monocyclic structure.
  9. 9Ineffective beta guard: Many beta-lactamases are ineffective against aztreonam and carbapenems (but ESBLs hydrolyze aztreonam).
  10. 10Hover car: Carbapenems include imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem and doripenem.
  11. 11Prism: Carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem) have a very broad spectrum covering gram-positive, gram-negative (including Pseudomonas) and anaerobic bacteria.
  12. 12Gas mask: Carbapenems treat anaerobic infections (e.g. aspiration pneumonia, intra-abdominal infections).
  13. 13"When others fail": Carbapenems are reserved for infections resistant to other antibiotics (ESBL-producing organisms, multidrug-resistant bacteria) and life-threatening infections.⭐ They do not cover MRSA, atypicals, or Enterococcus faecium.
  14. 14Mohawk helmet: Meropenem treats bacterial meningitis (imipenem is avoided because of seizure risk).⭐ Used empirically in healthcare-associated meningitis.
  15. 15Amy: Imipenem, a carbapenem that is always co-administered with cilastatin.
  16. 16Cilastin oil: Cilastatin inhibits renal dehydropeptidase I in the proximal tubule, preventing degradation of imipenem, prolonging its half-life and reducing nephrotoxicity.
  17. 17Mud puddle: Carbapenems commonly cause GI side effects (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea).
  18. 18Rust spots: Carbapenems can cause skin rash (and hypersensitivity; low cross-reactivity with penicillins).
  19. 19Shaking: Imipenem lowers the seizure threshold, especially at high doses or in renal impairment, and in patients with CNS disease.⭐ Meropenem has lower seizure risk but is reduced by valproate (carbapenems lower valproate levels).
  20. 20Ineffective rainbow beta guard: Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) do not hydrolyze carbapenems, making them drugs of choice for ESBL infections; resistance arises with carbapenemases (KPC) or porin loss.

6. Vancomycin

Exam snapshot
  • Drug: vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic given IV for systemic infection (not absorbed orally; oral form acts in the gut lumen for C. difficile).
  • Mechanism: binds the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of peptidoglycan precursors, blocking transglycosylation and transpeptidation (cell wall synthesis); bactericidal; not affected by altered PBPs or beta-lactamases.
  • Indications: MRSA, Staph epidermidis, penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae, Enterococcus (ampicillin-resistant), empiric therapy for meningitis, endocarditis and osteomyelitis; oral for C. difficile colitis.
  • Adverse effects: vancomycin flushing syndrome (formerly red man syndrome, histamine release), nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, thrombophlebitis, DRESS/eosinophilia, neutropenia; therapeutic drug monitoring (trough or AUC).
  • Buzzwords: no activity against gram-negative bacteria (cannot cross outer membrane); VRE has D-Ala-D-Lac; infuse slowly and pretreat with antihistamine to prevent flushing.

17. Amphotericin & Flucytosine

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: amphotericin B (conventional deoxycholate and liposomal), nystatin (topical/oral swish only), flucytosine (5-FC).
  • Mechanism: amphotericin B and nystatin bind ergosterol and form membrane pores causing ion leakage and cell death; flucytosine is converted by fungal cytosine deaminase to 5-FU, inhibiting DNA and RNA synthesis.
  • Uses: amphotericin B for severe systemic mycoses (cryptococcal meningitis with flucytosine, mucormycosis, disseminated endemic fungi, invasive Candida/Aspergillus alternatives); nystatin for oral/vaginal/skin candidiasis.
  • Adverse effects: amphotericin 'shake and bake' infusion reactions (fever, chills, hypotension, headache, thrombophlebitis), nephrotoxicity with distal RTA, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, anemia; flucytosine - bone marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity.
  • Buzzwords: 'amphoterrible'; liposomal form and saline loading reduce renal toxicity; flucytosine never used alone (resistance); cryptococcal meningitis = ampho B + flucytosine then fluconazole.
  1. 1Amphibian: Amphotericin B is a polyene antifungal with the broadest spectrum of the systemic antifungals (Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, Mucor/Rhizopus, endemic dimorphic fungi).
  2. 2Non-sterol: Fungal cell membranes contain ergosterol instead of cholesterol, which is the target that gives polyenes selectivity.
  3. 3Holes in amphibian tank: Amphotericin B and nystatin bind ergosterol and insert into the membrane to form pores, causing leakage of K+ and other cytoplasmic contents and cell death (fungicidal).⭐ Polyenes = pores. Amphotericin 'tears' holes in membranes.
  4. 4Organ systems: Amphotericin B is used for severe, life-threatening systemic fungal infections: cryptococcal meningitis, invasive mucormycosis, disseminated histoplasmosis/blastomycosis/coccidioidomycosis, and severe Candida or Aspergillus infection (when other agents fail).
  5. 5Ivy: Amphotericin B is not absorbed orally and is given IV for systemic disease (oral use only for GI-luminal effect).
  6. 6Fat bubble: Liposomal amphotericin B has a lipid carrier that reduces binding to human cholesterol-containing membranes, giving less nephrotoxicity and fewer infusion reactions (at higher cost).⭐ Liposomal amphotericin = less toxic but more expensive.
  7. 7Amphibian binding cholesterol necktie: Toxicity arises because amphotericin also binds cholesterol in mammalian membranes, which explains renal, cardiac and infusion toxicities (nickname: 'amphoterrible').
  8. 8Fainting and holding head: Immediate infusion-related toxicities include hypotension and headache (via cytokine release); slow infusion and pre-medication (acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, hydrocortisone) can reduce them.
  9. 9Shake and bake: Fevers and chills ('shake and bake') are infusion-related reactions to amphotericin B from IL-1/TNF release.
  10. 10Tongue on brain: Intravenous amphotericin penetrates the CSF poorly, so intrathecal administration is sometimes used for CNS fungal infection (e.g., coccidioidal meningitis), although IV amphotericin with flucytosine is effective for cryptococcal meningitis.
  11. 11Shaking brain: Intrathecal amphotericin can cause CNS toxicity such as seizures, headache, arachnoiditis and neurologic deficits.
  12. 12Blue veiny pattern: Thrombophlebitis (peripheral vein irritation) is an infusion-related toxicity, so a central line is often used.
  13. 131-shaped acid tube: Cumulative nephrotoxicity from amphotericin includes distal (type 1) renal tubular acidosis, azotemia and decreased GFR from afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction and tubular damage.⭐ Amphotericin: hypoK, hypoMg, type 1 RTA, decreased GFR.
  14. 14Depleted potassium banana peel: Type 1 (distal) RTA with increased K+ wasting causes hypokalemia (monitor and replace potassium).
  15. 15Magnet falling into sink: Amphotericin causes renal magnesium wasting and hypomagnesemia; electrolytes must be monitored and replaced.
  16. 16Emergency saline wash: Pre-hydration with IV normal saline (sodium loading) before amphotericin decreases the risk and severity of nephrotoxicity.⭐ Saline loading is renal protection for amphotericin.
  17. 17Cracked, drained kidney and white frog: Damage to renal peritubular interstitial cells reduces erythropoietin production, causing normocytic normochromic anemia with amphotericin B.
  18. 18Flute: Flucytosine (5-FC) is an oral antifungal pyrimidine analog.
  19. 19C turned into U: Fungal cytosine deaminase converts flucytosine to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), which humans do little of (selective toxicity); 5-FU is then converted to 5-FdUMP (inhibits thymidylate synthase) and 5-FUTP (incorporated into RNA).⭐ Flucytosine resistance: loss of cytosine deaminase or permease.
  20. 20Smeared double helix and single strand: Flucytosine metabolites inhibit fungal DNA synthesis (thymidylate synthase) and disrupt RNA function, so it is fungistatic/fungicidal depending on species. Adverse effects: dose-dependent bone marrow suppression (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia), hepatotoxicity, GI upset.⭐ It is never used as monotherapy because of rapid resistance.
  21. 21Meningitis helmet: Flucytosine plus amphotericin B (induction) is the preferred regimen for cryptococcal meningitis, followed by fluconazole consolidation/maintenance.
  22. 22'Crypt': Cryptococcus neoformans (encapsulated yeast, India ink positive, in AIDS patients with CD4 <100) is treated with amphotericin B + flucytosine, then fluconazole.
  23. 23Nyce: Nystatin is a polyene with the same mechanism as amphotericin B (binds ergosterol to form pores) but is too toxic for systemic use, so it is used only topically or as an oral swish/swallow.
  24. 24Canada: Nystatin is active against Candida species (candidiasis).
  25. 25Snow on crotch: Topical nystatin (creams, vaginal tablets) treats mucocutaneous candidiasis such as vulvovaginal candidiasis and intertrigo/diaper dermatitis.
  26. 26Swish and swallow drinking fountain: Oral nystatin suspension is 'swish and swallow' for oropharyngeal candidiasis (thrush); it acts locally and is not absorbed from the GI tract.⭐ Clotrimazole troches are an alternative for oral thrush.

18. Azoles

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs ('-conazole'): fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, ketoconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole.
  • Mechanism: inhibit fungal cytochrome P-450 14-alpha-demethylase, blocking lanosterol -> ergosterol and disrupting the fungal membrane (fungistatic).
  • Uses: fluconazole - Candida (thrush, vaginal, esophageal), Cryptococcus; voriconazole - invasive aspergillosis; itraconazole - dimorphic fungi, onychomycosis, sporotrichosis; topical clotrimazole/miconazole - tinea, vaginal candidiasis; ketoconazole - topical tinea, off-label Cushing.
  • Adverse effects: CYP450 inhibition (drug interactions), hepatotoxicity, QT prolongation; voriconazole - visual disturbances; itraconazole - negative inotrope (CHF); ketoconazole - blocks steroid synthesis (gynecomastia, adrenal insufficiency); teratogenic.
  • Buzzwords: 'cone-azole'; fluconazole penetrates CSF; itraconazole/ketoconazole need gastric acid (avoid PPIs); voriconazole = scarecrow (Aspergillus) + color/blurry vision.
  1. 1Pine cones: Azole antifungals are named '-conazole' (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, ketoconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole); the pine-cone images cue the 'cone' in the suffix.
  2. 2Emerald perimeter: Ergosterol is the major sterol in the fungal cell membrane (mammalian membranes use cholesterol), so inhibiting its synthesis is selectively toxic.
  3. 3Crushed emerald builder: Azoles inhibit ergosterol synthesis, depleting ergosterol and causing accumulation of toxic methylated sterols that impair membrane function (fungistatic).
  4. 4Lane closed: Azoles block conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol by inhibiting the fungal cytochrome P-450 enzyme 14-alpha-demethylase.⭐ Azoles block 14-alpha-demethylase; terbinafine blocks squalene epoxidase.
  5. 5Broken chrome bumper: Azoles inhibit fungal CYP450 (14-alpha-demethylase) and also inhibit human CYP450 enzymes, causing many drug interactions (raising levels of warfarin, statins, calcineurin inhibitors, etc.).
  6. 6Car-crushing vortex: Voriconazole inhibits human CYP450 enzymes (CYP2C19, 2C9, 3A4), so it raises levels of many co-administered drugs; it is also metabolized by CYP2C19 (polymorphisms alter levels).⭐ Itraconazole and ketoconazole are strong 3A4 inhibitors; fluconazole is moderate.
  7. 7Blurry flashes: Voriconazole commonly causes transient visual disturbances (blurry vision, photopsia/flashes of light). Other effects: hepatotoxicity, visual hallucinations, photosensitivity/skin cancer.
  8. 8Sepia tone: Voriconazole may alter color vision (visual disturbance is transient and dose-related).
  9. 9Aspirating scarecrow with lung hyphae: Voriconazole is the first-line treatment for invasive aspergillosis (Aspergillus fumigatus: acute-angle, septate, branching hyphae), including pulmonary disease in immunocompromised hosts.⭐ Aspergillus -> voriconazole; Mucor -> amphotericin B (voriconazole is ineffective).
  10. 10Canada: Voriconazole is active against Candida species, including esophageal candidiasis and some fluconazole-resistant strains.
  11. 11Flying monkey: Fluconazole is an azole antifungal ('Flu' - Wizard of Oz flying monkey) with excellent oral bioavailability and penetration into CSF.
  12. 12Canada: Fluconazole treats Candida albicans infections (oral, vaginal, esophageal, and invasive disease when susceptible); C. glabrata and C. krusei are often resistant.
  13. 13White patches: Mucocutaneous candidiasis presents as white plaques (thrush) that scrape off, leaving an erythematous base; KOH shows yeast with pseudohyphae.
  14. 14Snow on crotch: A single 150 mg oral dose of fluconazole treats uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis. Fluconazole is avoided in pregnancy (teratogenic at high doses).
  15. 15Snow in gutter: Systemic fluconazole is first-line for esophageal candidiasis (odynophagia, AIDS-defining) and is often tried empirically before endoscopy.
  16. 16Crypt: Fluconazole treats Cryptococcus neoformans infection: consolidation and maintenance therapy for cryptococcal meningitis after amphotericin B + flucytosine induction; also used for milder disease.
  17. 17Meningitis mohawk helmets: Fluconazole penetrates the CNS/CSF very well, which is why it is used for cryptococcal meningitis (and coccidioidal meningitis).⭐ Fluconazole = best CSF penetration of the azoles.
  18. 18Good witch: Itraconazole (Glinda the good witch, 'I'-traconazole) is an azole for dimorphic fungi and dermatophytes; it is an inhibitor of CYP3A4, has negative inotropy (avoid in heart failure) and needs gastric acid for absorption (capsule).
  19. 19Dimorphic butterfly: Itraconazole treats endemic dimorphic fungi (mold in the environment at 25 C, yeast in tissue at 37 C): histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis (fluconazole preferred for meningitis) and sporotrichosis (lymphocutaneous, drug of choice).⭐ Mnemonic: Blasto, Histo, Cocci, Sporo respond to itraconazole; Paracoccidioides too.
  20. 20Tarnished slipper toes: Itraconazole treats onychomycosis (and dermatophyte infections), though terbinafine is first-line for nails.
  21. 21Tin man: Tinea (ringworm) infections are caused by dermatophytes (Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton).
  22. 22Close trim: Topical clotrimazole is used for tinea corporis, cruris and pedis (athlete's foot), and for oral thrush as troches.
  23. 23My cone: Topical miconazole is used for tinea infections (dermatophytosis) and candidal skin infections; it is available OTC.
  24. 24Snow on crotch: Topical clotrimazole and miconazole (creams or suppositories) treat vulvovaginal candidiasis.
  25. 25Tinman's key: Topical ketoconazole (cream or shampoo) treats dermatophytosis such as tinea corporis, and seborrheic dermatitis/dandruff and tinea versicolor (Malassezia).
  26. 26Adrenal lock: Ketoconazole inhibits human steroidogenic CYP enzymes (CYP17: 17-alpha-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase, plus cholesterol side-chain cleavage), blocking conversion of pregnenolone to 17-hydroxypregnenolone and thereby cortisol and sex hormone synthesis.   29. 29Breast bumps: Ketoconazole's anti-androgenic effects cause gynecomastia, decreased libido and impotence in men.

19. Griseofulvin, Terbinafine, Echinocandins

Exam snapshot
  • Antifungal targets: ergosterol (polyenes bind it; azoles and terbinafine block its synthesis), cell wall (echinocandins), microtubules (griseofulvin), DNA/RNA synthesis (flucytosine).

  1. 1Rusty tin man: Dermatophytes (Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton) infect keratinized tissue and cause tinea: corporis, pedis, cruris, capitis (scalp/hair) and onychomycosis (nails). KOH prep shows septate hyphae.

  8. 8Little tin man: Topical terbinafine treats tinea corporis, cruris and pedis (dermatophytosis of the skin).
  9. 9Tin man hat: Oral terbinafine (and griseofulvin) is first-line for tinea capitis in children; terbinafine is especially effective for Trichophyton species.
  10. 10Biting nails: Oral terbinafine is the first-line treatment for onychomycosis (nail infection), given for about 6-12 weeks because it concentrates in nail and skin; it is fungicidal.⭐ Terbinafine is superior to griseofulvin and itraconazole for onychomycosis.
  11. 11Non-sterol: Fungal cell membranes contain ergosterol rather than cholesterol, the basis of selective toxicity for polyenes and azoles, and the pathway terbinafine blocks upstream.

  13. 13Mud puddles: Terbinafine commonly causes GI upset (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea); also taste disturbance, headache and rash.
  14. 14Liver spot: Terbinafine can cause hepatotoxicity (rarely liver failure), so check LFTs before and during therapy and avoid in active liver disease. It is also a CYP2D6 inhibitor.⭐ Monitor LFTs with terbinafine, griseofulvin and ketoconazole.

20. Acyclovir, Valacyclovir, Famciclovir

Exam snapshot
  • Drugs: acyclovir, valacyclovir (prodrug), famciclovir (prodrug of penciclovir); related: cidofovir, foscarnet for resistant HSV/CMV.
  • Mechanism: guanosine analogs activated by viral thymidine kinase (HSV/VZV) then cellular kinases to the triphosphate, which inhibits viral DNA polymerase and terminates the chain (lack of 3'-OH).
  • Use: HSV (genital herpes, encephalitis, neonatal), VZV (shingles, varicella), and prophylaxis in immunocompromised hosts; valacyclovir/famciclovir preferred for zoster (better bioavailability, less frequent dosing).
  • Adverse effects: nephrotoxicity (crystalline nephropathy, interstitial nephritis) with IV use; neurotoxicity (delirium, hallucinations); dose-adjust in renal impairment; not active against CMV.
  • Buzzwords: 'guanosine analog, TK-dependent'; resistance = TK-deficient mutants -> foscarnet; foscarnet and cidofovir need no viral TK; hydrate patients; start zoster therapy within 72 h.
  1. 1Hermes: Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2), the target for acyclovir-class drugs (HSV-1: oral/labial, encephalitis; HSV-2: genital, neonatal).
  2. 2Recycling: Acyclovir is an antiviral used for HSV (and VZV) infection.
  3. 3Purine-shaped luggage: Acyclovir is a guanosine (purine) nucleoside analog.

  • Buzzwords: 'GET GAP on the Metro' (Giardia, Entamoeba, Trichomonas, Gardnerella, Anaerobes, Pylori); treats infections below the diaphragm; treat the sexual partner in trichomoniasis.
  1. 1Metro (the train): Metronidazole is a nitroimidazole antimicrobial active against anaerobic bacteria and protozoa; the 'Metro' pun in the name anchors the whole scene.⭐ Mnemonic: GET GAP on the Metro - Giardia, Entamoeba, Trichomonas, Gardnerella, Anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium), Pylori.
  2. 2Mr. Fragilis stabbed in the abdomen: Metronidazole covers Bacteroides fragilis, the main anaerobe in polymicrobial intra-abdominal infections (abscess, peritonitis, diverticulitis, appendicitis), usually combined with an agent covering enteric gram-negatives. It is also used for brain abscess and anaerobic infections below the diaphragm.
  3. 3Broken helix: Metronidazole is a prodrug that is reduced by anaerobic organisms' ferredoxin/nitroreductase systems into free-radical metabolites that fragment bacterial and protozoal DNA, making it bactericidal. Aerobic cells cannot activate it, which gives selectivity.⭐ Needs low redox potential, so it has no activity against aerobes.
  4. 4Do not enter: Metronidazole is active against Entamoeba histolytica (amebiasis). It is used for invasive disease (colitis, liver abscess) and must be followed by a luminal agent such as paromomycin or iodoquinol to clear cysts.
  5. 5Colonel Giardia with the protozoan shield: Metronidazole (or tinidazole) treats Giardia lamblia infection (foul-smelling, greasy diarrhea after drinking contaminated water or camping).
  6. 6Mr. Trichomonas with the strawberry: Metronidazole treats Trichomonas vaginalis vaginitis/cervicitis: frothy green-yellow malodorous discharge, strawberry cervix (colpitis macularis), pear-shaped motile trophozoites on wet mount, raised vaginal pH.⭐ Trichomonas = flagellated protozoan; 'strawberry cervix'.
  7. 7Mrs. Trichomonas: Trichomoniasis is sexually transmitted, so the patient and the sexual partner(s) must both be treated (even if asymptomatic) to prevent reinfection.
  8. 8Ms. Gardner with the venus fly trap: Metronidazole treats bacterial vaginosis (Gardnerella vaginalis overgrowth): thin gray-white 'fishy' discharge, clue cells, positive whiff test with KOH, pH >4.5. Partner treatment is not required for BV.⭐ BV: clue cells + whiff test; Trichomonas: motile protozoa. Both are treated with metronidazole.
  9. 9Helicopter: Helicobacter pylori triple therapy is a PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin; in penicillin-allergic patients metronidazole replaces amoxicillin. Metronidazole is also part of bismuth quadruple therapy (PPI + bismuth + metronidazole + tetracycline).
  10. 10Mlle. Clostridium with the gas mask: Metronidazole has broad anaerobic coverage, including Bacteroides, Prevotella, Fusobacterium and Clostridium species (e.g., C. perfringens gas gangrene adjunct). It lacks activity against aerobes and has poor activity against Actinomyces and Propionibacterium.
  11. 11Chocolate fondue fountain: Metronidazole treats Clostridioides difficile (pseudomembranous colitis, brown/'chocolate' watery stool) but oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin are now first-line; metronidazole is an alternative for mild-moderate disease when those are unavailable, and IV metronidazole is added with oral vancomycin for fulminant disease.⭐ Stop the offending antibiotic (clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins).
  12. 12'NO DRINKING ON METRO': Alcohol with metronidazole causes a disulfiram-like reaction (flushing, headache, tachycardia, palpitations, nausea/vomiting) due to inhibition of aldehyde dehydrogenase with acetaldehyde accumulation. Other adverse effects: metallic taste, peripheral neuropathy, CNS toxicity/seizures, and dark urine.⭐ Disulfiram-like reaction drugs: metronidazole, certain cephalosporins (cefotetan), sulfonylureas, procarbazine.
