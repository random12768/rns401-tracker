1. Psychosis — The Core Syndrome Foundational

Psychosis is a syndrome, not a disease — a distorted perception of reality that can manifest in numerous underlying conditions. The clinician's first task is to recognize psychosis; the second is to identify the etiology.

1.1 Conditions Causing Psychosis

CategoryExamplesKey Distinguishing Feature
Primary PsychiatricSchizophrenia, Schizoaffective, Brief Psychotic Disorder, Delusional DisorderCore diagnosis after exclusions
Mood DisordersBipolar I with psychotic features, MDD with psychotic featuresPsychosis only during mood episodes
Substance-InducedPCP, cocaine, amphetamines, cannabis, LSD, alcohol withdrawalTemporal relationship to substance; resolves with abstinence
Medical/NeurologicalDelirium, CNS tumors, temporal lobe epilepsy, anti-NMDA-R encephalitis, syphilis, HIV, Wilson's diseaseMedical findings on workup; often visual hallucinations
Medication-InducedCorticosteroids, levodopa, stimulants, anticholinergicsTemporal relationship to medication start

1.2 Types of Delusions (High-Yield)

Persecutory

Belief of being plotted against, harmed, or harassed. Most common type. "The FBI is monitoring my apartment."

Referential (Ideas of Reference)

Belief that external events/objects are specifically directed at oneself. "The news anchor is sending me secret messages."

Grandiose

Inflated self-worth, power, or identity. "I am the chosen one sent to save humanity."

Somatic

Fixed false belief about bodily function. Delusional infestation (Ekbom syndrome) — believes parasites crawl under skin.

Erotomanic

Belief that someone of higher status is in love with you (de Clérambault syndrome). Classic Delusional Disorder subtype.

Thought Insertion / Broadcasting

First-rank (Schneider) symptoms: thoughts are inserted by outside force, or broadcast to others. Strongly suggest schizophrenia.

2. Schizophrenia — DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Criteria Core Dx

⚠ DSM-5 vs DSM-IV-TR — Critical Difference
DSM-5 eliminated all schizophrenia subtypes (Paranoid, Disorganized, Catatonic, Undifferentiated, Residual). These are DSM-IV-TR classifications. Catatonia is now a specifier applicable across multiple diagnoses (MDD, bipolar, schizophrenia). Do NOT use old subtypes on Step 1.
CriterionRequirementClinical Notes
A — Active Symptoms≥2 of 5 symptoms for ≥1 month (or less if treated): delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, negative symptoms. At least 1 must be from the first 3.The "first 3" rule is tested! Cannot have only negative symptoms and catatonia.
B — Functional DeclineWork, interpersonal relations, or self-care markedly below prior levelRequired for schizophrenia (NOT required for schizophreniform)
C — DurationContinuous disturbance ≥6 months (including ≥1 month active-phase symptoms)Key timeline distinction from schizophreniform (1–6 months)
D — Exclusion: Schizoaffective/MoodSchizoaffective disorder and mood disorder with psychotic features are ruled outIf significant mood episode present concurrently → consider schizoaffective
E — Exclusion: Substances/MedicalSymptoms NOT due to direct physiological effects of a substance or another medical conditionAlways rule out organic causes first
💎 Step 1 Pearl — The Numbers
Schizophrenia timeline: ≥6 months total; ≥1 month active symptoms; ≥1 Criterion A symptom must be delusion, hallucination, or disorganized speech.
Schizophreniform: 1–6 months (functional decline NOT required)
Brief Psychotic Disorder: 1 day – 1 month (with or without stressor)

3. Symptom Domains Core Concept

3.1 Positive Symptoms

📋 Positive Symptoms — An Excess or Distortion of Normal Function
Delusions · Hallucinations (predominantly auditory) · Disorganized speech (word salad, loose associations, clang associations, tangentiality, circumstantiality) · Grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior
Neurobiological basis: ↑ Dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway
Treatment response: Respond well to both typical AND atypical antipsychotics (D2 blockade)

Thought Disorder Glossary

TermDefinitionExample
Word SaladRandom, incomprehensible sequence of words"Green Tuesday bicycle sings the hospital clock"
Loose AssociationsIdeas shift without logical connection (derailment)Starts talking about family, jumps to astronomy, then color of socks
Clang AssociationsWord choice driven by sound/rhyme, not meaning"I need to flee, the key, the sea, agree with me"
NeologismsMade-up words"I have 'blurpiness' in my mind"
EcholaliaPathological repetition of another's wordsDoctor: "How are you?" Patient: "How are you? How are you?"
Thought BroadcastingBelief that thoughts are audible to others — first-rank symptom"Everyone in the room can hear my thoughts"

3.2 Negative Symptoms — The 5 A's

🧠 Mnemonic — The 5 A's of Negative Symptoms
5 A's of Negative Symptoms
Affect (flat / blunted) — reduced emotional expression; flat facial expression
Alogia — poverty of speech; brief, empty replies; decreased output
Avolition — decreased motivation; inability to initiate goal-directed behavior
Anhedonia — inability to experience pleasure; loss of interest
Asociality — social withdrawal; reduced social drive and interaction
🔬 Negative Symptoms — Neurobiological Basis
Neurobiological basis: ↓ Dopamine activity in the mesocortical pathway
Treatment response: Respond POORLY to typical antipsychotics; atypical antipsychotics (5-HT2A antagonism) have relatively better effect
Prognosis: Predominantly negative symptoms = worse prognosis — often persist even when positive symptoms are controlled

3.3 Cognitive Symptoms

Cognitive Symptoms
  • Poor executive function (planning, abstract reasoning, flexibility)
  • Working memory deficits
  • Impaired attention and processing speed
  • Social cognition deficits (difficulty reading emotions, social cues)
  • Neurobiological basis: prefrontal cortex dysfunction; also mesocortical DA hypofunction
  • Often present from premorbid phase — may predate psychosis by years
  • Poorly responsive to antipsychotics; targeted by psychosocial rehabilitation

4. Neurobiology of Schizophrenia Mechanisms

4.1 The Four Dopamine Pathways

1. Mesolimbic Pathway

Route: Ventral tegmental area (VTA) → limbic system (nucleus accumbens)
In schizophrenia: ↑ DA activity → POSITIVE symptoms
Drug effect: D2 blockade here = therapeutic (reduces positive symptoms)

2. Mesocortical Pathway

Route: VTA → prefrontal cortex
In schizophrenia: ↓ DA activity → NEGATIVE + COGNITIVE symptoms
Drug effect: D2 blockade here worsens negative symptoms (typical antipsychotics)

3. Nigrostriatal Pathway

Route: Substantia nigra → striatum (basal ganglia)
In schizophrenia: Not primarily dysfunctional
Drug effect: D2 blockade here = EPS (parkinsonism, acute dystonia, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia)

4. Tuberoinfundibular Pathway

Route: Hypothalamus → anterior pituitary
Normal function: DA tonically inhibits prolactin release
Drug effect: D2 blockade → ↑ prolactin → galactorrhea, gynecomastia, amenorrhea, sexual dysfunction

💎 Step 1 Pearl — DA Pathway Summary
Mesolimbic ↑ → positive symptoms → D2 blockade = therapeutic
Mesocortical ↓ → negative/cognitive symptoms → D2 blockade = harmful (worsens)
Nigrostriatal block → EPS (motor side effects)
Tuberoinfundibular block → hyperprolactinemia

4.2 Glutamate / NMDA Hypothesis

🔬 Glutamate Hypothesis of Schizophrenia
  • NMDA receptor hypofunction is an alternative/complementary model to dopamine excess
  • PCP (phencyclidine) and Ketamine are NMDA antagonists → can induce a schizophrenia-like state with BOTH positive AND negative symptoms
  • This is why NMDA antagonism model is superior to pure DA hypothesis — it explains negative symptoms that dopamine theory does not
  • The anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis (an autoimmune condition) can also produce psychosis — important medical differential
  • Clinical implication: Ketamine dissociative anesthesia can worsen psychosis in schizophrenia patients

4.3 Serotonin Hypothesis

5-HT2A receptors modulate dopamine release. Atypical antipsychotics block both D2 AND 5-HT2A receptors. The 5-HT2A antagonism in the nigrostriatal and mesocortical pathways increases DA release locally → reduces EPS risk and partially improves negative symptoms compared to typical antipsychotics.

5. Epidemiology, Genetics & Risk Factors Epi

📊 Epidemiology

  • Lifetime prevalence: 1.5% (FA 2026; corrected from 1%)
  • Males > females in prevalence
  • Males: earlier onset (late teens–early 20s)
  • Females: later onset (late 20s–30s); better prognosis
  • Suicide: ~10% die by suicide — major cause of death
  • Substance comorbidity: very high (~50%); cannabis worsens course

🧬 Genetic Risk

  • General population: ~1.5%
  • First-degree relative: ~10%
  • Both parents affected: ~40%
  • Monozygotic (MZ) twin: ~50% — NOT 100%
  • Dizygotic (DZ) twin: ~15%
Key concept: MZ concordance of 50% confirms that genetics alone does not cause schizophrenia → stress-diathesis model
⚠ High-Yield Environmental Risk Factors
  • Adolescent cannabis use → ↑ incidence and worsened course (FA 2026 — frequently tested)
  • Obstetric complications: Prenatal infections (influenza 2nd trimester), birth hypoxia, maternal malnutrition
  • Urban upbringing and social disadvantage → ↑ risk
  • Migration (social defeat hypothesis) → ↑ risk in immigrant populations
  • Season of birth: winter/spring birth → slight ↑ risk (possible prenatal viral exposure)
  • Neurodevelopmental origin: premorbid cognitive/motor/social deficits often present years before onset

6.1 Prognostic Factors

✅ Better Prognosis

  • Female sex
  • Late age of onset
  • Acute / abrupt onset
  • Predominantly positive symptoms
  • Good premorbid functioning
  • Married / strong social support
  • Good treatment compliance
  • No family history of schizophrenia
  • No substance use comorbidity

🚨 Worse Prognosis (FA 2026)

  • Male sex
  • Early onset / gradual (insidious) onset
  • Predominantly negative symptoms
  • Poor premorbid functioning
  • Strong family history of psychosis
  • Long Duration of Untreated Psychosis (DUP)
  • Social isolation
  • Substance use comorbidity
  • Ventriculomegaly on imaging

8. Treatment of Psychotic Disorders Treatment

📋 General Treatment Principles
  • First-line: Atypical (second-generation) antipsychotics for most patients
  • Biopsychosocial approach: Medications + CBT + Social skills training + Family therapy + Psychoeducation
  • Acute agitation: Benzodiazepines + antipsychotics (IM haloperidol + lorazepam commonly used)
  • Compliance: Long-acting injectable (LAI) antipsychotics for poor compliance (e.g., paliperidone palmitate, aripiprazole LAI)
  • Antidepressants can be added if comorbid depression (common)

8.1 Typical (First-Generation) Antipsychotics

PotencyExamplesD2 BlockadeEPS RiskAnti-HAM Effects
High-PotencyHaloperidol, Fluphenazine, Trifluoperazine++++HIGHLow (less sedation/orthostasis)
Low-PotencyChlorpromazine, Thioridazine++LowerHIGH anti-HAM: sedation, orthostasis, dry mouth, urinary retention

Anti-Muscarinic → dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, tachycardia

8.2 Atypical (Second-Generation) Antipsychotics

📋 Mechanism: D2 Antagonism + 5-HT2A Antagonism
The dual blockade of D2 and 5-HT2A receptors accounts for:
  • Lower EPS risk (5-HT2A antagonism in nigrostriatal → DA release → offsets D2 blockade)
  • Relatively better efficacy for negative symptoms
  • BUT greater metabolic side effects (weight gain, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia → metabolic syndrome)
DrugKey Feature / Side EffectUSMLE Niche
Risperidone / PaliperidoneMost EPS among atypicals; significant hyperprolactinemiaLowest metabolic effects among atypicals; LAI formulation available
OlanzapineHighest metabolic risk (weight gain, DM, dyslipidemia)"Olanzapine makes you olanzafat"
QuetiapineSignificant sedation; moderate metabolic riskOften used for insomnia/anxiety (off-label); avoid in narrow-angle glaucoma
AripiprazolePartial D2 agonist (unique!); low metabolic and EPS risk"Aripiprazole is a partial agonist" — does NOT cause hyperprolactinemia

8.3 Clozapine — Treatment-Resistant Schizophrenia

🚨 Clozapine — Indications & Critical Monitoring
Indications:
  • Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failed ≥2 adequate antipsychotic trials)
  • Persistent suicidality in schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder
Mechanism: Weak D2 antagonism + strong D4, 5-HT2A, M1, H1 antagonism
🚨 Agranulocytosis: Life-threatening — requires mandatory ANC monitoring (weekly ×6 months → biweekly → monthly). REMS program.
"Monitor WBCs CloZEly"
Other SE: Weight gain (severe), seizures (dose-dependent), myocarditis, hypersalivation (sialorrhea), sedation, no significant EPS or tardive dyskinesia

9. Antipsychotic Adverse Effects — ADAPT Framework Safety

🧠 Mnemonic — ADAPT (EPS Timeline)
Extrapyramidal Symptoms in Order of Onset
Acute Dystonia — Hours to days — sustained muscle contractions; oculogyric crisis; torticollis. Rx: Benztropine or Diphenhydramine (IM)
Drug-induced Parkinsonism — Days to weeks — bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, shuffling gait. Rx: Benztropine or Amantadine
Akathisia — Days to months — subjective restlessness, inner urge to move; patient can't sit still. Rx: Propranolol (β-blocker) 1st line; Benzodiazepines; lower dose
Prolactinemia — Weeks to months — galactorrhea, gynecomastia, amenorrhea, sexual dysfunction
Tardive Dyskinesia — Months to years — involuntary choreiform movements; orofacial (lip-smacking, tongue protrusion). Rx: VMAT2 inhibitors: Valbenazine or Deutetrabenazine. Reduce/switch antipsychotic.
💎 EPS Distinction — Must Know
Akathisia vs. Parkinsonism vs. Tardive Dyskinesia:
Akathisia = subjective inner restlessness + motor (pacing); patient FEELS restless → Propranolol
Parkinsonism = SLOWED movement (bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity) → Benztropine
Tardive Dyskinesia = LATE-onset involuntary movements (orofacial, choreoathetoid); may be irreversible → VMAT2 inhibitor
Do NOT give benztropine for TD (anticholinergic may worsen TD)

9.1 Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) — Psychiatric Emergency

🚨 NMS — Life-Threatening Emergency
Cause: Any antipsychotic (but especially high-potency typicals like haloperidol); also rapid dose increases
Mechanism: Extreme dopamine blockade in hypothalamus (temperature regulation) + nigrostriatal (rigidity)
🧠 Mnemonic — FEVER
NMS Features
Fever — hyperthermia (38–40°C or higher)
Encephalopathy — altered mental status, confusion, decreased consciousness
Vitals unstable — tachycardia, labile blood pressure, diaphoresis
Enzymes ↑ — markedly elevated CK (rhabdomyolysis) → myoglobinuria → AKI
Rigidity — "lead-pipe" rigidity (vs. cog-wheel in drug-induced Parkinsonism)
FeatureNMSSerotonin Syndrome
CauseAntipsychotics (dopamine blockade)Serotonergic agents (SSRIs + MAOIs; linezolid; tramadol + SSRI)
OnsetDays to weeks (gradual)Hours (rapid — 24 hrs)
RigidityLead-pipe rigidityHyperreflexia, myoclonus (NOT lead-pipe)
FeverHyperthermia (often severe)Hyperthermia (often severe)
ReflexesNormal or decreasedHyperreflexia; clonus
TreatmentSTOP antipsychotic; Dantrolene + Bromocriptine (DA agonist); cooling, supportive careSTOP serotonergic agent; Cyproheptadine (5-HT antagonist); cooling, benzos

MCQ 1Lecture 8a · Duration-Based Diagnosis — Schizophreniform vs Others

A 28-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by his family due to increasingly bizarre behavior over the past 3 months. He claims that television news anchors are sending him secret messages through their broadcasts and believes his thoughts are being broadcasted to others. He has also been observed talking to himself and has stopped attending work. His family reports no significant mood disturbances or history of substance abuse. His symptoms have been present consistently for this 3-month period. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

  • A. Brief Psychotic Disorder
  • B. Schizophreniform Disorder
  • C. Schizophrenia
  • D. Delusional Disorder
  • E. Schizoaffective Disorder
✅ B — Schizophreniform Disorder

Key reasoning: This patient has classic positive symptoms — delusions of reference ("news anchors sending secret messages"), thought broadcasting (first-rank Schneiderian symptom), probable auditory hallucinations ("talking to himself"), and functional decline (stopped attending work). Duration = 3 months.

  • Schizophreniform = same Criterion A as schizophrenia, but duration 1–6 months. At 3 months, this patient does NOT yet meet schizophrenia's ≥6-month threshold.
  • Why not Brief Psychotic? Brief psychotic disorder lasts <1 month. This patient has had 3 months of symptoms.
  • Why not Schizophrenia? Requires ≥6 months of continuous disturbance. Only 3 months here.
  • Why not Delusional Disorder? Delusional disorder = isolated delusion with daily functioning OTHERWISE INTACT. This patient has multiple positive symptoms and functional decline.
  • Why not Schizoaffective? No mood episodes reported; no evidence of concurrent major depressive or manic episode.

Educational Objective: The schizophrenia spectrum disorders are differentiated primarily by DURATION: <1 month (Brief) → 1–6 months (Schizophreniform) → ≥6 months (Schizophrenia). Know the timeline cold.

❌ Common Traps

The most common error is jumping to Schizophrenia when you see classic positive symptoms. Always calculate the duration first. If duration <6 months → Schizophreniform until proven otherwise. The question explicitly states "3-month period."

MCQ 2Lecture 8a · Symptom Classification & Dopamine Pathways

A 45-year-old woman with a long-standing diagnosis of schizophrenia presents with a blunted affect, poverty of speech, and a profound lack of motivation to engage in daily activities or social interactions. Her family notes these symptoms have been persistent despite treatment for her auditory hallucinations, which are currently well-controlled. These symptoms are most accurately described as:

  • A. Positive symptoms, related to mesocortical dopamine hyperactivity
  • B. Negative symptoms, related to mesolimbic dopamine hypoactivity
  • C. Positive symptoms, related to mesolimbic dopamine hyperactivity
  • D. Negative symptoms, related to mesocortical dopamine hypoactivity
  • E. Cognitive symptoms, related to nigrostriatal pathway dysfunction
✅ D — Negative Symptoms / Mesocortical Dopamine Hypoactivity

Symptoms identified: Blunted affect (flat affect), alogia (poverty of speech), and avolition (lack of motivation) = classic 3 of the 5 A's of negative symptoms.

  • Negative symptoms → ↓ Dopamine activity in the mesocortical pathway (VTA → prefrontal cortex)
  • Positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) → ↑ DA in the mesolimbic pathway (VTA → limbic/nucleus accumbens)
  • Her hallucinations responded to treatment (D2 blockade in mesolimbic) but negative symptoms persist — because D2 blockade in mesocortical pathway can actually worsen negative symptoms with typical antipsychotics

Why not E? Nigrostriatal = motor control → D2 blockade there = EPS (parkinsonism, dystonia). Cognitive symptoms are also related to mesocortical but are a distinct domain.

Educational Objective: Master the 4 dopamine pathways. Negative symptoms = mesocortical hypofunction. Atypical antipsychotics (5-HT2A antagonism) have relatively better efficacy for negative symptoms.

❌ Common Traps

Confusing MESO-LIMBIC (positive) with MESO-CORTICAL (negative). Memory trick: "LIMbic = LIMited to positive symptoms only." Mesocortical goes to the CORTEX — that's where higher-order, cognitive, and negative functions live.

MCQ 3Lecture 8a · Prognostic Factors in Schizophrenia

A 22-year-old male is diagnosed with schizophrenia. His symptoms began insidiously at age 17, primarily with social withdrawal and odd beliefs, which progressed to overt psychosis. He has predominantly negative symptoms and a strong family history of schizophrenia. His premorbid functioning was poor. Based on these factors, what is his likely prognosis?

  • A. Good, due to early onset
  • B. Poor, due to acute onset
  • C. Good, due to predominantly negative symptoms
  • D. Poor, due to gradual onset and predominantly negative symptoms
  • E. Good, due to strong family history
✅ D — Poor Prognosis

This patient has stacked multiple poor prognostic factors:

  • 🔴 Early onset (age 17) → poor prognosis
  • 🔴 Gradual/insidious onset → poor prognosis (vs. acute onset = better)
  • 🔴 Predominantly negative symptoms → poor prognosis (respond poorly to treatment)
  • 🔴 Poor premorbid functioning → poor prognosis
  • 🔴 Strong family history → worse prognosis (high genetic loading)
  • 🟢 Good prognosis markers (ABSENT here): female sex, late onset, acute onset, positive symptoms, good premorbid function, social support

Educational Objective: Know all prognostic factors — USMLE frequently gives a patient profile and asks to predict prognosis. Early + insidious + negative + poor premorbid = worst prognosis profile.

❌ Common Traps

Early onset = WORSE prognosis (not better). Strong family history = WORSE prognosis. Students often intuitively reverse these. Also: negative symptoms = worse prognosis because they are treatment-resistant — not better.

MCQ 4Lecture 8a · Delusion Classification — Somatic Delusion

A 30-year-old woman presents to her physician convinced that she is infested with parasites crawling under her skin, despite multiple dermatological examinations revealing no evidence of infestation. She has no other psychotic symptoms, her mood is euthymic, and her daily functioning, apart from her distress about the "infestation," is largely intact. This specific belief is best classified as:

  • A. Persecutory delusion
  • B. Grandiose delusion
  • C. Somatic delusion
  • D. Erotomanic delusion
  • E. Tactile hallucination
✅ C — Somatic Delusion (Delusional Infestation / Ekbom Syndrome)

Somatic delusion = a fixed, false belief about bodily function or sensations. Delusional infestation (Ekbom syndrome) is the classic somatic delusion — unwavering belief of being infested by parasites despite no evidence.

Why not tactile hallucination (E)? The question emphasizes the belief of infestation as the core problem. A tactile hallucination = perception of sensation without stimulus. Delusional disorder focuses on the fixed false belief, not the sensory experience. In clinical practice, both may coexist, but on USMLE, the primary feature described is the belief → delusion.

Diagnosis here: Delusional Disorder, Somatic type — she has one fixed delusion, daily functioning is OTHERWISE INTACT (she still functions), no other psychotic symptoms, mood is euthymic.

Educational Objective: Know all delusion subtypes and their classic presentations. Somatic delusions about infestation (Ekbom syndrome) or bodily abnormality are classic USMLE favorites.

❌ Common Traps

Choice E (tactile hallucination) is the most common distractor. Remember: the question emphasizes the BELIEF, not a perceived sensation. Also, this presentation matches Delusional Disorder (not schizophrenia) because daily functioning is intact and there are no other psychotic symptoms.

MCQ 5Lecture 8a · Schizoaffective Disorder — The 2-Week Rule

A 35-year-old man with a history of bipolar disorder experiences a 3-week period of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, and racing thoughts, consistent with a manic episode. During this same 3-week period, he also develops auditory hallucinations and delusions of grandeur. His psychiatrist notes that for the past year, even during periods when his mood was stable and euthymic, he has experienced persistent auditory hallucinations for at least 4 weeks. Which diagnosis best fits this clinical picture?

  • A. Schizophrenia, paranoid type
  • B. Bipolar I Disorder with psychotic features
  • C. Schizoaffective Disorder, bipolar type
  • D. Brief Psychotic Disorder
  • E. Major Depressive Disorder with psychotic features
✅ C — Schizoaffective Disorder, Bipolar Type

The critical rule for schizoaffective disorder: Psychotic symptoms must occur for ≥2 weeks in the ABSENCE of a major mood episode during the overall course of the illness. This patient satisfies both components:

  • ✅ Concurrent manic episode + psychosis (hallucinations + delusions) during the 3-week manic period
  • ✅ Persistent hallucinations for 4 weeks when mood was euthymic (stable, no mood episode) — this satisfies the ≥2-week standalone psychosis requirement

Why not Bipolar I with psychotic features (B)? Bipolar I with psychotic features = psychosis occurs ONLY during mood episodes. This patient has psychosis that persists independently of mood episodes (4 weeks euthymic with hallucinations) → Schizoaffective, not Bipolar with psychosis.

Why not Schizophrenia? Note that "paranoid type" no longer exists in DSM-5 (DSM-IV-TR subtype). Also, he has prominent mood episodes that are a major feature of his illness — pointing to schizoaffective over pure schizophrenia.

Educational Objective: The schizoaffective 2-week rule: psychosis must occur for ≥2 weeks without mood episodes. If psychosis only occurs with mood episodes → mood disorder with psychotic features.

❌ Common Traps

Choice A says "paranoid type" — this was eliminated in DSM-5 and is immediately recognizable as a wrong answer. Choice B (Bipolar with psychotic features) is correct ONLY if psychosis is always tied to mood episodes. Here, 4 weeks of euthymic hallucinations disqualify Bipolar + psychosis and require schizoaffective diagnosis.

MCQ 6Lecture 8b · Mechanism of Typical Antipsychotics

A psychiatrist is treating a patient with schizophrenia who is experiencing significant positive symptoms, including auditory hallucinations and paranoid delusions. The psychiatrist decides to start a first-generation (typical) antipsychotic. What is the primary mechanism of action responsible for the efficacy of this class of medication against these symptoms?

  • A. Antagonism of 5-HT2A receptors in the mesocortical pathway
  • B. Agonism of D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway
  • C. Antagonism of D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway
  • D. Antagonism of D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway
  • E. Potentiation of GABAergic transmission
✅ C — D2 Antagonism in the Mesolimbic Pathway

Typical antipsychotics (haloperidol, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine, etc.) exert their antipsychotic therapeutic effect by blocking D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway. Positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) are caused by excess dopamine activity in this pathway (VTA → nucleus accumbens).

  • A (5-HT2A antagonism in mesocortical) = mechanism of atypical antipsychotics — not typicals
  • B (D2 agonism) = wrong direction — typical antipsychotics are ANTAGONISTS (blockers)
  • D (D2 antagonism in nigrostriatal) = also occurs with typicals, but this causes EPS (side effect), NOT the therapeutic benefit
  • E (GABA potentiation) = benzodiazepines, barbiturates — not antipsychotics

Educational Objective: Therapeutic effect of typical antipsychotics = D2 blockade in MESOLIMBIC pathway → reduced positive symptoms. D2 blockade in nigrostriatal → EPS (unwanted). Know which pathway produces which effect.

❌ Common Traps

Answer D (nigrostriatal D2 blockade) is the most dangerous distractor — it is a real effect of typical antipsychotics but produces the SIDE EFFECT (EPS), not the therapeutic benefit. The therapeutic benefit lives in the MESOLIMBIC pathway. These two are commonly confused.

MCQ 7Lecture 8b · Tardive Dyskinesia — Recognition & Treatment

A 55-year-old male patient with chronic schizophrenia has been treated with haloperidol for many years. He presents to his outpatient clinic with involuntary, repetitive movements of his lips, tongue, and jaw (lip-smacking and chewing motions). He is unaware of these movements. Which of the following adverse effects is most likely responsible for these symptoms?

  • A. Acute dystonia
  • B. Akathisia
  • C. Drug-induced Parkinsonism
  • D. Tardive Dyskinesia
  • E. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome
✅ D — Tardive Dyskinesia (TD)

Classic TD presentation: Long-term typical antipsychotic use (years of haloperidol) + involuntary, repetitive orofacial movements (lip-smacking, chewing, tongue protrusion) + patient is UNAWARE of the movements.

ADAPT timeline to distinguish EPS:

  • A — Acute Dystonia: Hours to days; sustained painful muscle contractions (torticollis, oculogyric crisis); treated with IM benztropine
  • B — Akathisia: Days–months; SUBJECTIVE restlessness (inner urge to move, pacing); patient KNOWS they're restless; treated with propranolol
  • C — Drug-induced Parkinsonism: Days–weeks; slowing, tremor, rigidity; treated with benztropine/amantadine
  • D — Tardive Dyskinesia: Months–years of antipsychotic exposure; involuntary choreiform movements; orofacial; may be irreversible; treat with VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine or deutetrabenazine)
  • E — NMS: Emergency with fever, rigidity, AMS, elevated CK — totally different presentation

Educational Objective: Tardive Dyskinesia = late-onset, involuntary orofacial movements from long-term DA blockade (dopamine receptor upregulation). Treatment: switch to lower-potency or atypical; add VMAT2 inhibitor (valbenazine). Do NOT give benztropine (may worsen TD).

❌ Common Traps

"Long-term" and "unaware of movements" are the key TD clues. Akathisia is OPPOSITE — patient is very aware and distressed. Parkinsonism causes SLOWING. Benztropine is correct for acute dystonia and parkinsonism but is WRONG for TD (and may worsen it by further reducing cholinergic tone).

MCQ 8Lecture 8b · Atypical Antipsychotics — Mechanism of Advantage

A 30-year-old patient with schizophrenia experiences both positive symptoms (hallucinations) and significant negative symptoms (avolition, social withdrawal). Her physician is considering an atypical (second-generation) antipsychotic. Compared to typical antipsychotics, atypical antipsychotics generally offer an advantage in treating negative symptoms due to what additional mechanism of action?

  • A. Stronger D2 receptor antagonism
  • B. 5-HT2A receptor antagonism
  • C. Muscarinic receptor agonism
  • D. Histamine H1 receptor antagonism
  • E. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptor antagonism
✅ B — 5-HT2A Receptor Antagonism

Atypical antipsychotics have dual mechanism: D2 antagonism + 5-HT2A antagonism. The 5-HT2A blockade provides advantages over typicals:

  • Lower EPS risk: 5-HT2A blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway increases DA release locally → offsets D2 blockade → less EPS
  • Better negative symptoms: 5-HT2A blockade in the mesocortical pathway releases DA → improves dopamine tone in prefrontal cortex → relatively better negative/cognitive symptoms
  • Why not A (stronger D2)? Atypicals are actually WEAKER D2 antagonists than high-potency typicals like haloperidol. Less D2 blockade, not more, is part of why they have fewer EPS.
  • C (Muscarinic agonism): Xanomeline-trospium (Cobenfy) IS a muscarinic agonist (M1/M4) — new 2024 FDA approval. But "adding" muscarinic agonism is not the standard atypical mechanism.
  • D (H1) and E (α1): These cause SIDE EFFECTS (sedation, orthostasis) — they are not therapeutic mechanisms for negative symptoms.

Educational Objective: Atypical antipsychotics = D2 + 5-HT2A antagonism. The 5-HT2A component explains lower EPS risk and relatively better effect on negative symptoms compared to typicals.

❌ Common Traps

Answer A (stronger D2) is the most common mistake — atypicals are actually weaker D2 antagonists, which is WHY they cause less EPS. Also, H1 antagonism (D) and α1 antagonism (E) cause sedation and orthostasis respectively — these are unwanted side effects, not benefits for negative symptoms.

MCQ 9Lecture 8b · Clozapine — Agranulocytosis Monitoring

A 40-year-old patient with treatment-resistant schizophrenia is started on clozapine after failing trials of two other antipsychotic medications. Which of the following potential adverse effects requires regular, close monitoring with this specific medication?

  • A. Hyperprolactinemia
  • B. Acute dystonia
  • C. Agranulocytosis
  • D. Significant weight loss
  • E. Tardive dyskinesia
✅ C — Agranulocytosis

Clozapine is indicated for treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failed ≥2 adequate antipsychotic trials). Its most critical and potentially life-threatening side effect is agranulocytosis (severe drop in neutrophil/ANC count → life-threatening infection risk).

Monitoring protocol: Mandatory absolute neutrophil count (ANC) monitoring — Weekly ×26 weeks → Biweekly ×26 weeks → Monthly thereafter. Part of the REMS program. "Monitor WBCs CloZEly"

  • A — Hyperprolactinemia: Less common with clozapine (vs. typical antipsychotics and risperidone) — not the PRIMARY monitoring concern
  • B — Acute dystonia: Very low risk with clozapine due to weak D2 binding
  • D — Weight loss: OPPOSITE — clozapine causes significant weight GAIN (metabolic syndrome)
  • E — Tardive dyskinesia: Very rare with clozapine (one of its advantages over typicals)

Educational Objective: Clozapine = treatment-resistant schizophrenia + suicidality. Must-know side effect: agranulocytosis requires mandatory WBC/ANC monitoring. The blood monitoring is the defining safety feature of clozapine use.

❌ Common Traps

Students often select metabolic effects (hyperglycemia, weight gain) because they are common with clozapine. However, the question asks what requires "regular, close monitoring" — agranulocytosis is the MOST CRITICAL (life-threatening) and the reason for the mandatory REMS monitoring program specific to clozapine.

MCQ 10Lecture 8b · First-Line Pharmacologic Treatment of Psychosis

A 25-year-old female is diagnosed with her first episode of psychosis, characterized by delusions and hallucinations. After ruling out substance-induced or medical causes, schizophrenia is suspected. According to general treatment principles for psychotic disorders, which of the following is considered the mainstay of pharmacologic treatment?

  • A. Benzodiazepines
  • B. Antidepressants (SSRIs)
  • C. Antipsychotics
  • D. Mood stabilizers (e.g., lithium)
  • E. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)
✅ C — Antipsychotics

Antipsychotics (both typical and atypical) are the cornerstone of pharmacological treatment for all primary psychotic disorders including schizophrenia. For a first episode, atypical (second-generation) antipsychotics are preferred as first-line due to the more favorable side effect profile.

  • A — Benzodiazepines: Used ADJUNCTIVELY for acute agitation (IM lorazepam for combative patient in ED) but are NOT the mainstay and have no long-term antipsychotic effect
  • B — SSRIs: May be added for comorbid depression in schizophrenia patients but are NOT antipsychotic agents
  • D — Mood stabilizers: First-line for bipolar disorder; may augment in schizoaffective (bipolar type) but are NOT the mainstay for schizophrenia
  • E — ECT: Reserved for treatment-resistant cases, MDD with psychosis, catatonia, acute suicidality; not first-line for uncomplicated first-episode psychosis

Treatment Pearl: Complete biopsychosocial approach = antipsychotics + CBT for psychosis + social skills training + family psychoeducation + coordinated specialty care (for first-episode psychosis programs).

Educational Objective: Antipsychotics are the pharmacological mainstay for psychotic disorders. Atypical antipsychotics are preferred first-line for most patients, especially in first-episode psychosis.

❌ Common Traps

Benzodiazepines (A) are useful adjuncts for acute agitation but are NOT antipsychotic. This is a frequent distractor. ECT (E) is an important treatment but is reserved for treatment-resistant or emergency situations — not first-line for a stable first-episode presentation.

Master Rapid Review All Topics

⚡ Schizophrenia & Psychotic Disorders — Must-Know Facts
  • Schizophrenia DSM-5: ≥2 Criterion A symptoms (≥1 from delusions/hallucinations/disorganized speech); ≥1 month active; ≥6 months total; functional decline; no subtypes in DSM-5
  • Positive symptoms = ↑ Mesolimbic DA → respond to D2 blockade (typicals + atypicals)
  • Negative symptoms (5 A's) = ↓ Mesocortical DA → respond better to atypicals (5-HT2A antagonism)
  • Cognitive symptoms = prefrontal/mesocortical dysfunction; targeted by psychosocial rehab
  • Prevalence: 1.5% (FA 2026). Males onset earlier; worse prognosis. MZ twin ~50% concordance.
  • Cannabis: adolescent use → ↑ incidence + worsened course (FA 2026 — frequently tested)
  • Duration spectrum: Brief <1 month → Schizophreniform 1–6 months → Schizophrenia ≥6 months
  • Schizoaffective rule: Psychosis must occur ≥2 weeks WITHOUT a mood episode. If only with mood → mood disorder with psychotic features
  • Delusional Disorder: ≥1 month isolated delusion; function OTHERWISE INTACT; no hallucinations or disorganized speech
  • Folie à deux = Shared Delusional Disorder — one is the primary inducer
  • Glutamate/NMDA: PCP + Ketamine = NMDA antagonists → mimic BOTH positive AND negative symptoms
  • EPS ADAPT: Acute Dystonia (hrs) → Parkinsonism (days–wks) → Akathisia (days–months) → Tardive Dyskinesia (months–years)
  • Akathisia Rx: Propranolol (β-blocker) first-line. NOT benztropine.
  • TD Rx: VMAT2 inhibitors (Valbenazine or Deutetrabenazine). NOT benztropine (worsens TD).
  • NMS: FEVER mnemonic; lead-pipe rigidity; ↑CK; treat with dantrolene + bromocriptine. Stop antipsychotic.
  • NMS vs Serotonin Syndrome: NMS = lead-pipe rigidity + normal reflexes; SS = hyperreflexia + clonus
  • Clozapine: Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failed ≥2 trials) + suicidality → agranulocytosis = mandatory WBC monitoring (REMS)
  • Xanomeline-Trospium (2024): First non-dopaminergic antipsychotic; M1/M4 muscarinic agonist; no EPS, no TD, no hyperprolactinemia
  • Anti-HAM (low-potency typicals): Antihistaminergic (sedation) + Anti-α1 (orthostasis) + Antimuscarinic (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention)
  • Chlorpromazine → corneal deposits; Thioridazine → retinal pigmentation (pigmentary retinopathy)
  • Aripiprazole: Partial D2 agonist (unique) → no hyperprolactinemia; lowest metabolic risk
  • Olanzapine: Highest metabolic risk ("Olanzafat"); Ziprasidone → QTc prolongation
