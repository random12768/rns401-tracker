1. Escherichia coli (ETEC & EHEC)

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: UTI (cystitis/pyelonephritis), neonatal meningitis (K1), gram-negative sepsis; EHEC = bloody diarrhea + HUS; ETEC = traveler's watery diarrhea.
  • Lactose fermenter: pink on MacConkey, metallic green sheen on EMB; oxidase negative, indole positive.
  • EHEC O157:H7: sorbitol-negative (colorless on sorbitol-MacConkey), Shiga-like toxin (inactivates 60S ribosome), undercooked beef.
  • HUS triad: thrombocytopenia, MAHA with schistocytes, acute kidney injury; do NOT give antibiotics for EHEC.
  • ETEC: LT (like cholera, increases cAMP) and ST (increases cGMP) cause watery diarrhea; treat with fluids.
  1. 1E. cola: Escherichia coli is a gram-negative bacillus (pink rod on Gram stain) and a normal member of gut flora, but it is also the leading cause of several infections. It is a facultative anaerobe in the Enterobacteriaceae family.
  2. 2Red hues: Red/pink coloring reminds you that E. coli is gram-negative: it has a thin peptidoglycan layer with an outer membrane containing LPS (endotoxin), which stains pink with safranin counterstain.
  3. 3Pink milk carton: E. coli ferments lactose, forming pink colonies on MacConkey agar (acid from lactose fermentation turns the pH indicator pink). Other lactose fermenters include Klebsiella and Enterobacter.
  4. 4Glass capsule: Many strains of E. coli are surrounded by a polysaccharide capsule, which helps them evade phagocytosis.
  5. 5K cake + glass capsule: Some strains express K capsule (K antigen), a key virulence factor in extraintestinal infections (e.g., sepsis, meningitis, UTI) because it resists phagocytosis.
  6. 6Green coaster: E. coli typically forms distinctive metallic green colonies on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar; the metallic sheen is a classic buzzword for strong lactose fermentation.
  7. 7Catalase cat: E. coli is catalase positive: catalase converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, protecting the bacterium from oxidative killing. Catalase-positive organisms are particularly a problem in chronic granulomatous disease.
  8. 8Fimbriae ribbons: Fimbriae (pili) of E. coli mediate adhesion to host cells, an important virulence factor. P fimbriae bind uroepithelium and are linked to pyelonephritis, preventing the bacteria from being washed out by urine flow.
  9. 9"1" bladder cup: E. coli is the most common cause of urinary tract infections (cystitis and pyelonephritis), usually by ascending spread from the gut flora. It is the most common cause of uncomplicated UTI.
  10. 10Blood red shake: E. coli is a major cause of gram-negative sepsis. LPS (endotoxin, lipid A) triggers release of TNF-α and IL-1, leading to fever, hypotension and septic shock; urinary tract and GI/biliary sources are common.
  11. 11K cake + baby + meningitis helmet: Strains with the K1 capsular antigen cause neonatal meningitis, acquired from the maternal birth canal.⭐ Neonatal meningitis: think group B Strep, E. coli (K1), and Listeria.
  12. 12Hamburger: Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) is classically transmitted through undercooked ground beef (also unpasteurized juice and raw produce). The infectious dose is very low.⭐ Undercooked burger + bloody diarrhea = EHEC (O157:H7).
  13. 13Red stool: EHEC causes bloody diarrhea (hemorrhagic colitis) by damaging the intestinal lining through Shiga-like toxin production. Fever is typically absent or low-grade, and fecal leukocytes are scant.⭐ Antibiotics and antimotility agents are avoided in EHEC because they can raise the risk of HUS.
  14. 14Sorbitol free soda: O157:H7 does not ferment sorbitol, so it forms colorless colonies on sorbitol-MacConkey agar, distinguishing it from most other E. coli strains, which are sorbitol fermenters.
  15. 15She-gorilla toy: EHEC produces Shiga-like toxin (Shiga toxin 1/2, phage-encoded), nearly identical to the Shiga toxin of Shigella dysenteriae. It inactivates the 60S ribosomal subunit (cleaves 28S rRNA) in host cells, disrupting protein synthesis and killing the cell.
  16. 16Glomerular straw: Shiga-like toxin damages endothelial cells of glomerular capillaries and causes hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). This endothelial injury leads to acute kidney injury; HUS usually follows the diarrheal illness by about a week, mainly in children.
  17. 17Plate aggregation: Endothelial damage exposes the surface to platelets, triggering platelet aggregation and microthrombi. Platelet consumption causes thrombocytopenia (the classic HUS triad is thrombocytopenia, MAHA and acute kidney injury).⭐ HUS: low platelets but normal PT/PTT (unlike DIC); schistocytes on smear.
  18. 18Popped red balloon: In microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), red cells are mechanically sheared into schistocytes as they pass through small vessels filled with fibrin/platelet clots, lowering the red cell count. Labs show elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and a negative Coombs test.
  19. 19"Only $1.57": E. coli O157:H7 is the classic enterohemorrhagic serotype: Shiga-like toxin causes bloody diarrhea and HUS. The "$1.57" in the picture is a mnemonic for O157. Management is supportive (fluids); avoid antibiotics.
  20. 20Water truck: Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) is a common cause of traveler's diarrhea, acquired by ingesting contaminated water or food. It causes watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping, nausea and vomiting; it colonizes the small bowel via fimbriae (colonization factors) and does not invade.
  21. 21eL Agua: Heat-labile toxin (LT, mnemonic: eL = Labile) ADP-ribosylates Gs, increasing adenylyl cyclase activity, so cAMP rises, inhibiting sodium/chloride absorption and promoting chloride and water secretion, causing secretory (watery) diarrhea. It works like cholera toxin.
  22. 22San Gabriel: Heat-stable toxin (ST, mnemonic: San Gabriel = Stable, Guanylate) activates guanylate cyclase, raising cGMP, increasing chloride secretion and reducing sodium absorption, causing secretory (watery) diarrhea.⭐ LT = cAMP (like cholera); ST = cGMP. Remember: LT is Labile (heat-inactivated), ST is Stable.
  23. 23Spilling water: ETEC causes traveler's diarrhea: watery diarrhea, abdominal cramping, nausea and vomiting, with no blood and no significant inflammation. It is usually self-limited and treated with oral rehydration; antibiotics (e.g., rifaximin, azithromycin, fluoroquinolone) are used in more severe cases.

2. Proteus mirabilis

Exam snapshot
  • Clinical clue: complicated UTI with alkaline urine, flank pain, and staghorn calculi.
  • Microbiology: gram-negative bacillus, lactose non-fermenter, urease positive, highly motile (swarming on agar), H2S producer.
  • Diagnosis: urine culture; alkaline urine pH; struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) stones, coffin-lid crystals, on imaging/urinalysis.
  • Treatment: antibiotics (e.g., trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for uncomplicated UTI); large stones often need urologic removal because bacteria persist in the stone.
  • Buzzwords: 'swarming', 'urease', 'struvite', 'staghorn calculi', 'alkaline urine', 'fishy/ammonia odor'.
  1. 1Proteus: Proteus mirabilis, a gram-negative bacillus.
  2. 2Red hues: Red hues = gram-negative: Proteus mirabilis is a gram-negative bacillus (lactose non-fermenter).
  3. 3Swarming tentacles: Proteus mirabilis shows swarming motility on agar plates, spreading in concentric waves (a clue to its flagellar motility).⭐ 'Swarming' on culture is a classic buzzword.
  4. 4Staghorn helmet: Urease-positive bacteria like Proteus can cause staghorn calculi (struvite stones) that fill the renal pelvis and calyces like stag horns, causing flank pain and renal impairment and harboring bacteria.⭐ Staghorn calculus = think Proteus.
  5. 5Ammonia bottle: Proteus produces urease, which hydrolyzes urea into CO2 and ammonia, raising urine pH. The alkaline urine promotes precipitation of magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite) stones.
  6. 6Stone: Struvite stones cause pain and urinary tract damage and act as a nidus for recurrent Proteus infections, since bacteria hide within the stone.⭐ Urine pH is alkaline (>7).
  7. 7Peeing: Proteus mirabilis is a common cause of urinary tract infections, especially complicated cases or those with abnormal urinary tracts or catheters.
  8. 8Stinky fish: Proteus mirabilis produces a characteristic fishy (ammonia) odor.
  9. 9Eggs: Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (a sulfonamide combination) is active against Proteus mirabilis and commonly used for uncomplicated UTIs; the egg is a cue for the antibiotic choice.⭐ Fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins are other options.

3. Staphylococcus epidermidis & saprophyticus

Exam snapshot
  • Clinical clue: S. epidermidis - prosthetic valve/joint or catheter infection; S. saprophyticus - UTI in a young sexually active woman.
  • Micro: gram-positive cocci in clusters, catalase positive, coagulase negative; S. epidermidis is novobiocin sensitive, S. saprophyticus is novobiocin resistant.
  • Diagnosis: culture; repeated positive blood cultures for epidermidis; urinalysis and culture for saprophyticus.
  • Treatment/prevention: S. epidermidis - vancomycin (often methicillin resistant), remove device; S. saprophyticus - TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin.
  • Buzzwords: biofilm, blood culture contaminant, honeymoon cystitis, novobiocin, coagulase negative.
  1. 1Plumber: Staphylococcus epidermidis is a gram-positive coccus.
  2. 2Sappy couple: Staphylococcus saprophyticus is a gram-positive coccus.
  3. 3Purple hues: S. epidermidis and S. saprophyticus are gram-positive cocci (purple = gram-positive).
  4. 4Catalase cat: Both produce catalase (catalase positive), an enzyme that converts hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen.⭐ Catalase positive separates staphylococci from streptococci.
  5. 5Runny juice: Both are coagulase negative (do not convert fibrinogen to fibrin, so no clot); the coagulase test distinguishes them from coagulase-positive S. aureus.⭐ They are the "coagulase-negative staphylococci".
  6. 6Ammonia spray: Both produce urease (urease positive), hydrolyzing urea into carbon dioxide and ammonia.
  7. 7Plumber's exposed navel: S. epidermidis is susceptible to novobiocin, whereas S. saprophyticus is resistant.⭐ Saprophyticus = resistant ("Sapro = Stubborn to novobiocin").
  8. 8Dirty hands: S. epidermidis is part of the normal flora of skin and mucous membranes.
  9. 9Petri dish pan: As normal skin flora, S. epidermidis is a common contaminant of blood cultures.⭐ Multiple positive cultures suggest true infection rather than contamination.
  10. 10Plumbing joints: S. epidermidis is one of the most frequently isolated pathogens in prosthetic joint infections.
  11. 11Tubing: S. epidermidis is a leading cause of catheter-associated (intravascular device) infections.
  12. 12Heart valve: S. epidermidis is the most common cause of prosthetic valve endocarditis (early, within about 2 months of surgery).
  13. 13Gunk on pipes and tubes: S. epidermidis produces biofilm (extracellular polysaccharide slime), allowing adherence to prosthetic devices and IV catheters.⭐ Biofilm is the key virulence factor and protects against antibiotics.
  14. 14Van: Vancomycin is the drug of choice for S. epidermidis because most strains are resistant to nafcillin and methicillin (mecA); biofilm formation further impairs antibiotic penetration.⭐ Often requires device removal as well.
  15. 15Honeymooner: S. saprophyticus commonly causes uncomplicated urinary tract infection in young, sexually active women (honeymoon cystitis).⭐ Second most common cause of UTI in this group.
  16. 16Bladder cup: S. saprophyticus is an important cause of uncomplicated UTI, second after E. coli (E. coli and Klebsiella are also frequent causes).⭐ Suspect in a UTI that is nitrite negative, urease positive with alkaline urine.
  17. 17Inflamed prostate fruit: S. saprophyticus has been reported as a cause of acute bacterial prostatitis (uncommon; E. coli is far more common).

4. Intestinal Nematodes (Enterobius, Ancylostoma, Necator, Ascaris, Strongyloides, Trichinella)

Exam snapshot
  • Pinworm (Enterobius): nocturnal perianal itching in a child; Scotch tape test; pyrantel pamoate (treat household).
  • Hookworms (Ancylostoma, Necator): larvae penetrate bare feet, then lungs, then gut; iron deficiency anemia; eggs in stool; albendazole/pyrantel.
  • Ascaris: ingested eggs, lung migration (Loeffler), giant worm can cause intestinal obstruction; eggs in stool; albendazole.
  • Strongyloides: skin penetration plus autoinfection; larvae (not eggs) in stool; hyperinfection with steroids/HTLV-1; ivermectin.
  • Trichinella: undercooked pork or bear; periorbital edema, fever, myalgias, high CK; encysts in striated muscle; albendazole.
  • Eosinophilia is common in tissue-migrating nematodes; skin-penetrators (hookworm, Strongyloides) go blood to lungs to gut.
  1. 1Vermin lady entering wall: Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm) is an intestinal nematode (roundworm). It is the most common helminth infection in the US, especially in school-age children and daycare or household settings.
  2. 2Rocks outside hole: Gravid female worms migrate out of the anus at night to lay eggs on the perianal skin, causing intense perianal pruritus (worse at night). Pruritus leads to scratching and disrupted sleep.
  3. 3Rats eating by egg rocks: Transmission is fecal-oral: infective eggs on contaminated hands, bedding, toys, and surfaces are ingested. Scratching puts eggs under the fingernails, causing autoinfection and rapid spread within households.
  4. 4Scotch tape cape: Scotch tape test: press clear adhesive tape to the perianal skin (best done in the morning before bathing) and examine it microscopically for eggs. Stool O&P is usually negative because eggs are laid on the skin, not in the gut.⭐ Diagnose pinworm with the tape test, not a stool exam.
  5. 5"PAM!" + vermin lady: Pyrantel pamoate is the treatment for pinworm; it is a nicotinic agonist that causes depolarizing spastic paralysis of the worm, which is then expelled. Treat the whole household and repeat in about 2 weeks.
  6. 6Bendy metal bars: Albendazole is a broad-spectrum benzimidazole anthelmintic active against Enterobius, hookworms (Ancylostoma, Necator), Ascaris, Strongyloides, and Trichinella. It binds beta-tubulin and inhibits microtubule formation in the worm. It is contraindicated in the first trimester of pregnancy (teratogenic).
  7. 7America dude: Ancylostoma duodenale (Old World hookworm) and Necator americanus (New World hookworm) are the two hookworms. Both are intestinal nematodes.
  8. 8Rings around ankle: Ancylostoma duodenale is the Old World hookworm (Europe, Asia, Africa). It is an intestinal nematode with teeth-like cutting plates in its mouth.
  9. 9Rings around neck: Necator americanus is the New World hookworm (the Americas and sub-Saharan Africa). It is an intestinal nematode with cutting plates in its mouth.
  10. 10Hook: Hookworms are roundworms with a hook-like (curved) head and cutting mouthparts that attach to the small-intestinal wall and feed on blood.
  11. 11Red boots on America dude: Hookworm larvae in contaminated soil penetrate intact skin, classically of bare feet (going barefoot is the risk factor). The skin entry can cause a pruritic rash (ground itch).
  12. 12Arrow symbol on America dude: After skin penetration the larvae travel via the bloodstream to the lungs, ascend the trachea, and are swallowed at the pharynx. They mature in the small intestine, attach, suck blood, and release eggs in stool to continue the cycle.
  13. 13Iron + small pale steam holes: Chronic intestinal blood loss from attached worms causes iron deficiency anemia (microcytic, hypochromic). Think of it in a patient from an endemic area or with a barefoot-exposure history.
  14. 14Grenades in water: Diagnosis is by finding eggs in stool (O&P). Hookworm eggs are oval with a thin shell and a segmented (multi-cell) embryo inside.
  15. 15Bilobed slingshot kid + pink granules: Eosinophilia is common, especially during larval migration through the tissues. Eosinophils are bilobed with bright red-orange granules.
  16. 16"PAM!" + America dude: Pyrantel pamoate treats hookworm infection (nicotinic agonist causing spastic paralysis of the worm). Albendazole or mebendazole are alternatives, and iron is given for anemia.
  17. 17Lumbering tree man: Ascaris lumbricoides is the giant human roundworm, the largest intestinal nematode. Infection occurs by ingesting embryonated eggs from soil-contaminated hands, vegetables, or water (fecal-oral).
  18. 18Arrow symbol on lumbering tree man: Ingested eggs hatch in the small intestine, and larvae penetrate the gut wall and travel via the bloodstream to the lungs. They ascend the trachea, are swallowed at the pharynx, and mature into adults in the small intestine; eggs pass in stool.
  19. 19Respiratory leaf: During lung migration, larvae can cause cough, wheezing, and transient pulmonary infiltrates with eosinophilia (Loeffler syndrome).
  20. 20Obstructing rubble: A heavy worm burden can cause intestinal obstruction (classically at the ileocecal valve), and may also cause biliary obstruction or pancreatitis if worms migrate. Obstruction is most common in children.⭐ Large ball of worms -> small bowel obstruction in a child from an endemic area.
  21. 21Acorns in water: Diagnosis is by finding characteristic eggs in stool; fertilized eggs are round with a thick, bumpy (mammillated) shell. Adult worms may also be seen passed in stool or vomit.
  22. 22Pink granules by lumbering tree man: Eosinophilia is a common finding, especially during the tissue-migration phase. Treatment is albendazole or mebendazole (pyrantel is an alternative).
  23. 23Strong guy: Strongyloides stercoralis (threadworm) is an intestinal nematode. Unlike the other worms in this video, it can complete its life cycle within the host, which allows persistence for decades.
  24. 24Red boots on strong guy: Filariform (infective) larvae in soil penetrate intact skin, classically of the feet, just like hookworms.
  25. 25Arrow symbol on strong guy: After skin penetration, larvae go through the bloodstream to the lungs, up the trachea, and are swallowed. Adult females embed in the small-intestinal mucosa and lay eggs that hatch there, so rhabditiform larvae (not eggs) are passed in stool; some mature into filariform larvae and cause autoinfection.
  26. 26Rocks inside hole: Autoinfection: rhabditiform larvae molt into filariform larvae inside the gut, penetrate the intestinal wall or perianal skin, and re-enter the cycle. This lets infection persist for decades and, in immunosuppressed patients, causes hyperinfection and disseminated disease (including gram-negative sepsis).⭐ Corticosteroids / HTLV-1 -> Strongyloides hyperinfection; screen before starting steroids.
  27. 27Larval worms in water: Diagnosis is by finding rhabditiform larvae in stool (not eggs, in contrast to the other nematodes). Stool is often negative at first, so serology or repeated stool exams may be needed.⭐ Larvae, not eggs, in stool.
  28. 28Pink granules by strong guy: Eosinophilia is common in chronic strongyloidiasis. It may be absent in disseminated disease, a bad prognostic sign.
  29. 29"No dumping, drains to river": Ivermectin is the treatment of choice for Strongyloides; it activates glutamate-gated chloride channels, causing paralysis of the worm. Albendazole is an alternative, and treatment must also be given to carriers before immunosuppression.
  30. 30Porky trickster + spiral suit: Trichinella spiralis (pork worm) is a nematode that causes trichinosis. Its life cycle includes an intestinal phase and a muscle phase.
  31. 31Pig: Infection occurs by eating undercooked or raw meat containing encysted larvae. Classic sources are pork and wild game such as bear.
  32. 32Glasses: Larval migration causes a hypersensitivity/vasculitic reaction, producing periorbital edema (puffy eyes), often with conjunctival hemorrhages.
  33. 33Green drool: The intestinal phase (adult worms in the gut, days 1-2 after ingestion) causes nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain.
  34. 34Sweat drops: The invasive phase (larvae migrating) causes fever, along with periorbital edema and myalgias.
  35. 35Red pipes: Larvae released in the gut penetrate the intestinal wall, enter the bloodstream, and are carried to skeletal (striated) muscle. Larval spread in the blood is what triggers fever and edema.
  36. 36Striated brick: Larvae preferentially invade striated muscle (e.g., extraocular muscles, diaphragm, masseters, tongue), where they encyst. Muscle biopsy shows encysted larvae.
  37. 37Explosives in brick: Larvae encyst within striated muscle cells and can calcify later. Muscle cell damage causes elevated creatine kinase (CK).
  38. 38Inflammatory fire: Larval encystment causes myositis, with muscle pain (myalgia), tenderness, and inflammation. Treatment is albendazole or mebendazole (steroids for severe symptoms).
  39. 39Pink granules by pig: Marked eosinophilia is typical. Diagnosis rests on history, eosinophilia, elevated CK, serology, and muscle biopsy.⭐ Eosinophilia + myalgia + periorbital edema after undercooked pork or bear meat = Trichinella.

5. Trematodes

Exam snapshot
  • Schistosoma: cercariae penetrate skin in freshwater; eggs with lateral spine (S. mansoni, S. japonicum) or terminal spine (S. haematobium).
  • S. mansoni/japonicum cause hepatosplenic disease, periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension; S. haematobium causes hematuria and bladder squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Clonorchis sinensis: raw freshwater fish in Asia; bile ducts, pigmented gallstones, cholangitis, cholangiocarcinoma.
  • Paragonimus westermani: raw crab/crayfish; lung fluke with cough and hemoptysis mimicking TB; operculated eggs in sputum or stool.
  • Treatment: praziquantel for all three groups (Clonorchis alternative: albendazole).
  1. 1San Franschisto Aquatic Park: Schistosoma spp. are freshwater blood flukes. Snail-released cercariae penetrate human skin during water exposure and cause schistosomiasis (intestinal, hepatic or urinary, depending on species).
  2. 2Swimmer: Cercariae are free-swimming larvae in contaminated freshwater lakes, rivers and ponds (Africa, Asia, South America), so swimming, wading or bathing puts people at risk. Skin penetration is followed by migration to the liver.
  3. 3Snails by fish: Freshwater snails are the intermediate hosts for Schistosoma and release the infectious cercariae. Snail control and avoiding contaminated water help prevent infection.
  4. 4Liver-shaped coral: After skin penetration, larvae enter the blood and mature into adult worms in the portal venous system of the liver. Acute infection may cause Katayama fever (fever, urticaria, eosinophilia).
  5. 5Blue fish swimming towards liver-shaped coral: Normal portal flow carries blood from the gut toward the liver. This sets up the direction the worms then reverse.
  6. 6Swimming opposite direction: Adult worms migrate against portal flow into the mesenteric (or vesical) venules, where they lay eggs. The eggs, not the worms, cause disease via granulomatous inflammation.
  7. 7Merman statue: Schistosoma mansoni (Africa, Caribbean, South America) lives in the mesenteric veins and causes intestinal and hepatosplenic disease.
  8. 8Lateral fin + merman statue: S. mansoni eggs have a prominent lateral spine, seen on stool microscopy.⭐ Mansoni = lateral spine. Eggs are seen in stool.
  9. 9Japanese tourist: Schistosoma japonicum (East Asia) lives in the mesenteric veins and causes intestinal and hepatosplenic disease.
  10. 10Round fish + japanese tourist: S. japonicum eggs are round with a tiny, inconspicuous lateral spine. Eggs are seen in stool.
  11. 11Aquarium swimmer: Swimmer's itch (cercarial dermatitis) is a transient allergic skin reaction to cercariae of bird schistosomes. The larvae die in the skin because humans are not their host, so there is no systemic disease.
  12. 12Porthole crack/degraded liver coral: Chronic egg deposition causes granulomas, periportal ('pipestem') fibrosis and cirrhosis, leading to presinusoidal portal hypertension with splenomegaly and varices. Liver function is often relatively preserved.⭐ Schisto = presinusoidal portal hypertension (hepatosplenomegaly, varices).
  13. 13Yellow coat: Hepatic schistosomiasis can cause jaundice and cholestasis because egg-induced periportal inflammation and fibrosis obstruct bile flow.
  14. 14Swordfish with terminal bill: Schistosoma haematobium (Africa, Middle East) lives in the vesical venous plexus around the bladder.
  15. 15Terminal bill: S. haematobium eggs have a large terminal spine and are found in urine, not stool.⭐ Haematobium = terminal spine, eggs in urine.
  16. 16Bleeding jellyfish: S. haematobium causes urinary schistosomiasis, often with terminal hematuria, dysuria and later obstructive uropathy.
  17. 17Crab + swordfish: Chronic S. haematobium infection is associated with squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder (not the usual urothelial carcinoma).⭐ S. haematobium = squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder.
  18. 18Pretzel + fish: Praziquantel is the treatment of choice for all Schistosoma species. It acts by increasing calcium permeability and paralyzing the worm.
  19. 19Orca: Clonorchis sinensis is the Chinese (Asian) liver fluke, found in East Asia.
  20. 20Snails + orca: Snails are the first intermediate host (releasing cercariae that encyst in freshwater fish). Humans are infected by eating undercooked or raw freshwater fish.
  21. 21Seagull: Adult flukes reside in the bile ducts. Eggs and worms cause chronic inflammation, ductal dilatation, mechanical obstruction and thickening of the biliary walls.
  22. 22Sclerosed rope: Chronic infection can lead to sclerosing cholangitis (recurrent pyogenic cholangitis), with inflammation and destruction of bile ducts.
  23. 23Crab + orca: Clonorchis is a known risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma, a malignancy of the biliary epithelium.⭐ Clonorchis = cholangiocarcinoma.
  24. 24Black rocks + seagulls: Infection can cause pigmented gallstones (bile stasis and bilirubin precipitation), which can lead to biliary obstruction and cholangitis.
  25. 25"Capped" seagull eggs: Clonorchis eggs are small and operculated (capped), seen on stool exam. Eggs have a lid and a shouldering rim.
  26. 26Pretzel + orca: Praziquantel is the treatment of choice, with albendazole as an alternative.
  27. 27Penguins: Paragonimus westermani is the (Japanese/Asian) lung fluke, endemic to Asia and parts of Africa and Latin America.
  28. 28Red speckled lung spots: Flukes encyst in the lung and cause chronic cough, hemoptysis (rusty sputum) and cystic lesions. It can mimic tuberculosis, so consider it in endemic-area patients with TB-like symptoms and a negative TB workup.⭐ Hemoptysis + eosinophilia in a patient from Asia: think Paragonimus, not TB.
  29. 29Snails + penguins: Snails are the first intermediate host, releasing cercariae that encyst in crustaceans. Humans are infected by eating them.
  30. 30Crab legs: Infection is acquired by eating raw, undercooked or pickled freshwater crabs or crayfish containing metacercariae.
  31. 31"Capped" penguin eggs: Paragonimus eggs are operculated (capped) and found in sputum or stool, because swallowed sputum passes into stool.
  32. 32Pretzel + penguins: Praziquantel is the drug of choice for Paragonimus westermani.

6. Staphylococcus aureus

Exam snapshot
  • Classic: abscesses, impetigo, post-influenza pneumonia, acute endocarditis (IVDU, tricuspid), septic arthritis and osteomyelitis
  • Gram-positive cocci in clusters; catalase +, coagulase + (distinguishes from S. epidermidis/saprophyticus), golden pigment, beta-hemolytic, ferments mannitol
  • Toxins: TSST-1 (superantigen, toxic shock syndrome), exfoliative toxin (scalded skin), preformed enterotoxin (food poisoning in 1-6 h)
  • Protein A binds Fc of IgG, blocking opsonization and phagocytosis; nasal carriage is the reservoir
  • Treatment: nafcillin/oxacillin for MSSA; vancomycin for MRSA (altered PBP, mecA gene)
  1. 1Golden staff: Staphylococcus aureus is a gram-positive coccus that grows in grape-like clusters; its colonies are golden because of staphyloxanthin pigment.
  2. 2Purple robe: S. aureus is a gram-positive coccus (purple on Gram stain), seen in clusters.
  3. 3Catalase cat: S. aureus is catalase positive: catalase converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen (bubbles on testing). Staphylococci are catalase +, whereas streptococci are catalase -.
  4. 4Coagulated river: S. aureus is coagulase positive: coagulase converts fibrinogen to fibrin, forming a clot in plasma. This distinguishes it from other catalase-positive, coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis, S. saprophyticus).⭐ Coagulase + = S. aureus.

14. Dermatophytes (Epidermophyton, Trichophyton, Microsporum)

Exam snapshot
  • Tinea (ringworm): red, scaly, itchy, annular lesions with raised edges and central clearing on skin, scalp, groin, feet, or nails.

  2. 2Rings of rust on tin man: Same point: tinea lesions are red, annular (ring-shaped) plaques, hence 'ringworm'.
  3. 3"E," "T," and "M" munchkins: The main dermatophyte genera are Epidermophyton, Trichophyton, and Microsporum.⭐ Trichophyton is the most common cause of tinea.
  4. 4Naked munchkins: Dermatophytes are pathogens, not part of the normal skin flora.
  5. 5Dog: Transmission is by direct contact with infected animals or humans, or indirectly through shared environments and fomites.⭐ Microsporum canis is acquired from pets.
  6. 6Rings on hat: Tinea capitis: infection of the scalp and hair, mainly in children, with patchy hair loss, scaling, broken hairs, and sometimes inflammation.⭐ Treat with oral therapy (griseofulvin or terbinafine); topicals are not enough.
  7. 7Rings on groin: Tinea cruris (jock itch): red, itchy, often ring-shaped rash of the groin and inner thighs; more common with warmth, moisture, sweating, or tight clothing.
  8. 8Rings on feet: Tinea pedis (athlete's foot): scaling, redness, itching, and sometimes cracking or blisters, especially between the toes; common in locker rooms, showers, and tight shoes.
  9. 9Rings on body: Tinea corporis: red, scaly, ring-shaped patches with raised edges and central clearing, on any part of the body; seen in wrestlers (tinea gladiatorum).
  10. 10Athletic head band: Athletes are at higher risk because of close contact, shared equipment, and warm, moist environments (swimmers, wrestlers); common infections include tinea corporis, pedis, and cruris.
  11. 11Scratching: Dermatophyte infections typically cause a red, itchy, scaly rash, often with raised edges and central clearing.
  12. 12KOH salt: KOH microscopy of scrapings reveals septate hyphae and confirms the diagnosis.⭐ KOH dissolves keratinocytes so hyphae are seen.

  16. 16Turban + biting nails: Oral antifungals (terbinafine, griseofulvin, or azoles) are used for onychomycosis, tinea capitis (turban), and severe or resistant skin infections.⭐ Terbinafine is first-line for onychomycosis; monitor liver enzymes.
  17. 17Grease + biting nails: Oral griseofulvin is used for more severe dermatophyte infections; it deposits in keratin-rich tissues (nails, hair, skin) but is often poorly tolerated due to GI side effects.⭐ Griseofulvin: microtubule inhibitor; hepatotoxic, teratogenic, CYP450 inducer.

15. Sporothrix schenckii

Exam snapshot
  • Rose gardener with a thorn prick developing nodules and ulcers that ascend the arm along lymphatics.
  • Thermally dimorphic fungus: mold at 25-30 C, yeast at 35-37 C; found in soil, plants, thorns, sphagnum moss.
  • Biopsy or culture; yeast phase shows cigar-shaped budding yeast; mold phase shows rosette (daisy-like) conidia.
  • Itraconazole for cutaneous and lymphocutaneous disease; amphotericin B for severe or disseminated disease; SSKI is historical.
  • Rose gardener's disease, lymphocutaneous spread, cigar-shaped yeast, rosette conidia.
  1. 1Shanked by a thorn: Sporothrix schenckii is a dimorphic fungus that causes cutaneous (subcutaneous) mycosis after traumatic inoculation.
  2. 2Garden: S. schenckii lives in soil, plant debris, tree bark, and thorny vegetation such as rose bushes and sphagnum moss.
  3. 3Dimorphic butterfly: S. schenckii is thermally dimorphic: mold in cooler environments (25-30 C) and yeast at body temperature (35-37 C).⭐ Mold in the cold, yeast in the heat.
  4. 4Roses: Sporotrichosis is called "rose gardener's disease" because infection follows a prick from thorns or other contaminated plant material.⭐ Rose gardener's disease.
  5. 5Branching rose stems: The mold phase has septate, branching hyphae with small round conidia in rosette or daisy-like clusters at the tips of conidiophores (spore-bearing stalks).
  6. 6Cigar: The yeast phase appears as small oval to elongated, cigar-shaped yeast cells with narrow-based budding, often surrounded by histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and granulomatous inflammation.⭐ Cigar-shaped yeast.
  7. 7Thorny vine around arm: Sporotrichosis typically follows traumatic inoculation of contaminated plant matter or soil, causing subcutaneous nodules and ulcers.
  8. 8Ascending vines: In lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis, the fungus ascends from the inoculation site along lymphatics, forming sequential subcutaneous nodules that may enlarge, become tender, and ulcerate, usually on the hands or forearms.⭐ Ascending nodular lymphangitis after a thorn prick.
  9. 9Roses on vines on arm: Same point: nodules arranged along the lymphatic channel of the arm (lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis).
  10. 10Pinecones: Oral itraconazole is the preferred treatment for cutaneous and lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis.⭐ Severe or disseminated disease needs amphotericin B.
  11. 11Potassium iodide pesticide: A saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI) was historically used for sporotrichosis; modern antifungals (itraconazole) are now preferred.

16. Tissue Nematodes (Dracunculus, Wuchereria, Onchocerca, Toxocara, Loa loa)

Exam snapshot
  • Clues: Dracunculus = painful leg ulcer with emerging worm; Onchocerca = river blindness plus itchy skin; Wuchereria = elephantiasis; Loa loa = Calabar swellings and worm in eye; Toxocara = visceral/ocular larva migrans.
  • All are tissue nematodes with eosinophilia; vectors: copepods (Dracunculus), black fly (Onchocerca), mosquito (Wuchereria), deer fly (Loa), dog/cat feces eggs (Toxocara).
  • Diagnosis: skin snip (Onchocerca), blood smear (Wuchereria, Loa), adult worm emergence (Dracunculus), serology/clinical (Toxocara).
  • Treatment: ivermectin (Onchocerca), diethylcarbamazine (Wuchereria, Loa), albendazole (Toxocara), supportive worm extraction (Dracunculus).
  • Buzzwords: Guinea worm, river blindness, leopard skin, elephantiasis, Calabar swelling, ocular larva migrans.
  1. 1Dracula: Dracunculus medinensis (Guinea worm), a tissue nematode.
  2. 2Water cooler: Infection occurs when humans drink water containing copepods (small crustaceans) that have ingested D. medinensis larvae.⭐ Prevention is by filtering drinking water.
  3. 3Small cups: Copepods serve as the intermediate hosts, harboring infective larvae.
  4. 4Shoelace emerging from foot: The adult female worm emerges through a painful skin ulcer, typically on the lower extremity, when the limb contacts water.⭐ Worm emerges from a blistering leg ulcer.
  5. 5Wrapped shoelace: Traditional treatment is slow extraction by winding the emerging worm around a stick over days to weeks.⭐ There is no effective drug; pulling too fast breaks the worm.
  6. 6Bilobed slingshot kid + pink granules by Dracula: Eosinophilia is a common finding in D. medinensis infection (bilobed eosinophils with bright red-orange granules), especially during larval migration.
  7. 7Fly guy: Onchocerca volvulus causes onchocerciasis (river blindness); an infected black fly (Simulium) bite injects larvae that mature into adults in the skin and release microfilariae.⭐ Black fly breeds near fast-flowing rivers.
  8. 8Covering eyes: Microfilariae in ocular tissues can cause visual impairment and ultimately blindness, a severe complication of onchocerciasis.⭐ Onchocerciasis is a leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide.
  9. 9Pink granules + fly guy: Eosinophilia is a common finding in Onchocerca volvulus infection.
  10. 10Light and dark spots: Onchodermatitis: inflammation from dying microfilariae causes pruritic dermatitis with hyperpigmented and hypopigmented patches ('leopard skin').
  11. 11Microscope: Diagnosis is by finding Onchocerca microfilariae in a skin snip (biopsy) under the microscope.⭐ Skin snip, not blood smear, is used for Onchocerca.
  12. 12No dumping, drains to river: Ivermectin is the treatment of choice for onchocerciasis, killing microfilariae.⭐ Ivermectin kills microfilariae, not adults, so repeated dosing is needed; DEC can precipitate severe reactions (Mazzotti).
  13. 13Witch: Wuchereria bancrofti, a tissue (filarial) nematode.
  14. 14Hammer pants: W. bancrofti causes lymphatic filariasis: adult worms block lymphatic vessels, causing impaired drainage, chronic inflammation, and fibrosis, leading to elephantiasis (massive lymphedema of limbs and genitals).
  15. 15Sleeves and collar ruffles: Infection can cause lymphangitis and painful lymphadenopathy from inflammation of lymphatic vessels and nodes.
  16. 16Witch coughing: Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia is a hypersensitivity reaction to microfilariae in the lungs, with nocturnal cough, wheezing, dyspnea, and marked eosinophilia.⭐ Nocturnal cough with high eosinophilia in an endemic area.
  17. 17Mosquitoes: A variety of mosquitoes (Culex, Aedes, Anopheles) transmit Wuchereria bancrofti.
  18. 18Blood smear + witch: Diagnosis is confirmed by finding microfilariae on a thick blood smear, ideally drawn at night.⭐ Microfilariae show nocturnal periodicity.
  19. 19Slingshot granules + witch: Eosinophilia is a common finding in W. bancrofti infection.
  20. 20"Diet and Carb" + witch: Diethylcarbamazine is effective against W. bancrofti, targeting microfilariae and adult worms.⭐ Doxycycline (against Wolbachia) is also used.
  21. 21Wolfman: Toxocara canis, a tissue nematode that causes toxocariasis (visceral and ocular larva migrans).
  22. 22Poop bag: Infection is acquired by ingesting food or soil contaminated with eggs from dog (or cat) feces.⭐ Think of a toddler eating dirt or a child with puppy exposure.
  23. 23Sleep mask: Ocular larva migrans results from larval migration to the eye, causing significant visual impairment or blindness.⭐ A retinal granuloma can mimic retinoblastoma.
  24. 24Slingshot granules + wolfman: Eosinophilia is a common finding in Toxocara infection, especially visceral larva migrans.
  25. 25Bent chair legs + wolfman: Albendazole treats visceral toxocariasis; ocular toxocariasis is difficult to treat and management aims to prevent further eye damage (e.g., corticosteroids).
  26. 26Lagoon swamp creature: Loa loa, a tissue (filarial) nematode (African eye worm).
  27. 27Lumps on arms and legs: Loa loa causes recurrent Calabar swellings: transient, soft, slightly red, itchy, migratory subcutaneous swellings, mainly on the extremities.⭐ Calabar swellings are transient and migratory.
  28. 28Worm in eye: Migration of the adult worm across the conjunctiva is a hallmark clinical feature.⭐ Adult worm visibly crossing the eye = Loa loa.
  29. 29Blood smear + swamp creature: Loa loa is diagnosed by microfilariae in peripheral blood smears or by adult worms seen in the conjunctiva.⭐ Microfilariae show diurnal periodicity.
  30. 30Flies: Deer flies (Chrysops) are the vectors of Loa loa.⭐ Chrysops is also called the mango fly.
  31. 31Pink granules + swamp creature: Eosinophilia is a common finding in Loa loa infection.
  32. 32"Diet and Carb" + swamp creature: Diethylcarbamazine is the treatment of choice for Loa loa, killing microfilariae and adults.⭐ Check for high microfilarial counts before treating to avoid encephalopathy.
  33. 33Bent chair legs + swamp creature: Albendazole is sometimes used when multiple courses of diethylcarbamazine fail to cure Loa loa.
