1. Nomenclature & Naming of Tumors

A neoplasm is an abnormal mass of cells whose growth is autonomous, persisting even after the stimulus that provoked it is removed, because each cell carries a heritable genetic alteration driving the disordered proliferation. Every tumor is built from two compartments: the parenchyma, the proliferating neoplastic cells that determine the tumor's behavior and supply the basis for its name, and the supporting stroma of connective tissue, blood vessels, and immune cells that forms the scaffold and feeds the parenchyma. Naming a tumor is therefore an exercise in reading two facts off the slide: the cell of origin and whether the lesion is benign or malignant.

High-Yield Notes

The default rule is that the suffix -oma marks a benign tumor, and this holds cleanly for tumors of mesenchymal origin: lipoma (fat), fibroma (fibroblast), chondroma (cartilage), osteoma (bone), leiomyoma (smooth muscle), and rhabdomyoma (striated muscle). Benign epithelial tumors are named more elaborately, by cell of origin (adenoma from glands), by microscopic architecture (cystadenoma forming cystic masses, papilloma forming finger-like projections), or by macroscopic shape (a polyp projecting above a mucosal surface).

Malignant tumors split by the same two germ-line families. A malignancy of epithelial cells is a carcinoma (squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, transitional/urothelial carcinoma), while a malignancy of mesenchymal cells is a sarcoma (liposarcoma, fibrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma). When parenchyma dominates an epithelial tumor it is called medullary (breast, thyroid); when dense fibrous stroma dominates, the reaction is desmoplasia and the tumor is hard, or scirrhous (infiltrating duct carcinoma of breast, linitis plastica of stomach).

Tumors with more than one parenchymal cell type from a single germ layer are mixed tumors: the pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary gland shows epithelium plus cartilage-like myxoid tissue from divergent differentiation of one cell line. A teratoma arises from totipotent germ cells and contains tissues from more than one germ layer (often all three: hair, teeth, cartilage, gut, neural tissue); a mature ovarian teratoma is the dermoid cyst. Memorize the -oma misnomers that are actually malignant: lymphoma, melanoma, seminoma, mesothelioma, and hepatoma (hepatocellular carcinoma).

2. Benign vs Malignant: Characteristics

Separating a benign tumor from a malignant one is the central judgment of surgical pathology, and it rests on four criteria: differentiation and anaplasia, rate of growth, local invasion, and metastasis. Differentiation measures how closely tumor cells resemble their normal counterparts in both shape and function; benign tumors are typically well differentiated, while malignant tumors range from well differentiated to wholly anaplastic. These criteria are read off appearance and behavior together, because no single cytologic feature is absolute.

High-Yield Notes

Benign tumors closely resemble the parent tissue, grow slowly with few normal-appearing mitoses, push the surrounding tissue aside as an expansile ball that is usually encapsulated, and never metastasize. Malignant tumors are less differentiated, grow at rates that track their degree of differentiation (anaplastic tumors grow fastest), show numerous and abnormal mitoses with areas of necrosis and hemorrhage, infiltrate across tissue planes so that clean surgical enucleation is impossible, and can metastasize.

Anaplasia is the morphologic hallmark of malignancy, meaning lack of differentiation. Its features are nuclear and cellular pleomorphism (variation in size and shape), hyperchromatic nuclei, an increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio approaching 1:1, prominent nucleoli, abundant and atypical (tripolar) mitoses, loss of polarity with disorganized architecture, and tumor giant cells. Among these, abnormal mitotic figures are the single most reliable cytologic sign of malignancy.

Metastasis is the most important feature distinguishing malignant from benign, because it unequivocally marks a tumor as malignant. Nearly all malignant tumors can metastasize, with two key exceptions that invade locally but virtually never spread: basal cell carcinoma of the skin and gliomas of the brain. On the epithelial surface, progression runs from dysplasia (disordered but reversible growth confined to the epithelium with an intact basement membrane), through carcinoma in situ (full-thickness atypia, still above an intact basement membrane), to invasive carcinoma once the basement membrane is breached. The breach of the basement membrane is the dividing line between in situ and invasion.

3. Invasion, Metastasis & Routes of Spread

Invasion and metastasis are the two features that most reliably mark a tumor as malignant, and metastasis is the single property that most often kills the patient. A metastasis is a tumor implant that is physically discontinuous with the primary tumor, seeded after malignant cells complete an ordered metastatic cascade: they detach from one another, chew through the extracellular matrix, enter a vessel, survive the hostile circulation, exit at a distant site, and finally colonize new tissue. In general the larger, faster growing, and more aggressive the primary, the more likely it has already spread. This section follows that cascade step by step and then maps the three routes of spread and the organ tropism each one predicts.

High-Yield Notes

The cascade opens with detachment. Loss of the intercellular adhesion molecule E-cadherin frees epithelial cells from one another and is seen in nearly all epithelial cancers, caused by E-cadherin gene mutation, by activation of beta-catenin, or by the transcription factors SNAIL and TWIST that suppress E-cadherin during the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Cells next perform ECM degradation: they secrete or induce stromal cells to secrete proteases, chiefly the matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-9 is a gelatinase, a type IV collagenase that dissolves basement membrane and liberates VEGF from matrix stores), plus cathepsin D and urokinase. Degraded collagen and laminin fragments are themselves chemotactic and angiogenic. Cells then attach to matrix via receptors for laminin and fibronectin and migrate, driven by autocrine motility factors and stromal hepatocyte growth factor.

Once a cell achieves intravasation it must survive in the circulation, where it faces mechanical shear, anoikis (apoptosis from loss of anchorage), and NK and T cell killing; tumor cells improve their odds by aggregating into emboli with platelets and leukocytes. After extravasation, the site of colonization is set partly by simple mechanics (most metastases lodge in the first capillary bed reached) and partly by specific organ tropism, in which tumor chemokine receptors match ligands on target endothelium (breast cancer CXCR4 and CCR7 home to bone and lung). Homing also depends on adhesion molecules such as integrins, laminin receptors, and CD44. Normally CD44 sits on T cells, where it binds hyaluronate on high endothelial venules to guide lymphocyte homing into nodes; tumor cells co-opt the same molecule, and its overexpression favors metastatic spread. Routes of spread predict the tumor: lymphatic spread is typical of carcinomas, hematogenous spread is typical of sarcomas (and uses veins, not arteries), and transcoelomic seeding scatters cells across body cavities.

Bone is one of the most common sites of metastasis, and the radiographic reaction of the bone tells you which primary to suspect. Metastases disturb the normal coupling of bone resorption and formation, tipping it one of two ways. In osteoblastic (osteosclerotic) lesions the tumor drives osteoblasts to lay down disorganized new bone, producing dense white foci on X-ray and a high alkaline phosphatase with usually normal calcium. In osteolytic (osteoclastic) lesions tumor-released PTHrP, RANKL, and cytokines activate osteoclasts to resorb bone, producing lucent "punched out" defects, releasing calcium to cause hypercalcemia, and predisposing to pathologic fracture. The single most tested contrast is prostate (osteoblastic) versus most other carcinomas and myeloma (osteolytic).

Two radiographic patterns of spread are themselves exam buzzwords. Cannonball metastases are multiple large, round, well-circumscribed nodules scattered through both lungs; the classic primaries are renal cell carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, and (in bone/soft tissue) sarcomas. Separately, when a biopsy of a metastasis or primary shows sheets of "small round blue cells" — tumor cells with scant cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nuclei that stain intensely blue on H&E — think of the pediatric/embryonal tumor family: Ewing sarcoma, neuroblastoma, lymphoma/ALL, rhabdomyosarcoma, Wilms tumor, retinoblastoma, medulloblastoma, and small-cell carcinoma. The distinction is then made on immunostains and translocations.

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Lose E-cadherin to detach, lose basement membrane (MMP type IV collagenase) to invade, then survive the blood to spread. SNAIL and TWIST drive the EMT that begins it.
  • Carcinomas seed lymphatics first; sarcomas seed blood first. The classic carcinoma exceptions that go hematogenous early are follicular thyroid, renal cell, and hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Veins, not arteries, carry hematogenous spread because arterial walls are thicker; portal drainage points metastases to liver, systemic venous drainage points them to lung.
  • A Krukenberg tumor is bilateral ovarian metastasis of signet-ring cells (mucin pushing the nucleus to the rim), classically from the stomach; a sentinel node is the first node draining a tumor and guides surgical staging.
  • Bone mets: prostate is the classic osteoblastic primary (dense lesions, high alk phos, normal calcium); multiple myeloma, renal cell, and most other carcinomas are osteolytic (punched-out lesions, hypercalcemia, pathologic fracture).
  • Cannonball lung mets = renal cell, choriocarcinoma, sarcoma. Small round blue cell tumor = Ewing, neuroblastoma, lymphoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Wilms, retinoblastoma, small-cell carcinoma.

4. Cancer Epidemiology

Cancer is the second most common cause of death in the United States, where a resident has roughly a one in five lifetime chance of dying of it. Two separate metrics describe any cancer: its incidence (how often it occurs) and its death rate (how often it kills), and the two lists do not match because some common tumors are indolent while some less common ones are highly lethal. Four broad factors shift an individual's risk: geographic and environmental exposures, inherited predisposition, acquired (nonhereditary) predisposing conditions, and age. Recognizing the classic geography, the inherited syndromes, and the precancerous lesions lets you predict the tumor before the histology is given.

High-Yield Notes

For incidence, the ranked leaders in men are prostate, lung, colorectal and in women breast, lung, colorectal. For mortality the order shifts: in men lung, prostate, colorectal; in women lung, breast, colorectal. Lung cancer is the single leading cause of cancer death in adults of both sexes. In children the common cancers are acute lymphoblastic leukemia, CNS tumors, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, retinoblastoma, and bone or skeletal muscle sarcomas. Cancer incidence climbs sharply after age 55.

Inherited predisposition accounts for less than 10% of all cancers and falls into autosomal dominant syndromes (a single inherited mutant tumor suppressor allele), autosomal recessive DNA repair defects, and familial clustering (early onset, often bilateral or multiple tumors). Acquired precancerous conditions arise mostly at sites of chronic inflammation (Virchow), and a defined set of precursor lesions map to specific carcinomas.

5. Molecular Basis of Neoplasia

Cancer is fundamentally a genetic disorder: nonlethal damage to DNA, inherited or acquired, lets a single cell escape normal growth control and expand into a monoclonal tumor. Carcinogenesis is multistep, accumulating mutations in four classes of regulatory genes: proto-oncogenes that drive growth, tumor suppressor genes that restrain it, genes regulating apoptosis, and DNA repair genes. Whether a mutation is a gain or loss of function and whether it behaves dominantly or recessively at the cellular level is the single most testable distinction in this section, and it maps directly onto the hallmark capabilities a cell must acquire to become malignant.

High-Yield Notes

An oncogene is the activated, gain of function version of a proto-oncogene; a single mutated allele is enough, so it is dominant at the cell level. A tumor suppressor works the opposite way: it must lose function, and both alleles must be inactivated (the two-hit hypothesis), so it is recessive at the cell level. In familial retinoblastoma the first hit is inherited in the germline and only one somatic hit is needed, explaining early bilateral tumors; sporadic cases require two somatic hits in one cell.

The most common mutation overall is the point mutation (classically RAS and p53). Oncogenes are also activated by translocation (MYC in Burkitt, BCR-ABL in CML) and amplification (N-MYC in neuroblastoma, HER2 in breast). p53, the "guardian of the genome" on chromosome 17p, arrests the cell in G1, drives DNA repair, and triggers apoptosis through BAX; it is mutated in over half of all human cancers and is lost germline in Li-Fraumeni. RB on chromosome 13 holds the cell out of S phase; its hypophosphorylated form is the active brake, which HPV E7 and other viral proteins disable. Apoptosis is evaded when BCL2 is overexpressed (t(14;18), follicular lymphoma), blocking mitochondrial release of cytochrome c. Limitless replication comes from reactivated telomerase preserving telomere length.

6. Carcinogenesis: Chemical, Radiation & Microbial

A carcinogen is any agent that damages genetic material in a way that drives malignant transformation, and three broad classes do this: chemicals, radiant energy, and microbial agents. Chemical carcinogenesis is a multistep process whose two best tested stages are initiation (an irreversible mutation in DNA) and promotion (a nonmutagenic stimulus that pushes the initiated, mutated cell to proliferate). Radiation injures DNA either by generating free radicals (ionizing radiation) or by forming pyrimidine dimers (ultraviolet light), and roughly 15 to 20 percent of human cancers worldwide are caused by oncogenic viruses, bacteria, and parasites. For the exam the highest yield is the one-to-one matching of each specific carcinogen or microbe to the specific cancer it produces.

High-Yield Notes

Initiation versus promotion: an initiator causes a permanent, irreversible DNA mutation; a promoter (for example estrogen) is not itself mutagenic but stimulates the initiated cell to enter the cell cycle, and its effect is reversible. Promotion must follow initiation to cause a tumor. Chemical carcinogens are highly reactive electrophiles that strip electrons from DNA; they are either direct-acting (no metabolic conversion needed, such as the alkylating agents cyclophosphamide and busulfan, which is why these anticancer drugs are themselves carcinogenic) or indirect-acting procarcinogens that must be metabolically activated to an ultimate carcinogen (beta-naphthylamine, deconjugated in the bladder).

Radiation: ionizing radiation (gamma rays, X-rays, alpha and beta particles) hydrolyzes water into hydroxyl free radicals that mutate DNA; the most common radiation-induced cancer overall is leukemia (myelogenous, with CML the classic type). The classic solid tumor is papillary thyroid carcinoma, especially after childhood head-and-neck irradiation (the Chernobyl signature); it is driven by a RET/PTC rearrangement and shows Orphan-Annie-eye nuclei, nuclear grooves, and psammoma bodies on histology. Ultraviolet B light forms pyrimidine dimers that distort DNA and cause basal cell carcinoma (most common), squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma, especially when nucleotide excision repair fails (xeroderma pigmentosum).

The three UV-driven skin cancers are a favorite comparison. All three are preceded by actinic (solar) keratosis — a rough, "sandpaper"-textured premalignant lesion on sun-exposed skin from failed DNA repair. Anchor each to its buzzword: basal cell = rodent ulcer, squamous cell = keratin pearls, melanoma = ABCDE + dysplastic nevi.

Tumors express antigens the immune system can recognize, and the dominant antitumor effector is the CD8 cytotoxic T lymphocyte. High-Yield Notes

Tumor antigens fall into a few testable buckets, and immune evasion is how a tumor with antigens still grows: loss of MHC class I, secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, and engagement of checkpoints such as PD-L1. Separately, inherited defects in DNA repair produce a mutator phenotype with early, often multiple cancers.

8. Clinical Aspects & Paraneoplastic Syndromes

Cancer harms the host through local mass effects, cachexia, and paraneoplastic syndromes: remote effects from tumor-secreted hormones, cytokines, or antibodies that are not explained by the tumor mass or its metastases. They are heavily tested because the syndrome often appears before the cancer is found, so the clinical picture points back to a specific tumor.

High-Yield Notes

Cachexia is a hypercatabolic loss of muscle and fat driven by TNF (cachectin), IL-1, and IL-6, not by reduced intake, and it is a major cause of cancer death. Memorize the classic paraneoplastic pairings: the same tumor recurs across many of them, especially small cell lung carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. The single most-tested fact the deck flags is that hypercalcemia is the most common paraneoplastic syndrome: extraosseous tumors secrete PTHrP (binding the same PTH/PTHrP receptor to raise bone resorption and renal Ca reabsorption) — distinct from hypercalcemia caused by osteolytic bone destruction in myeloma or skeletal metastases.

9. Grading, Staging & Lab Diagnosis

Once a tumor is identified as malignant, two separate questions drive management and prognosis. Grading asks how abnormal the tumor cells look, judged on the microscope from their degree of differentiation and mitotic activity. Staging asks how far the tumor has spread, judged from the size of the primary lesion and its extension to lymph nodes and distant sites. The two are independent, and on exams the recurring point is that stage is the more powerful prognostic factor. This section also covers the laboratory toolbox used to diagnose, classify, and follow cancers, from the H&E biopsy that remains the gold standard to the serum tumor markers that track tumor burden and signal recurrence.

High-Yield Notes

Grading is histologic. It estimates the degree of differentiation and the number of mitoses, and is reported as Grade I (well differentiated) through Grade IV (anaplastic, undifferentiated). The less a tumor resembles its tissue of origin, the higher the grade and the more aggressive the behavior. Staging uses the TNM system: T for the size and local invasion of the primary tumor (a size above 2 cm correlates with metastatic ability), N for regional lymph node involvement, and M for distant metastasis to sites such as liver or lung. Within TNM the components run from least to most important, and the presence of distant metastasis (M1) outweighs everything else. When forced to choose, staging predicts outcome better than grading.

Diagnosis rests first on morphology. Excisional or incisional biopsy fixed in 10% formalin and stained with H&E is the gold standard. Frozen section gives a rapid intraoperative answer (for example, is a margin clear, is this node positive) on fresh unfixed tissue. Fine needle aspiration cytology is fast and minimally invasive for palpable lesions in breast, thyroid, and lymph node. Exfoliative cytology (the Pap smear) screens for cervical and other shed-cell malignancies. Immunohistochemistry uses labeled antibodies to assign lineage to an undifferentiated tumor, to localize the origin of a metastasis, and to read prognostic or therapeutic targets such as ER, PR, and HER2 in breast cancer. Flow cytometry classifies leukemias and lymphomas by surface markers and DNA content, and molecular methods (FISH, PCR, microarray) detect translocations, residual disease, and inherited predisposition.

10. Hypersensitivity Reactions

Hypersensitivity is an excessive or inappropriate immune response, on re-exposure to an antigen, that damages host tissue. The four Gell and Coombs types are distinguished by the effector mechanism, but for pathology the higher-yield task is to recognize each type from its classic disease, its biopsy morphology, and its immunofluorescence pattern. Types I, II, and III are antibody mediated and the fourth is T cell mediated. This section keys each clinical and histologic clue to the correct type so that a vignette describing linear immunofluorescence, lumpy-bumpy deposits, or a positive patch test immediately points to the right mechanism.

High-Yield Notes

Type I (immediate, anaphylactic) is IgE mediated. Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils, releasing histamine within minutes. Systemic exposure (penicillin, insect venom) gives anaphylaxis with hypotension and bronchoconstriction; local disease gives allergic rhinitis, asthma, urticaria (wheal and flare), and food allergy. The asthmatic airway shows mucus plugging, goblet cell and smooth muscle hyperplasia, and an eosinophil-rich infiltrate. Atopy is the genetic tendency toward these reactions, marked by family history, high serum IgE, and peripheral eosinophilia. The skin prick test produces a histamine-mediated wheal and flare.

Type II (antibody mediated, cytotoxic) uses IgG or IgM against a fixed cell or matrix antigen, acting by complement-mediated lysis, opsonized phagocytosis, ADCC, or receptor modulation. Cytotoxic examples include autoimmune and drug-induced hemolytic anemia (penicillin as a hapten on the red cell), ABO transfusion reactions, hemolytic disease of the newborn (Rh; prevented by RhoGAM), and Goodpasture syndrome, where antibody to type IV collagen of the basement membrane gives crescentic glomerulonephritis with hemoptysis and a linear immunofluorescence pattern. Receptor-directed (noncytotoxic) examples are Graves disease (anti-TSH receptor, stimulatory), myasthenia gravis (anti-acetylcholine receptor, blocking), and rheumatic fever (anti-streptococcal M protein cross-reacting with cardiac myosin).

Type III (immune complex) arises in antigen excess: soluble antigen-antibody complexes deposit in vessels, joints, and glomeruli, fix complement, and recruit neutrophils, which cause fibrinoid necrosis. Classic diseases are SLE, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, serum sickness, and the localized Arthus reaction; complement is consumed (low serum levels) and immunofluorescence shows a granular lumpy-bumpy pattern. Type IV (delayed, cell mediated) needs no antibody: sensitized CD4 Th1 cells and macrophages (or CD8 cytotoxic T cells) drive the injury 48 to 72 hours after exposure. Examples are contact dermatitis (poison ivy, nickel), the tuberculin (PPD) reaction with induration, caseating granuloma formation, type 1 diabetes, and graft rejection. Confirm with a patch test.

11. Transplant Rejection & GVHD

Graft rejection is the destruction of transplanted tissue by the recipient's immune system, driven almost entirely by differences in HLA alleles between donor and recipient. For the pathologist the single most useful organizing fact is timing: how long after transplantation the graft fails tells you the mechanism, and the mechanism in turn predicts the biopsy picture. Hyperacute rejection strikes in minutes, acute rejection over days to weeks, and chronic rejection over months to years, each with a signature morphology on the renal allograft biopsy. Graft-versus-host disease is the mirror image: instead of the host attacking the graft, donor T cells in the graft attack the host, and its targets (skin, gut, liver) and clinical triad (rash, diarrhea, jaundice) are the testable core.

High-Yield Notes

Hyperacute rejection occurs within minutes to hours and is caused by preformed antidonor antibodies already circulating in the recipient (a type II reaction). These come from prior pregnancies, prior transfusions, or ABO incompatibility. Antibody and complement deposit in vessel walls, producing endothelial injury, fibrin-platelet thrombi, neutrophil accumulation in glomeruli and peritubular capillaries, and fibrinoid necrosis of arterial walls. The kidney becomes cyanotic, mottled, and flaccid (the "white graft") and must be removed.

Acute rejection is the most common type, appears within days to weeks, and is reversible with increased immunosuppression. It has two arms. The cellular arm (type IV, T cell mediated) shows an interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate with tubulitis (lymphocytes between tubular epithelial cells) and endothelitis (lymphocytes undermining the endothelium). The humoral arm (antibody mediated) shows a necrotizing vasculitis with neutrophils and fibrinoid necrosis; the diagnostic marker is C4d deposition in peritubular capillaries, signaling complement activation by donor-specific antibody.

Chronic rejection develops over months to years in patients who survived acute episodes, driven by a smoldering CD4 delayed-hypersensitivity reaction. The hallmark is vascular: intimal smooth muscle proliferation narrowing the lumen, called graft arteriosclerosis, plus interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and glomerulosclerosis. It is irreversible and presents as a slow, progressive rise in serum creatinine.

Graft-versus-host disease arises mainly after allogeneic bone marrow transplant (also non-irradiated blood transfusions and lymphoid-rich solid organs). Per the Billingham criteria the graft must contain immunocompetent donor T cells and the host must be immunocompromised. Donor CD8 T cells attack host epithelium of skin, gut, and liver, giving the triad of rash, bloody diarrhea, and jaundice. Chronic GVHD resembles systemic sclerosis with dermal fibrosis and cholestatic liver disease.

12. Autoimmune Diseases

Autoimmune disease results when self-tolerance fails and lymphocytes attack the body's own tissues, arising from inherited susceptibility genes acting together with environmental triggers such as infection, drugs, and ultraviolet light. For the boards the most reliable shortcut through this large topic is the autoantibody: each disease carries a hallmark antibody that is either highly sensitive (good for screening) or highly specific (good for confirming the diagnosis). Layered onto each antibody is a characteristic constellation of clinical features and a distinctive morphology, so the exam question typically gives you a young woman with a multisystem illness, or a patient with a focal organ pattern, and asks you to name the antibody, the disease, or the classic biopsy lesion.

High-Yield Notes

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a multisystem type III (immune-complex) disease of young women, roughly nine times more common in females and more frequent in African-American and Hispanic patients. ANA is sensitive but nonspecific (a good screen); anti-double-stranded DNA and anti-Smith are specific (good for confirmation), and anti-dsDNA titers track disease and correlate with lupus nephritis. Diagnostic morphology includes the wire-loop lesion of diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis (subendothelial immune-complex deposits), LE/hematoxylin bodies, the photosensitive malar (butterfly) rash, and Libman-Sacks endocarditis (sterile verrucous vegetations on either surface of the valve leaflets). Serum complement falls during flares. Drug-induced lupus (procainamide, hydralazine, isoniazid) spares the kidney and CNS and is marked by anti-histone antibodies.

Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma) is fibrosis of skin and viscera. The diffuse form carries anti-Scl-70 (anti-DNA topoisomerase I) and threatens the lungs and kidneys; the limited form is CREST (Calcinosis, Raynaud, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia) and carries the anticentromere antibody. Sjogren syndrome destroys lacrimal and salivary glands, giving the sicca complex of dry eyes and dry mouth with a dense lymphocytic infiltrate on lip biopsy; its antibodies are anti-Ro (SS-A) and anti-La (SS-B), and patients carry an increased risk of B-cell lymphoma. Mixed connective tissue disease overlaps lupus, scleroderma, and myositis features and is defined by high-titer anti-U1 RNP.

Rheumatoid arthritis is a symmetric small-joint proliferative synovitis; rheumatoid factor (IgM against the Fc of IgG) is sensitive while anti-citrullinated peptide antibody is specific and prognostic. Its morphology is the destructive pannus eroding cartilage and bone, with subcutaneous rheumatoid nodules (central fibrinoid necrosis ringed by palisading macrophages). A few organ-specific antibody diseases round out the list: Graves disease (stimulating anti-TSH-receptor antibody, hyperthyroidism with exophthalmos and pretibial myxedema), myasthenia gravis (anti-acetylcholine-receptor antibody, fatigable weakness), Goodpasture syndrome (anti-GBM antibody, linear immunofluorescence, lung and kidney injury), and rheumatic fever (anti-streptococcal antibody cross-reacting with myocardium via molecular mimicry).

13. Immunodeficiency Diseases

Immunodeficiencies divide into primary (inherited, often X linked so males predominate, usually appearing between 6 months and 2 years as maternal antibody wanes) and secondary (acquired through infection, immunosuppression, or malnutrition). On a pathology exam the recognizable signal is not the gene but the infection signature and the complications: which class of organism keeps returning, when symptoms start, and what tumor or autoimmune disease tends to follow. This section frames the named syndromes by their clinical and morphologic fingerprint and ends with the pathology of HIV and AIDS.

High-Yield Notes

The clearest clue is the organism class. Antibody (B cell) defects present after 6 months with recurrent pyogenic, encapsulated bacterial infections of the sinopulmonary tract (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus) plus enteroviral and Giardia infections, because opsonizing IgG is missing. T cell defects instead bring early opportunistic infection by viruses, fungi, and intracellular organisms and tend to be more severe. Combined defects show both. Phagocyte and complement defects give their own signatures: chronic granulomatous disease yields catalase positive abscesses, leukocyte adhesion deficiency delays umbilical cord separation, and terminal complement loss invites recurrent Neisseria.

Several syndromes carry a heavily tested malignancy or autoimmune tail: common variable immunodeficiency (lymphoma, gastric carcinoma, autoimmunity), Wiskott Aldrich (non Hodgkin lymphoma), and ataxia telangiectasia (ATM DNA repair defect). The morphologic hooks are a tiny lymphocyte depleted thymus in SCID and rudimentary germinal centers in Bruton agammaglobulinemia.

HIV/AIDS is the dominant secondary immunodeficiency. The virus depletes CD4 helper T cells, inverting the CD4/CD8 ratio, and AIDS is defined at a CD4 count below 200 or an AIDS defining illness. Opportunistic infections cause most deaths, and the AIDS defining neoplasms are Kaposi sarcoma (HHV-8) and aggressive B cell lymphomas, with primary CNS lymphoma being EBV related.

14. Amyloidosis

Amyloidosis is a group of diseases that share one feature: the extracellular deposition of a misfolded protein that aggregates into insoluble fibrils with a cross beta pleated sheet architecture. Although the precursor proteins are chemically diverse, they all fold into the same fibrillar shape, so they all stain with Congo red and all show apple green birefringence under polarized light. The clinical disease arises from physical pressure as the inert deposits encroach on and atrophy the cells of an organ; amyloid itself does not provoke inflammation.

High-Yield Notes

The exam centers on matching the fibril type to its precursor and clinical setting. AL amyloid is made of immunoglobulin light chains produced by a monoclonal plasma cell proliferation, so it is the type seen in multiple myeloma and primary amyloidosis; look for Bence Jones protein and marrow plasmacytosis. AA amyloid derives from the acute phase reactant SAA made by the liver under IL-1 and IL-6, so it complicates chronic inflammation such as rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic infection, and it is the fibril of familial Mediterranean fever. ATTR comes from transthyretin: a mutant form causes familial amyloid polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy, while the normal wild type form accumulates in the aging heart as senile systemic amyloidosis. Beta-2 microglobulin accumulates in long term dialysis and deposits in the carpal ligament to cause carpal tunnel syndrome. A beta is the plaque protein of Alzheimer disease, and amylin deposits in the pancreatic islets of type 2 diabetes.

Organ involvement drives the clinical picture. The kidney is the most common and most serious site, giving heavy proteinuria and the nephrotic syndrome from glomerular deposition, and renal failure is a leading cause of death. The heart develops a restrictive cardiomyopathy with arrhythmias and conduction block. The spleen shows either follicular deposits, the firm tapioca like sago spleen, or sinus and pulp deposits, the lardaceous spleen. The liver can become massively enlarged yet keep near normal function. The tongue enlarges into macroglossia. Diagnosis rests on biopsy: abdominal fat pad aspirate and rectal or gingival biopsy are the low risk first choices, confirmed with Congo red and typed by immunohistochemistry.

15. OSCE: Breast & Skin Lesion Exam

The breast and skin OSCE stations test whether you can triage a lump or a lesion from the history and the physical exam alone, then choose the correct first investigation. The whole task reduces to one decision: does this finding read as benign, as a non-neoplastic physiologic change, or as a carcinoma? Each diagnosis carries a tight cluster of age, consistency, mobility, and associated signs that the examiner is listening for. This section organizes those clusters and the buzzword cues so that a single line of the stem points you to the answer.

High-Yield Notes

For a breast lump, three findings drive the differential: the patient's age, the consistency and mobility of the lump, and whether pain is cyclical. A young woman with a firm, smooth, freely mobile, painless lump (the "breast mouse") has a fibroadenoma. A woman in her late 20s to 40s with cyclical, mid-cycle pain and lumpy "bumpy" breasts has fibrocystic change, a non-neoplastic hormonal response. After 40 or post-menopause, a hard, fixed, irregular lump with skin or nipple changes is carcinoma until proven otherwise. Consistency is a useful shorthand: a lump that feels firm like the tip of the nose suggests a fibroadenoma, whereas one that feels hard like the forehead/knuckle suggests carcinoma. A lump of at least 1 cm is clinically significant.

Nipple changes are their own examiner favorite. Nipple retraction, peau d'orange (dermal lymphatic invasion producing an orange-peel dimpled skin), and an eczematous, scaling nipple (Paget disease of the breast, signaling underlying ductal carcinoma) all point to malignancy. Work-up sequence: the standard breast assessment is the triple test — clinical exam, imaging (ultrasound if <30 or pregnant, mammography if older), and tissue sampling. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is done before core biopsy as the quick first sampling step. On exam, gently pressing the nipple and watching it spring back, and assessing whether the lump is tethered, are the maneuvers being graded.

For a skin lesion, the central rule is ABCDE for melanoma: Asymmetry, irregular Border, variegated Color, Diameter over 6 mm, and Evolution over time — where E (evolution) is the single most important feature and the best single answer when a station asks which warrants biopsy. A patient with more than 50 moles or several atypical (dysplastic) nevi is at higher melanoma risk. By contrast, the classic benign mimic is the seborrheic keratosis: a brown, greasy, "stuck-on" plaque (the "coins glued to the back" description) that can be scraped off — multiple such lesions appearing suddenly (Leser-Trélat sign) hint at visceral malignancy. Actinic keratosis is the rough, sandpaper-textured premalignant lesion that precedes squamous cell (and other UV) skin cancers.

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Young + mobile + painless = fibroadenoma; cyclical pain + lumpy = fibrocystic; old + hard + fixed + skin/nipple change = carcinoma.
  • Firm "like the nose" leans benign; hard "like the knuckle" leans malignant; peau d'orange, retraction, and Paget disease are red flags.
  • Discharge: milky = prolactin/galactorrhea, bloody = intraductal papilloma or carcinoma, green/yellow = duct ectasia or infection.
  • FNAC comes before core biopsy; ultrasound for young/pregnant, mammography for older.
  • Melanoma ABCDE — E (evolution) is the best single answer; a scrapeable "stuck-on" lesion is a benign seborrheic keratosis, not melanoma.

16. ICM: Skin Exam Technique & Lesion Morphology

The dermatology OSCE rewards a systematic description of any lesion in the examiner's own vocabulary, because the morphology terms map directly onto the differential. Every lesion is first sorted as flat, raised, or fluid-filled and by size, which fixes the primary-lesion name; you then layer on number, color, shape, texture, location, and configuration. This section is the language of the skin exam plus the maneuvers that make it complete.

High-Yield Notes

A primary lesion is the first thing that appears on the skin; the single most useful split is flat versus raised ("can I feel it with my eyes closed?") and the 1 cm cut-off. Flat and small is a macule, flat and large is a patch; raised, solid, and small is a papule, raised solid and large is a plaque; raised and fluid-filled is a vesicle (<1 cm) or bulla (>1 cm). A pus-filled papule is a pustule and a transient edematous raised lesion is a wheal. Loss of epidermis is an erosion; loss extending into dermis is an ulcer.

Once the primary lesion is named, describe it fully: Number (solitary or multiple), Size (measure in mm, long axis then perpendicular), Color (for red lesions blanch with pressure or a glass slide — blanching means dilated vessels, whereas non-blanching red signals extravasated blood, i.e. petechiae/purpura), Shape (annular = ring with central clearing, nummular = coin-like with no clearing, polygonal), Texture (smooth, verrucous/warty, or scaly), Location, and Configuration. Examination uses good overhead or natural lighting, a ruler, and a dermoscope (cross-polarized handheld magnifier that reveals pigment and vascular patterns). The integrated full-body exam folds skin inspection into the general exam — e.g. when auscultating the lungs, open the gown and inspect the back (a site patients cannot self-examine) for melanoma.

Synthesis and traps to avoid:
  • Flat = macule (<1 cm) or patch (>1 cm); raised solid = papule (<1 cm) or plaque (>1 cm); raised fluid = vesicle (<1 cm) or bulla (>1 cm).
  • Blanching red = vascular dilation; non-blanching red = petechiae/purpura (extravasated blood) and is the worrisome finding.
  • Annular = ring with central clearing (tinea); nummular = coin-shaped without clearing (nummular eczema).
  • Inspect hard-to-see sites (back, posterior legs) during the integrated exam; these hide the deadliest melanomas.

17. ICM: Benign, Premalignant & Malignant Skin Tumors

The core OSCE skill is sorting a skin tumor into benign, premalignant, or malignant from its bedside appearance. Benign lesions are symmetric, uniform, and stable; malignant lesions break those rules (the melanoma ABCDE). Skin cancers are the most common cancers overall, caused by genetic predisposition plus UV exposure, with BCC > SCC > melanoma by frequency and melanoma the most lethal.

High-Yield Notes

Benign lesions: a seborrheic keratosis is a tan-to-brown, greasy, "stuck-on" verrucous plaque with horn cysts (a sudden eruption of many = the Leser-Trélat sign of visceral malignancy); a lipoma is a soft, mobile, non-tender subcutaneous nodule of mature fat; a benign nevus is symmetric with a uniform color and a regular border; solar lentigines are flat brown macules on sun-exposed skin. The key premalignant lesion is actinic (solar) keratosis — a rough, "sandpaper" scaly macule/papule that can progress to squamous cell carcinoma.

For pigmented lesions, the ABCDE rule flags malignant melanoma: A = Asymmetry, B = Border irregularity, C = Color variegation, D = Diameter >6 mm, and E = Evolution (change over time), where E is the single most important feature.   • Purple plaques in an HIV patient = Kaposi sarcoma (HHV-8), not bruises.

18. ICM: Inflammatory & Papulosquamous Dermatoses

Most non-tumor rashes seen in the OSCE are papulosquamous (raised + scaly) or eczematous, and each has a signature lesion, distribution, and buzzword. The exam game is matching the scale quality and the body site to the diagnosis — silvery scale on extensors for psoriasis, a herald patch for pityriasis rosea, violaceous polygonal papules for lichen planus, and a flexural itchy rash for atopic dermatitis.

High-Yield Notes

Psoriasis gives well-demarcated, raised, red plaques with thick silvery scale on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, and sacrum, from epidermal hyperproliferation; guttate psoriasis is a shower of small plaques in a young person after a streptococcal throat infection. Pityriasis rosea begins with a single herald patch on the trunk, followed days later by smaller fawn-colored plaques in a "Christmas-tree" distribution (viral, self-limited). Lichen planus is the 6 P's: pruritic, purple (violaceous), polygonal, planar (flat-topped) papules and plaques, with lacy white Wickham striae and oral involvement (T-cell attack on basal keratinocytes, drug-triggered).

  • Lichen planus = the 6 P's with Wickham striae and oral lesions.

19. ICM: Skin Infections & Infestations

Infectious dermatoses are recognized by the pattern and the primary lesion: grouped vesicles for herpes, a unilateral dermatomal band for shingles, spreading warm erythema for cellulitis, and intensely itchy burrows for scabies. Sorting viral, bacterial, fungal, and parasitic causes from the bedside picture is the exam task.

High-Yield Notes

Herpes simplex produces grouped vesicles on an erythematous base: HSV-1 for facial cold sores, HSV-2 for genital lesions. Herpes zoster (shingles) is reactivated varicella-zoster that gives a painful vesicular eruption in a unilateral dermatomal band that does not cross the midline. Cellulitis is a spreading, warm, tender, poorly demarcated erythema of the deep dermis and subcutis (streptococci and staphylococci). Furuncles are follicular abscesses and several coalescing form a carbuncle (Staphylococcus aureus).
