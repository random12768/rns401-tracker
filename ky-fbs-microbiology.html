Ky's FBS2 Microbiology Study Guide

This guide is built around active recall, not passive review. The old study cycle, reading a textbook-style chapter end to end and only then testing yourself, feels productive but is weak for long-term retention: most of the effort goes into recognizing material rather than retrieving it. This guide flips that. Each section is a UWorld-style question bank that forces you to commit to an answer first, then learn from the full explanation, which is where the real encoding happens.

How to use it well: keep your lecture slides open as the primary teaching source. For each section, work one question at a time, read the vignette carefully, and choose an answer before revealing anything, even when you are unsure: a wrong guess you then correct is remembered far better than a fact you simply read. After submitting, study the whole answer panel, not just the green header: confirm why the correct option is right, read why each distractor is wrong, absorb the teaching block, and use the slide reference to flip back to the deck for anything that did not land. Treat a missed question as a signal to revisit that slide, then return later and attempt it again. Work through every section; the goal is to be able to retrieve, explain, and apply each concept, not just recognize it.

1. Intro to Microbial Agents

Medical microbiology studies a small set of agent classes that differ at the level of cell architecture and genome, and those differences drive everything downstream: which drugs work, how the organism replicates, and how it is killed. The first sorting question is always prokaryote versus eukaryote. Bacteria are prokaryotes: they have no true membrane-bound nucleus, carry a single circular chromosome, run 70S ribosomes (30S plus 50S subunits), and most are wrapped in a peptidoglycan cell wall. Fungi are eukaryotes with a membrane-bound nucleus and 80S ribosomes (40S plus 60S), an ergosterol-containing membrane, and a chitin cell wall (no peptidoglycan).

Below cellular life sit two acellular agents. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites with no ribosomes and no metabolism of their own; they carry either DNA or RNA but never both, and they hijack host machinery to replicate. Prions are the most stripped-down agent of all: a misfolded host protein (PrPSc) with no nucleic acid whatsoever. Because there is no genome and no membrane, prions resist standard sterilization and cause progressive spongiform encephalopathies such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.

High-Yield Notes

The exam-relevant contrasts are the ribosome size (the basis for selective antibiotic toxicity: 70S bacterial ribosomes are targeted by aminoglycosides, macrolides, and tetracyclines without harming the 80S human ribosome), the cell wall chemistry (peptidoglycan in bacteria versus chitin and ergosterol-membrane in fungi), and the genome type. Lock in that a virus carries one nucleic acid type only, and that a prion carries none.

Selective toxicity hinge: The 70S versus 80S ribosome split is why protein synthesis inhibitors can poison bacteria while sparing human cells. Antifungals instead exploit ergosterol (azoles block synthesis, amphotericin B binds it), which human cholesterol membranes lack.
Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD): Rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus and ataxia caused by accumulation of protease-resistant PrPSc. There is no nucleic acid, so the agent survives routine autoclaving, formalin, and standard disinfectants; spongiform vacuolation of gray matter is the pathologic hallmark.

2. Disinfection & Sterilization

Infection control rests on precise definitions, and the boards test the boundaries between them. Sterilization destroys all microbial life including bacterial endospores; nothing survives. Disinfection kills or removes vegetative pathogens from inanimate objects but does not reliably kill spores. Antisepsis is the same idea applied to living tissue (skin, mucosa), so the agent must be nontoxic enough for skin contact. Asepsis is the broader practice of preventing contamination of a sterile field in the first place. The single word that separates sterilization from everything else is sporicidal: only a sporicidal process is true sterilization.

The reference standard is the autoclave: saturated moist heat at 121°C, 15 psi, for 15 minutes (or 132°C for 4 minutes), which is reliably sporicidal because pressurized steam denatures proteins far faster than dry air. Heat-sensitive items need alternatives: ethylene oxide gas sterilizes by alkylating nucleic acids and proteins, and 0.22 µm membrane filtration (with HEPA filters for air) physically removes microbes from heat-labile liquids. Pitfalls to memorize: pasteurization (HTST 72°C for 15 s) lowers microbial load and kills vegetative pathogens but is not sterilization, and 60 to 70% alcohols are not sporicidal.

High-Yield Notes

A favorite exam stem ranks agents by how hard they are to kill. From most resistant to least: prions > bacterial endospores > mycobacteria > Gram-negative bacteria > Gram-positive bacteria > enveloped viruses > fungi. The practical message is that any process validated against endospores will handle everything below them on the ladder, but prions sit above even spores and require extended or chemical decontamination. UV light at 260 nm kills by inducing thymine dimers in DNA; it is a surface and air method only, because it does not penetrate solids or shadowed areas.

3. Classification, Staining & Normal Flora

Bacterial identification begins with the Gram stain, the single most useful first test in clinical microbiology. The steps run in fixed order: crystal violet (primary stain), Gram iodine (mordant that traps the dye), alcohol or acetone (decolorizer, the critical differential step), and safranin (counterstain). The result is determined by wall architecture. Gram-positive organisms have a thick peptidoglycan layer that retains the crystal-violet/iodine complex during decolorization and stay purple. Gram-negative organisms have thin peptidoglycan plus an outer membrane; the decolorizer washes the complex out and they take up safranin, appearing pink.

Some clinically important organisms are poorly seen on Gram stain and require other approaches. The acid-fast stain uses carbol fuchsin to penetrate mycolic-acid-rich walls that then resist acid-alcohol decolorization, capturing Mycobacterium and partially Nocardia. Beyond staining, GC content (the percent of guanine plus cytosine in genomic DNA) is a classic taxonomic tool for grouping related species. Finally, normal flora colonize body sites and both protect against pathogens and cause disease when displaced.

High-Yield Notes

Memorize the organisms that do not Gram stain well, why, and the alternative method. Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma have no cell wall (no Gram alternative); Mycobacteria have a waxy mycolic-acid wall (acid-fast stain); spirochetes such as Treponema and Leptospira are too thin to resolve (dark-field or fluorescent antibody); Chlamydia is obligate intracellular (inclusion-body stains); Rickettsia, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma, and Coxiella are obligate intracellular (Giemsa or tissue stains); and Legionella stains weakly and needs prolonged counterstain or silver stain. On the acid-fast stain, Mycobacterium is fully acid-fast and the branching Nocardia is weakly acid-fast, while non-acid-fast cells take up the blue methylene counterstain.

Bacteria are also classified by shape and arrangement and by molecular tools. GC content (the percent of base pairs where guanine bonds cytosine) was the first DNA property used taxonomically: it is constant for an organism, ranges from about 25 to 75 percent across bacteria, and differs by only 3 to 5 percent between closely related groups. Cocci are named by how they cluster.

Decolorizer is the differential step: If a tech skips or over-applies alcohol/acetone, Gram-negative rods can read as false purple. The thick peptidoglycan of Gram-positives, not the dye itself, is what physically traps the crystal-violet/iodine complex.

4. Bacterial Structure & Function

Bacterial structures are the targets of nearly every antibacterial drug and the source of most virulence, so the cell envelope is where structure meets the exam. The defining wall polymer is peptidoglycan, a mesh of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) sugars whose adjacent strands are stitched together by transpeptidase enzymes, the penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Understanding how the Gram-positive and Gram-negative envelopes differ explains drug penetration, beta-lactam resistance, and the clinical effects of endotoxin.

High-Yield Notes

Peptidoglycan is built as a NAG-NAM glycan backbone, and the cross-links between strands are formed by transpeptidase (a PBP) joining the terminal D-Ala-D-Ala of one peptide stem to the next. Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) are structural analogs of D-Ala-D-Ala that covalently inhibit the PBP, halting cross-linking and leaving a weak wall that bursts under osmotic pressure. Vancomycin works one step upstream: it binds the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the peptidoglycan precursor itself, sterically blocking the transpeptidase from reaching its substrate (it does not bind the PBP). Lysozyme, found in tears and saliva, hydrolyzes the NAG-NAM glycosidic bond directly.

The Gram-negative outer membrane is both a virulence factor and a permeability barrier: its outer leaflet is lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and the periplasmic space beneath it concentrates degradative enzymes including beta-lactamase, which is why many Gram-negatives are intrinsically resistant to penicillins. The clinically critical fragment of LPS is Lipid A.

Endotoxin (Lipid A) and septic shock. Lipid A is the toxic, heat-stable component of LPS released chiefly when Gram-negative bacteria lyse. It triggers macrophages to release TNF-alpha, IL-1, and IL-6, producing fever, hypotension, and DIC. Because it is a structural lipid, endotoxin is not inactivated by autoclaving and cannot be converted to a toxoid, so no vaccine targets it directly.
Vancomycin vs beta-lactams. Both block wall cross-linking, but vancomycin binds the D-Ala-D-Ala substrate while beta-lactams inhibit the PBP enzyme. Vancomycin is a large molecule that cannot cross the Gram-negative outer membrane, so it is used for Gram-positives only.

Several accessory structures recur in exam stems. The capsule is a polysaccharide layer that is antiphagocytic and is the chief virulence factor of encapsulated organisms (it is also the target of polysaccharide vaccines). Endospores, formed by Bacillus and Clostridium, contain dipicolinic acid and are extraordinarily resistant to heat, drying, and chemicals, so killing them requires autoclaving. Mycoplasma uniquely lacks a cell wall entirely and stabilizes its membrane with sterols, which is why beta-lactams are useless against it.

5. Bacterial Growth & Nutrition

Bacteria divide by binary fission, and the way a population grows over time, plus the oxygen and nutrient conditions it requires, drives both laboratory diagnosis and antibiotic timing. The growth curve has four phases, and the key clinical point is that beta-lactams act during the log phase when the wall is being actively synthesized. Oxygen tolerance and culture media are the other two high-yield axes: they determine which organisms grow where and how the lab distinguishes them.

High-Yield Notes

The four phases of the growth curve are lag (metabolic adaptation, no division), log / exponential (maximal division, where cell-wall-active drugs are most effective), stationary (nutrient depletion balances death; this is when many organisms make toxins and form endospores), and death / decline. During log phase, numbers follow 2n, where n is the number of generations, so a culture starting at one cell with a 20-minute generation time reaches roughly a thousand cells after about 10 doublings.

Why log phase matters. Penicillins and other cell-wall inhibitors require active peptidoglycan synthesis to work, which occurs during exponential growth. Bacteria in stationary or dormant phases synthesize little wall and are relatively tolerant of beta-lactams.

Oxygen requirements hinge on whether an organism possesses the detoxifying enzymes catalase and superoxide dismutase. Organisms lacking them are poisoned by oxygen radicals. This single fact explains the entire spectrum below.

Culture media are classified by purpose, and the exam tests the distinction between selective (suppresses unwanted organisms so a target can grow), differential (lets organisms grow but distinguishes them by appearance), and enriched (supplies special growth factors for fastidious organisms). Haemophilus and chocolate agar. Haemophilus influenzae cannot grow on plain blood agar because the intact red cells lock away the cofactors it needs. Gently heated (chocolate) agar lyses the red cells, releasing factor V (NAD) and factor X (hemin), which support growth. A fastidious Gram-negative rod growing only on chocolate agar points to Haemophilus.

Finally, iron is a growth-limiting nutrient that the host deliberately sequesters. Bacteria capture it using siderophores, high-affinity iron-chelating molecules that strip iron from host transferrin and lactoferrin, a recurring virulence and nutrition theme.

6. Bacterial Genetics

Bacteria evolve rapidly because they exchange genes horizontally between cells, not only vertically by division. Three mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer dominate the exam: transformation, transduction, and conjugation. On top of these, mobile elements such as plasmids and transposons carry and shuffle the genes, most notably the genes for antibiotic resistance and for several classic exotoxins. Knowing the vehicle, the DNase sensitivity, and what each mechanism transfers lets you solve almost any genetics vignette.

High-Yield Notes

Transformation is the uptake of naked DNA released into the environment by a lysed cell. The recipient must be competent, and the incoming DNA is integrated by RecA-dependent recombination. Because the DNA is free in solution, this is the only mechanism blocked by adding DNase to the medium, a favorite distinguishing clue. Transduction uses a bacteriophage as the delivery vehicle: generalized transduction mispackages random fragments of host DNA into phage heads, while specialized transduction (from a lysogenic prophage) carries the specific genes flanking the integration site. Conjugation requires direct cell contact through a sex pilus and transfers DNA from an F+ donor to an F- recipient.

MechanismVehicleBlocked by DNase?What is transferred

Within conjugation, the donor state matters. An F+ cell carries the F (fertility) plasmid and transfers a copy of that plasmid to an F- cell. When the F plasmid integrates into the chromosome it creates an Hfr (high-frequency recombination) cell that can transfer chromosomal genes at high frequency. Separately, R plasmids carry antibiotic-resistance genes and spread resistance rapidly through a population by conjugation, which is the mechanism behind multidrug-resistant outbreaks.

PlasmidCarriesClinical relevance

Lysogenic conversion and toxin genes. Several major exotoxins are encoded not by the bacterial chromosome but by a lysogenic prophage integrated through specialized transduction. The classic set is diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, botulinum toxin, and the Shiga toxin of enterohemorrhagic E. coli. A strain that loses the phage loses the ability to make the toxin.
The DNase test. If adding DNase to the medium prevents gene transfer, the mechanism is transformation, because only free naked DNA is exposed to the enzyme. Transduction and conjugation protect the DNA inside a phage capsid or pass it through cell-to-cell contact, so DNase has no effect.

Finally, transposons and insertion sequences are mobile jumping genes that excise and reinsert within or between DNA molecules. They cause insertional mutagenesis (disrupting whatever gene they land in) and frequently carry antibiotic-resistance genes, hopping them from plasmid to chromosome and accelerating the spread of resistance.

7. Antibiotics & Resistance

Antibacterials exploit structures bacteria possess but human cells do not, the principle of selective toxicity. The boards organize antibiotics by their molecular target, so the single most useful mental map is target plus effect: a drug is bactericidal (kills the organism outright) or bacteriostatic (halts growth and lets host defenses finish the job). Cell wall agents and aminoglycosides are cidal; most protein and folate inhibitors are static. This distinction matters clinically because immunocompromised hosts, endocarditis, and meningitis demand cidal therapy. Five target families cover essentially every board question: cell wall synthesis, the 30S ribosomal subunit, the 50S ribosomal subunit, folate (DNA precursor) synthesis, and nucleic acid synthesis.

Cell wall inhibitors are the largest group. Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and the monobactam aztreonam) share a beta-lactam ring that binds penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), the transpeptidases that crosslink peptidoglycan; blocking crosslinking leaves a weak wall and the cell lyses. Vancomycin takes a different route, binding the terminal D-Ala-D-Ala of the peptidoglycan precursor and sterically blocking elongation; it covers Gram-positives only because it is too large to cross the Gram-negative outer membrane. Polymyxins (polymyxin B, colistin) act like detergents that disrupt the Gram-negative outer membrane, a last-resort option limited by nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity.

Ribosomal inhibitors split by subunit. The 30S agents are the aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin), which are bactericidal, require aerobic uptake (useless against anaerobes), and carry the classic toxicities of ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity; and the tetracyclines (doxycycline), which are bacteriostatic, chelate divalent cations (avoid with dairy and antacids), cause tooth discoloration, and are contraindicated in pregnancy and young children. The 50S agents include macrolides (azithromycin, erythromycin; cause QT prolongation), chloramphenicol (dose-independent aplastic anemia and gray baby syndrome in neonates lacking glucuronyl transferase), clindamycin (notorious for C. difficile colitis), and linezolid (reversible MAO inhibition, serotonin syndrome risk). A memory aid: "buy AT 30, CCEL at 50" places Aminoglycosides and Tetracyclines at 30S, and Chloramphenicol, Clindamycin, Erythromycin (macrolides), and Linezolid at 50S.

Folate synthesis inhibitors exploit a pathway humans lack (we ingest folate). Sulfonamides inhibit dihydropteroate synthase and trimethoprim inhibits dihydrofolate reductase; given together (TMP-SMX) they produce a sequential blockade of the same pathway that is synergistic and bactericidal. Nucleic acid inhibitors are the last family: fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV, blocking DNA supercoiling and risking tendon rupture and QT prolongation; metronidazole is reduced inside anaerobes and protozoa to toxic free radicals that fragment DNA; and rifampin inhibits bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase and famously turns body fluids orange.

Resistance is the other half of this section and is tested heavily. Bacteria defeat antibiotics by four broad strategies: enzymatic inactivation, target alteration, reduced uptake or increased efflux, and bypass of the targeted pathway. The genetic origin matters too: intrinsic resistance is a built-in feature of the species (vancomycin cannot reach Gram-negative targets); chromosomal mutation arises spontaneously and is vertically inherited; and plasmid-mediated resistance rides on R plasmids spread horizontally by conjugation, which is how resistance jumps between species and causes outbreaks.

MRSA mechanism: Methicillin-resistant S. aureus carries the mecA gene, which encodes an altered transpeptidase, PBP2a, with very low affinity for beta-lactams. Because the target itself changed, no penicillin, cephalosporin (except ceftaroline), or carbapenem works; treat with vancomycin, linezolid, or daptomycin. Beta-lactamase inhibitors do not help here because the problem is the target, not an enzyme.
Vancomycin resistance: VRE and VRSA replace the terminal D-Ala-D-Ala of the peptidoglycan precursor with D-Ala-D-Lac. Vancomycin can no longer bind the precursor, so wall synthesis proceeds despite the drug. This single amino acid swap, carried on transferable genetic elements, is the reason vancomycin failure should immediately raise this mechanism.
Clostridioides difficile colitis: Broad-spectrum antibiotics, classically clindamycin and fluoroquinolones, wipe out colonic flora and let toxigenic C. difficile overgrow, releasing toxins A and B that cause pseudomembranous colitis with watery diarrhea. Diagnosis is by stool toxin or PCR; treat by stopping the offending drug and giving oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin.

Finally, susceptibility is measured in the lab. The Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion test places antibiotic-impregnated disks on a lawn of bacteria; the drug diffuses out and the clear zone of inhibition diameter is measured against standardized breakpoints to classify the organism as Susceptible, Intermediate, or Resistant. A larger zone means greater susceptibility. The broth-based minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) gives the quantitative counterpart, the lowest drug concentration that prevents visible growth.

30S vs 50S: "Buy AT 30, CCEL at 50." 30S = Aminoglycosides, Tetracyclines. 50S = Chloramphenicol, Clindamycin, Erythromycin (macrolides), Linezolid.

8. Parasitology & Antiparasitics

Parasites fall into two broad camps, and almost every board question begins by placing the organism in the right one. Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotes that often cycle between two forms: the trophozoite, the active, feeding, motile, and disease-causing form, and the cyst, the dormant, environmentally resistant, infective form that survives outside the host and transmits infection. Helminths are large multicellular worms divided into nematodes (roundworms), cestodes (tapeworms), and trematodes (flukes). A practical clinical clue separates the groups: tissue-invasive helminths provoke eosinophilia, whereas most luminal protozoa do not.

Life cycles hinge on the host distinction. The definitive host harbors the sexual reproductive stage of the parasite, while the intermediate host carries the larval or asexual stage. For malaria, the mosquito is the definitive host (sexual stage occurs there) and the human is the intermediate host, a frequently tested reversal of intuition. The protozoa themselves are grouped by locomotion: Sarcodina (amebas such as Entamoeba, move by pseudopods), Sporozoa (nonmotile intracellular parasites such as Plasmodium), Mastigophora (flagellates such as Giardia and Trichomonas), and Ciliata (ciliated, e.g., Balantidium).

Antiparasitic drugs are best learned by mechanism paired with target organism. Metronidazole is reduced to toxic free radicals inside anaerobic protozoa, treating Giardia, Entamoeba, and Trichomonas. For malaria, two drugs hit different stages: chloroquine kills blood-stage merozoites by blocking heme polymerization (toxic free heme accumulates), while primaquine eradicates the dormant liver hypnozoites of P. vivax and P. ovale to prevent relapse (screen for G6PD deficiency first). For worms, albendazole binds beta-tubulin and is broadly antihelminthic; praziquantel causes calcium influx and tetanic paralysis in flukes and tapeworms; ivermectin opens glutamate-gated chloride channels to paralyze Strongyloides and Onchocerca; and pyrantel pamoate causes a depolarizing neuromuscular block that paralyzes intestinal roundworms.

Trophozoite vs cyst: The trophozoite is the active, feeding, motile form that causes disease inside the host; the cyst is the dormant, resistant, infective form that survives in the environment and transmits the organism. Fecal-oral protozoa spread as cysts and cause symptoms as trophozoites.
Definitive vs intermediate host: The definitive host carries the sexual stage; the intermediate host carries the larval or asexual stage. For malaria the mosquito is definitive and the human is intermediate, the reverse of what most students assume.

9. Mycology & Antifungals

Fungi are eukaryotes, which is why selective toxicity is hard and most antifungals are aimed at structures we lack: an ergosterol plasma membrane and a cell wall of chitin and beta-glucan. This section covers fungal structure, the thermally dimorphic fungi that switch between yeast and mold, the laboratory stains and culture used to identify them, the mechanism of each antifungal class mapped to its molecular target, and the high-yield mycotoxins. Anchor everything to one idea: pick the target (membrane, cell wall, nucleic acid, or microtubule) and the drug, its toxicity, and its resistance pattern follow.

High-Yield Notes

Thermal dimorphism is a board favorite: the fungus is a mold in the cold (25°C, environment, infectious hyphae/spores) and a yeast in the heat (37°C, body tissue). The classic dimorphs are Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Coccidioides, Paracoccidioides, and Sporothrix. Coccidioides is the exception to the yeast form: in tissue it makes a spherule packed with endospores, not a budding yeast. Candida albicans is the reverse pattern, forming germ tubes and pseudohyphae at 37°C, which is the basis of its rapid identification test.

Antifungal mechanism is the single most tested mycology topic. Group the drugs by target. Azoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, ketoconazole) inhibit 14-alpha-demethylase, a fungal CYP450 enzyme that converts lanosterol to ergosterol, so they block ergosterol synthesis and inhibit human CYP450 (drug interactions). Amphotericin B binds preformed ergosterol and punches pores in the membrane, leaking ions; it is fungicidal but nephrotoxic with K+ and Mg2+ wasting plus infusion fevers. Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin) inhibit beta-glucan synthase, hitting the cell wall, and are first line for invasive Candida.

Laboratory diagnosis pairs a stain or test with a specific organism, and questions usually give you the prep and ask for the bug. A KOH prep dissolves keratin to reveal hyphae in skin scrapings. Most fungi grow on Sabouraud dextrose agar, which is acidic to suppress bacteria.

High-Yield Mycotoxins: Amanita phalloides (death cap) produces alpha-amanitin, which inhibits RNA polymerase II and causes fulminant hepatic failure. Aspergillus flavus on stored grains and nuts makes aflatoxin, which induces a TP53 mutation (codon 249) and is a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma.

10. Virology, Antivirals & Vaccines

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites built from a nucleic acid genome, a protein capsid, and sometimes a host-derived lipid envelope. This section covers the naked versus enveloped distinction and what it predicts about transmission and disinfection, capsid symmetry, the universal replication cycle, the genome-to-polymerase logic that tells you where a virus replicates and what enzyme it must carry, the major DNA and RNA families, the mechanisms of the core antivirals, and the trade-offs between live-attenuated and inactivated vaccines. The recurring exam skill is reasoning from genome type to behavior.

High-Yield Notes

The envelope determines durability. Enveloped viruses (herpes, influenza, HIV, hepatitis B, coronaviruses) are fragile: alcohol, detergents, and drying destroy the lipid coat, so they spread by close contact, droplets, and body fluids. Naked viruses (picornavirus, adenovirus, norovirus, papillomavirus, parvovirus) are hardy, resist desiccation and acid, survive on surfaces, and favor the fecal-oral route. Capsid symmetry is either helical or icosahedral. Release is by budding for enveloped viruses (often non-lytic) and by lysis for naked viruses.

The replication cycle is six universal steps: attachment, penetration, uncoating, macromolecular synthesis, assembly, and release. The most tested point is where synthesis occurs and what polymerase the virus must supply. The default rule: DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus (exception: poxvirus in the cytoplasm), and RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm (exceptions: influenza and retroviruses use the nucleus). Genome polarity sets the enzyme requirement.

Antivirals exploit virus-specific enzymes. Acyclovir is a prodrug activated by viral thymidine kinase (HSV/VZV) and then inhibits viral DNA polymerase after chain termination; resistance arises from loss of thymidine kinase. Ganciclovir treats CMV and is activated by the CMV UL97 kinase. Foscarnet inhibits viral polymerase directly without needing a kinase, so it covers thymidine kinase-deficient resistant strains. Oseltamivir inhibits influenza neuraminidase, blocking release of new virions.

11. Viral Lab Diagnosis & Viral Genetics

Diagnosing a viral infection means choosing the right tool for the question: see the virus, grow it, detect the antibody response, or amplify its nucleic acid. This section walks through microscopy clues, cell culture and cytopathic effect, serology with acute versus convalescent titers, and molecular testing, then turns to viral genetics: the four ways viral genomes interact (recombination, reassortment, complementation, phenotypic mixing) and how rapid RNA mutation drives antigenic drift and shift. The high-yield skill is matching a described finding to its named mechanism.

High-Yield Notes

Microscopy gives instant clues. Inclusion bodies and multinucleated giant cells point to specific viruses; a Tzanck smear showing multinucleated giant cells indicates HSV or VZV. Immunofluorescence (direct fluorescent antibody on brain tissue) confirms rabies, and electron microscopy can reveal virion shape. Cell culture uses primary lines (monkey kidney), semi-continuous lines (MRC-5 human fetal fibroblasts), or continuous lines (HeLa, Vero, MDCK); viral growth shows a cytopathic effect (CPE), including syncytia (cell fusion by viral fusion proteins, classic for RSV, measles, and HSV). Confirm an isolate by neutralization or hemagglutination inhibition.

Serology distinguishes timing. A rising antibody titer between acute and convalescent sera drawn 10 to 14 days apart proves recent infection. IgM signals acute infection while IgG indicates past exposure or immunity. ELISA detects viral antigen or antibody and is the screening test for HIV; a Western blot confirms HIV and requires at least two bands (for example gp160/gp120 plus p24/p31). Nucleic acid testing by PCR or RT-PCR is the most sensitive and rapid method, and viral load in copies per mL (low under 10,000, high over 10,000) monitors HIV therapy.

Mutation separates RNA from DNA viruses. RNA polymerase lacks proofreading, so RNA viruses mutate fast. Antigenic drift is the gradual accumulation of minor point mutations causing seasonal epidemics, while antigenic shift is an abrupt change from reassortment of whole segments causing pandemics. Mutation also underlies live-attenuated vaccines: Sabin oral polio uses mutated strains, and the cold-adapted, temperature-sensitive intranasal influenza vaccine grows at the cooler 33°C nasal mucosa but not in the warm lower airway.
